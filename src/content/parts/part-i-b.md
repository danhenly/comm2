## 1. Cancellation; Rescission

> Whenever a right to rescind a contract of insurance is given to the insurer by any provision of this chapter, such right must be exercised previous to the commencement of an action on the contract.
> — Sec. 48, sent. 1, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

| Element/Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Cancellation concept | Unilateral termination of a non-life policy by the insurer during its term, allowed only on statutory grounds and with statutory notice |
| Rescission concept | Judicial or extrajudicial unmaking of the contract from the beginning on a ground given by the Code, such as concealment, misrepresentation, or breach of warranty |
| Non-life cancellation grounds | Seven exclusive post-effective grounds under Sec. 64: nonpayment of premium, conviction of crime increasing hazard, fraud or material misrepresentation, willful or reckless acts increasing hazard, property becoming uninsurable, other insurance making total exceed value, Commissioner determination of Code violation |
| Cancellation procedure | Prior written notice stating the ground relied upon, with right of the insured to demand the facts under Sec. 65 |
| Non-renewal procedure | 45-day advance notice under Sec. 66 |
| Rescission timing | Insurer must exercise the right to rescind previous to commencement of an action on the contract under Sec. 48 |
| Reinsurance overlay | Cancellation of a treaty does not ipso facto kill cessions already made where the treaty carries an express continuing-liability clause |
| Pleading bar | Rescission cannot be granted in declaratory relief absent a pleaded and proved Code basis |

| Cancellation | Rescission |
|---|---|
| Prospective termination of a running non-life policy | Retroactive unmaking of the contract from inception |
| Only on the seven exclusive Sec. 64 grounds | Only on a Code-given ground such as concealment or breach of warranty |
| Requires prior written notice under Sec. 65 | Must be exercised before action on the contract under Sec. 48 |
| Insured may demand stated facts | Insurer must return premium on rescission except where fraud intervenes |
| Non-renewal needs 45-day notice under Sec. 66 | No renewal notice rule applies |

### Case: *Fieldmen's Insurance v. Asia Surety*, G.R. No. L-23447, July 31, 1970

Facts: Reinsurer cancelled seven reinsurance treaties and sought declaratory relief to end liability. Cessions had already been made under treaties containing express continuing-liability clauses.

Issue: Whether cancellation of the treaties ipso facto terminated liability on cessions already effected, and whether rescission could be granted in that declaratory action.

Held: No. Cancellation did not kill cessions already made under treaties with express continuing-liability clauses. Rescission could not be granted in declaratory relief absent a pleaded and proved basis.

*Doctrine: Treaty cancellation operates prospectively only. Accrued cessions survive where continuing liability was stipulated. Rescission needs a Code ground proved in a proper action.*

> Exam trap: Treaty cancellation is not rescission of cessions already ceded. Check for a continuing-liability clause before declaring liability ended.

On periods and notices, Sec. 65 requires prior written notice stating the ground and gives the insured the right to demand the supporting facts. Sec. 66 requires 45-day notice of non-renewal. Sec. 48, sentence 1, requires rescission before action on the contract.

**Q: N, a non-life insurer, sent a cancellation notice for a fire policy five days before termination, citing a ground not listed in Sec. 64. The insured demanded the facts and received none. May the insurer treat the policy as cancelled?**

A: No, the attempted cancellation is ineffective and the policy remains in force until validly cancelled or otherwise terminated.
Under Sec. 64 cancellation after effectivity is allowed only on seven exclusive statutory grounds, and under Sec. 65 it requires prior written notice stating the ground with a right in the insured to demand the supporting facts.
Here the notice gave a short five-day lead, invoked an unlisted ground, and the insurer ignored the demand for facts, so both the substantive ground and the procedural notice requirements fail.
The cancellation has no effect. The insurer remains bound and any denial on the basis of that cancellation will fail.

## 2. Ascertaining and Controlling Risks

> Each party to a contract of insurance must communicate to the other, in good faith, all facts within his knowledge which are material to the contract and as to which he makes no warranty, and which the other has not the means of ascertaining.
> — Sec. 28, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> Each party to a contract of insurance is bound to know all the general causes which are open to his inquiry, equally with that of the other, and which may affect the political or material perils contemplated; and all general usages of trade.
> — Sec. 32, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

| Element/Requisite        | Content                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Good-faith communication | Each party must communicate in good faith all material facts within his knowledge under Sec. 28                                                               |
| Warranty carve-out       | Duty covers facts as to which the party makes no warranty, since warranty facts are governed by warranty rules                                                |
| Means of ascertaining    | No duty to communicate what the other has the means of ascertaining, since equal access negates concealment                                                   |
| Bound-to-know rule       | Each party is bound to know general causes open to inquiry equally with the other and general usages of trade under Sec. 32                                   |
| Inquiries and control    | Insurer controls risk through application questions, inspection, warranties, conditions, and rate classification                                              |
| Waiver by silence        | Right to material information may be waived by policy terms or by neglect to inquire where the fact is distinctly implied in communicated facts under Sec. 33 |
| Remedy link              | Failure to communicate a material fact the other could not ascertain is concealment entitling the injured party to rescind under Sec. 27                      |

| Ascertaining risk | Controlling risk |
|---|---|
| Gathering material facts through disclosure, inquiry, and inspection | Shaping exposure through warranties, conditions, exclusions, and pricing |
| Governed by Sec. 28 good-faith communication duty | Governed by policy terms plus Sec. 32 bound-to-know allocation |
| Failure yields concealment and a right to rescind | Failure yields breach of warranty or condition and loss of recovery |

On periods and notices, there is no fixed day count in Secs. 28 and 32. Procedure is inquiry plus disclosure before inception. Sec. 65 prior written notice and Sec. 66 45-day notice apply at cancellation and non-renewal. Any Code-based rescission must precede action on the contract under Sec. 48, sent. 1.

**Q: P applied for marine cargo cover and disclosed the voyage route but not the well-known port congestion surcharge practice. The insurer asked no question on surcharges, issued the policy, then sought rescission for concealment. Will rescission prosper?**

A: No, rescission will not prosper because the undisclosed practice was equally open to inquiry and within general usage of trade.
Under Sec. 28 there is no duty to communicate what the other has the means of ascertaining, and under Sec. 32 each party is bound to know general causes open to inquiry and general usages of trade, with waiver by neglect to inquire under Sec. 33.
Here the surcharge practice was a general usage equally accessible to the insurer, the voyage disclosure implied added cost exposure, and the insurer neglected to inquire, so the fact was either bound-to-know or waived.
There is no concealment of a material fact the insurer could not ascertain, so the policy stands and rescission fails.

## 3. Concealment

> A neglect to communicate that which a party knows and ought to communicate, is called a concealment.
> — Sec. 26, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> A concealment whether intentional or unintentional entitles the injured party to rescind a contract of insurance.
> — Sec. 27, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> Each party to a contract of insurance must communicate to the other, in good faith, all facts within his knowledge which are material to the contract and as to which he makes no warranty, and which the other has not the means of ascertaining.
> — Sec. 28, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> Materiality is to be determined not by the event, but solely by the probable and reasonable influence of the facts upon the party to whom the communication is due, in forming his estimate of the disadvantages of the proposed contract, or in making his inquiries.
> — Sec. 31, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> Neither party to a contract of insurance is bound to communicate information of the matters following, except in answer to the inquiries of the other: (a) Those which the other knows; (b) Those which, in the exercise of ordinary care, the other ought to know, and of which the former has no reason to suppose him ignorant; (c) Those of which the other waives communication; (d) Those which prove or tend to prove the existence of a risk excluded by a warranty, and which are not otherwise material; and (e) Those which relate to a risk excepted from the policy and which are not otherwise material.
> — Sec. 30, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> The right to information of material facts may be waived, either by the terms of insurance or by neglect to make inquiry as to such facts, where they are distinctly implied in other facts of which information is communicated.
> — Sec. 33, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> Neither party to a contract of insurance is bound to communicate, even upon inquiry, information of his own judgment upon the matters in question.
> — Sec. 35, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> An intentional and fraudulent omission, on the part of one insured, to communicate information of matters proving or tending to prove the falsity of a warranty, entitles the insurer to rescind.
> — Sec. 29, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

| Element/Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Definition | Neglect to communicate what a party knows and ought to communicate under Sec. 26 |
| Duty bearer | Either party, but in practice the applicant for facts material to underwriting under Sec. 28 |
| Knowledge | Fact must be within the knowledge of the concealing party and unknown without means of ascertaining by the other |
| Materiality test | Probable and reasonable influence on the other in estimating disadvantages or in making inquiries, not judged by the event, under Sec. 31 |
| Causation | No need to show the concealed fact caused the loss; influence on underwriting judgment suffices |
| Intent | Intentional or unintentional concealment both entitle the injured party to rescind under Sec. 27 |
| Fraud | Fraud is not required for concealment, but intentional fraudulent omission proving falsity of a warranty separately rescinds under Sec. 29 |
| Agent knowledge | Knowledge of the approving officer or agent within authority is knowledge of the insurer and defeats concealment |
| Judgment exclusion | Neither party must communicate his own judgment even upon inquiry under Sec. 35 |
| Waiver | Right to information may be waived by policy terms or by neglect to inquire where implied in communicated facts under Sec. 33 |
| Remedy | Rescission by the injured party, exercised previous to action on the contract under Sec. 48 |

| Concealment | Representation | Warranty |
|---|---|---|
| Silence on a material fact that ought to be disclosed | Affirmative statement of fact made to induce the contract | Promise or condition incorporated in the policy |
| Tested by Sec. 31 probable influence on underwriting | Tested by truth and materiality at perfection | Tested by exact compliance once written |
| Intentional or unintentional suffices under Sec. 27 | Falsity plus materiality avoids the policy | Breach bars recovery even if immaterial to loss |

| Intentional concealment | Unintentional concealment |
|---|---|
| Deliberate withholding of a known material fact | Honest omission through forgetfulness or good-faith belief of immateriality |
| Still needs Sec. 31 materiality and Sec. 28 duty | Same requisites, intent does not add an element |
| Both entitle the injured party to rescind under Sec. 27 | Both entitle the injured party to rescind under Sec. 27 |

| Sec. 30 no-concealment matter | Content |
|---|---|
| Matters the other knows | Need not be communicated except in answer to inquiry |
| Matters the other ought to know in ordinary care | Need not be communicated where no reason to suppose ignorance, except in answer to inquiry |
| Matters waived | Need not be communicated where the other waives communication, except in answer to inquiry |
| Matters proving a risk excluded by warranty and not otherwise material | Need not be communicated except in answer to inquiry, without prejudice to Sec. 29 fraudulent omission |
| Matters relating to a risk excepted from the policy and not otherwise material | Need not be communicated except in answer to inquiry |

On periods and notices, concealment rescission has no day-count notice. It must be exercised previous to commencement of an action on the contract under Sec. 48, sent. 1. Sec. 65 prior written notice and Sec. 66 45-day notice govern cancellation and non-renewal, not concealment rescission.

**2023 Bar Q: Diego, a cancer survivor, filed an application for a life insurance policy with Garantiya Insurance Company (GIC). He delivered his application and insurance premium to Gabriela, the officer of GIC in charge of approving life insurance policies. Gabriela then approved the application of Diego on June 1, 2020. On January 30, 2023, Diego died in a vehicular accident. When the heirs of Diego claimed the insurance proceeds, GIC denied the claim on the ground that Diego had concealed the fact of his illness. Moreover, Gabriela knew about the illness of Diego but also concealed it from GIC. Is the denial of the insurance claim by GIC proper? Discuss.**

A: No, the denial is improper and the heirs may recover because the insurer is deemed to have known the illness through its approving officer and cannot claim concealment.
Under Secs. 26 to 28 and 31 concealment is neglect to communicate a known material fact the other has no means of ascertaining, judged by probable influence on underwriting, while knowledge of an agent in charge of approval is knowledge of the insurer and Sec. 33 waives inquiry neglected where the fact was knowable.
Here Gabriela, the officer in charge of approving life policies, knew of the cancer history when she approved the application on June 1, 2020, so GIC had the means of ascertaining and is charged with that knowledge, and death by later vehicular accident confirms materiality is not judged by the event.
There is no concealment against GIC, waiver and estoppel bar avoidance, and the claim denial fails so proceeds must be paid.

## 4. Representation

> Sec. 37. "A representation may be made at the time of, or before, issuance of the policy."
>
> Sec. 38. "The language of a representation is to be interpreted by the same rules as the language of contracts in general."
>
> Sec. 39. "A representation as to the future is to be deemed a promise, unless it appears that it was merely a statement of belief or expectation."
>
> Sec. 40. "A representation cannot qualify an express provision in a contract of insurance, but it may qualify an implied warranty."
>
> Sec. 41. "A representation may be altered or withdrawn before the insurance is effected, but not afterwards."
>
> Sec. 44. "A representation is to be deemed false when the facts fail to correspond with its assertions or stipulations."
>
> Sec. 45. "If a representation is false in a material point, whether affirmative or promissory, the injured party is entitled to rescind the contract from the time when the representation becomes false."
>
> Sec. 46. "The materiality of a representation is determined by the same rules as the materiality of a concealment."

| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Nature                                       | Collateral statement made at or before issuance; not necessarily in the policy                                                                                     |
| Timing                                       | At the time of, or before, issuance; alterable or withdrawable before insurance is effected, not afterwards (Secs. 37, 41)                                         |
| Future representation                        | Deemed a promise unless merely belief or expectation (Sec. 39)                                                                                                     |
| Relation to policy terms                     | Cannot qualify an express provision; may qualify an implied warranty (Sec. 40)                                                                                     |
| Interpretation                               | Same rules as language of contracts in general (Sec. 38)                                                                                                           |
| Falsity test                                 | False when facts fail to correspond with assertions or stipulations (Sec. 44)                                                                                      |
| Materiality test                             | Same rules as materiality of concealment (Sec. 46)                                                                                                                 |

**Q: A applied for fire insurance stating the warehouse was "concrete-walled" when it was half timber, but added "per my caretaker, who I have not verified." The policy as issued expressly describes the building as "concrete throughout." May the insurer rescind, and may A invoke the caretaker explanation?**

A: Yes, the insurer may rescind if the falsity is material, and A cannot defeat rescission by pointing to the caretaker source or by using the representation to contradict the express policy term.
Under Secs. 44–46, a representation is false when facts fail to correspond with its assertions, falsity in a material point entitles the injured party to rescind, and materiality follows concealment rules. Sec. 40 bars a representation from qualifying an express provision, and the Sec. 43 second-hand-information excuse fails when the source is the insured's agent whose duty is to inform or when no source explanation cures a material misstatement of own fact.
The timber construction contradicts the concrete assertion so the representation is false. A concrete-vs-timber wall plainly affects underwriting and is material. Because the policy expressly states concrete throughout, the representation cannot qualify or soften that term. The caretaker remark does not shift responsibility where A adopted the statement as his own to obtain cover.
The insurer is entitled to rescind from the time the representation became false, subject to proof of materiality at trial.

## 5. Warranties

> Sec. 67. "A warranty is either expressed or implied."
>
> Sec. 68. "A warranty may relate to the past, the present, the future, or to any or all of these."
>
> Sec. 69. "No particular form of words is necessary to create a warranty."
>
> Sec. 70. "Without prejudice to Section 51, every express warranty, made at or before the execution of a policy, must be contained in the policy itself, or in another instrument signed by the insured and referred to in the policy as making a part of it."
>
> Sec. 71. "A statement in a policy, of a matter relating to the person or thing insured, or to the risk, as fact, is an express warranty thereof."
>
> Sec. 72. "A statement in a policy, which imparts that it is intended to do or not to do a thing which materially affects the risk, is a warranty that such act or omission shall take place."
>
> Sec. 74. "The violation of a material warranty, or other material provision of a policy, on the part of either party thereto, entitles the other to rescind."
>
> Sec. 75. "A policy may declare that a violation of specified provisions thereof shall avoid it, otherwise the breach of an immaterial provision does not avoid the policy."
>
> Sec. 76. "A breach of warranty without fraud merely exonerates an insurer from the time that it occurs, or where it is broken in its inception, prevents the policy from attaching to the risk."

| Element/Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Definition and kinds | Warranty is either expressed or implied (Sec. 67) |
| Time coverage | May relate to past, present, future, or any or all (Sec. 68) |
| Form | No particular form of words necessary (Sec. 69) |
| Formality for express warranty | Made at or before execution, must be in the policy or in a signed instrument referred to in the policy as part of it, without prejudice to Sec. 51 (Sec. 70) |
| Warranty of fact | Policy statement of matter relating to person, thing, or risk, as fact, is an express warranty (Sec. 71) |
| Warranty of act or omission | Policy statement importing intent to do or not do a thing materially affecting risk is a warranty that it shall take place (Sec. 72) |
| Future-warranty excuse (Sec. 73, substance) | Non-performance of a future warranty is excused if loss happens first, or performance becomes unlawful or impossible |
| Material breach | Violation of material warranty or other material provision entitles the other party to rescind (Sec. 74) |
| Immaterial breach | Policy may declare specified violations avoid it; otherwise breach of immaterial provision does not avoid policy (Sec. 75) |
| Breach without fraud | Merely exonerates insurer from time of breach, or if broken in inception prevents attachment to risk (Sec. 76) |

| Express vs Implied Warranty | Express | Implied |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Written into policy or incorporated signed instrument (Sec. 70) | Imposed by law, read into contract though unwritten |
| Examples | Hydrant, hose, pressure, night-watchman clauses; Secs. 71–72 statements | Seaworthiness, legality of venture, no deviation in marine |
| Proof | Show policy text or incorporated instrument | Show legal implication applicable to that class of insurance |

| Affirmative vs Promissory Warranty | Affirmative | Promissory |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Affirms past or present fact (Sec. 71) | Promises future act or omission affecting risk (Sec. 72) |
| Breach timing | Broken in inception if untrue at execution | Broken when promised act fails; Sec. 73 excuses apply |
| Effect if without fraud | Prevents policy from attaching to risk (Sec. 76) | Exonerates insurer from time breach occurs (Sec. 76) |

| Material vs Immaterial Breach | Material | Immaterial |
|---|---|---|
| Test | Affects the risk or is declared material by parties | Does not affect risk and not declared avoidable |
| Remedy | Rescission under Sec. 74 | No avoidance unless policy declares violation avoids it (Sec. 75) |
| Sec. 76 overlay | Without fraud, exoneration from breach or non-attachment if in inception | Same exoneration logic, but avoidance denied for immaterial breach |

### Case: *Qua Chee Gan v. Law Union and Rock Insurance*, G.R. No. L-4611, Dec 17, 1955

Facts: Fire policies contained warranties on fire hydrants, hose, water pressure, and fire brigade. After loss by fire, the insurer denied liability for breach of those warranties.

Issue: Whether breach of the fire-appliance warranties avoided the policies where the insurer knew the actual conditions at issuance and continuously collected premiums.

Held: The insurer is liable and is estopped from invoking the warranties. The insurer inspected and knew the premises lacked the warranted appliances yet issued the policies and accepted premiums. Uberrimae fidei binds the insurer more strictly. Ambiguities are construed against the insurer. Alleged false swearing in proofs of loss must be wilful to forfeit.

*Doctrine: Knowledge plus continued acceptance of premiums waives or estops breach-of-warranty defenses. Insurance is uberrimae fidei on both sides, more strictly against the insurer.*

> Exam trap: Do not void on paper breach alone. Check insurer knowledge, inspection, and premium acceptance first, and require wilfulness for false-swearing forfeiture.

**Q: A fire policy warrants "two 2.5-inch hydrants with 100 psi at all times." At issuance the insurer inspects, sees one weak hydrant, issues anyway, and collects premiums for two years. After a fire, may it avoid solely for breach of that warranty with no fraud shown?**

A: No, the insurer cannot avoid on that warranty alone where it knew the true condition and accepted premiums, and without fraud Sec. 76 gives only exoneration-while-breached rather than automatic avoidance.
Secs. 74–76 distinguish material breach entitling rescission from immaterial breach that does not avoid absent policy declaration, and a non-fraudulent breach merely exonerates from occurrence or prevents attachment if broken in inception. Qua Chee Gan adds waiver and estoppel where the insurer knew the deficiency and took premiums, with uberrimae fidei and contra-proferentem against the insurer.
The hydrant warranty is material on its face, but the insurer's prior inspection and continued premium collection estop it from invoking a condition it knowingly wrote over a known non-compliance. With no fraud, Sec. 76 does not let it treat the whole policy as void ab initio for a supervening shortfall.
The breach defense fails on estoppel and waiver. The insurer remains liable subject to any valid Sec. 73 excuse analysis, which does not help it here.

## 6. Conditions; Other Insurance Clause

> Sec. 63. "A condition, stipulation, or agreement in any policy of insurance, limiting the time for commencing an action thereunder to a period of less than one (1) year from the time when the cause of action accrues, is void."

| Element/Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Condition defined | Contingency or event on which enforceability or loss of rights depends; suspensive or resolutory |
| Other-insurance clause defined | Condition requiring disclosure of co-insurance; breach commonly works forfeiture or avoidance per policy |
| Requisite 1: same subject matter | Both policies cover the identical property or subject |
| Requisite 2: same interest | Both cover the same insurable interest in that subject |
| Requisite 3: same risk or peril | Both cover the same risk for the same period in whole or in part |
| Effect of concurrence | All three present plus non-disclosure equals breach and forfeiture under the clause |
| Suit-time floor (Sec. 63) | Any policy condition cutting suit time to less than one year from accrual is void; one year or longer is valid |
| Waiver | Insurer collecting premiums with knowledge of other insurance waives the clause |

| Condition vs Warranty vs Exclusion | Condition | Warranty | Exclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Function | Triggers, suspends, or forfeits coverage on an event | Affirms fact or promises act as part of contract | Carves a peril, property, or use out of coverage |
| Location | Often a clause like other-insurance or suit-time limit | In policy or incorporated instrument (Sec. 70) | In insuring agreement or endorsement |
| Breach effect | Forfeiture or suspension per clause; Sec. 63 voids short suit limits | Secs. 74–76 rescission or exoneration | No liability for excluded matter; no rescission needed |
| Bar example | Waiver by knowledge plus premiums | Waiver and estoppel under Qua Chee Gan | Ambiguity construed against insurer |

| Other-Insurance Requisites | Same Subject Matter | Same Interest | Same Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question | Is the identical property covered? | Is the identical ownership or insurable interest covered? | Is the identical peril and overlapping period covered? |
| Failure effect | Different property means no breach | Different interest (e.g., mortgagor vs mortgagee) means no breach | Different peril or non-overlapping cover means no breach |
| Proof | Compare policies' descriptions and schedules | Compare named insureds and capacities | Compare perils, amounts, and periods |

### Case: *Multi-Ware Manufacturing v. Cibeles Insurance*, G.R. No. 230528, Feb 1, 2021

Facts: Fire policies on factory machinery contained an other-insurance clause requiring notice of co-insurance. The insured obtained additional policies on the same machinery without disclosure, then claimed after fire loss.

Issue: Whether undisclosed co-insurance on the same machinery breached the other-insurance clause and forfeited the claim.

Held: Yes. The co-insurance covered the same subject matter, same interest, and same risk, so non-disclosure breached the clause and worked forfeiture under its terms.

*Doctrine: Other-insurance forfeiture requires identity of subject matter, interest, and risk. Undisclosed concurring cover on all three breaches the condition.*

> Exam trap: Match all three identities before forfeiting. Same building but different interest or different peril is not a breach.

### Case: *La O v. Yek Tong Lin Fire & Marine*, G.R. No. 33131, Dec 30, 1930

Facts: Insurer invoked an other-insurance clause to avoid payment, pointing to other fire policies taken by related parties. The other policies covered different interests in the property.

Issue: Whether the other policies breached the other-insurance clause where interests differed, and whether premium acceptance with knowledge waived the defense.

Held: No breach. The other policies did not cover the same interest, so the triple-identity test failed. The insurer, having collected premiums with knowledge of the situation, also waived the defense.

*Doctrine: No breach exists without the same subject, same interest, and same risk. Insurer knowledge plus premium collection waives the other-insurance defense.*

> Exam trap: Different capacity or interest defeats forfeiture even over the same building. Always check waiver by knowledge and premiums.

**Q: A insures factory machines with X containing an other-insurance clause, then secretly insures the same machines with Y against the same fire risk for an overlapping period. Separately, the X policy says suit must be filed within 6 months of loss. After fire, X invokes both defenses. Decide each defense.**

A: X wins on the other-insurance breach but loses on the 6-month suit limit, which is void, leaving the insured the lawful period to sue.
Breach of an other-insurance condition forfeits where the same subject matter, same interest, and same risk concur with non-disclosure under Multi-Ware, while La O requires all three and recognizes waiver. Sec. 63 voids any policy condition limiting suit to less than one year from accrual.
The Y policy hits all three identities on the same machines, same ownership interest, and same fire peril, so non-disclosure breaches the X clause with no waiver shown. The 6-month limit falls below the Sec. 63 floor and is void, leaving the insured the lawful period to sue.
Forfeiture on the co-insurance ground stands, but the time-bar defense fails as void under Sec. 63.

## 7. Exclusion/Exception; Burden of Proof

### Verbatim Code

> Unless otherwise provided by the policy, an insurer is liable for a loss of which a peril insured against was the proximate cause, although a peril not contemplated by the contract may have been a remote cause of the loss; but he is not liable for a loss of which the peril insured against was only a remote cause.

> Source: Sec. 86, Insurance Code (RA 10607).

> Where a peril is especially excepted in a contract of insurance, a loss, which would not have occurred but for such peril, is thereby excepted although the immediate cause of the loss was a peril which was not excepted.

> Source: Sec. 88, Insurance Code (RA 10607).

> An insurer is not liable for a loss caused by the willful act or through the connivance of the insured; but he is not exonerated by the negligence of the insured, or of the insurance agents or others.

> Source: Sec. 89, Insurance Code (RA 10607).

### Elements table

| Element/Requisite | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Statutory exclusion (willful act) | Insurer not liable for loss caused by willful act or connivance of insured per Sec. 89 |
| Negligence rule | Negligence of insured, agents, or others does not exonerate insurer per Sec. 89 |
| Proximate cause rule | Insurer liable where peril insured against was proximate cause though unlisted peril was remote cause per Sec. 86 |
| Remote-cause non-liability | Insurer not liable where peril insured against was only remote cause per Sec. 86 |
| Especially excepted peril | Loss which would not have occurred but for excepted peril is excepted even if immediate cause was non-excepted peril per Sec. 88 |
| Contractual exception | Parties may add express exclusions; construed strictly against insurer; insurer proves exception |
| Burden sequence | Insured proves prima facie case (policy, loss within coverage); burden shifts to insurer to prove excepted risk |
| Fraudulent overclaim | Padded claim voids policy for fraud; insurer must still prove fraud with records |

### Distinction tables

#### Exclusion vs concealment vs warranty breach

| Basis | Exclusion/Exception | Concealment | Warranty breach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Carves out a peril or cause from coverage | Failure to disclose material fact at inception | Breach of promise or condition in policy |
| Effect | No liability for that loss even if policy valid | Rescission of contract | Rescission or forfeiture per terms and Sec. 74 |
| Who proves | Insurer proves loss falls in exception | Insurer proves concealment and materiality | Insurer proves breach and materiality |
| Cure | Cannot cure; loss simply not covered | Waivable by terms or neglect to inquire per Sec. 33 | Defenses of waiver and estoppel may apply |

#### Proximate vs remote cause (Sec. 86)

| Basis | Proximate cause | Remote cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Test | Dominant, efficient cause setting loss in motion | Subsidiary or background circumstance |
| Insured peril proximate | Insurer liable though remote peril unlisted | Not applicable |
| Insured peril only remote | Insurer not liable | Loss attributed to other proximate peril |
| Example | Murder as proximate cause of death | Defective drain pipe as internal vice, not sea peril |

#### Accident vs intentional act

| Basis | Accident | Intentional |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standpoint | From victim's view, unforeseen and unexpected | Willful act or connivance of insured |
| Murder/assault | Accidental as to victim; covered unless excluded | Assailant's intent does not make it intentional as to victim |
| Needless peril | Mere negligence still accident | Only reckless risk bordering on suicide is excluded |
| Stab wounds example | Not applicable | Nine close-range stab wounds show intentionally inflicted injury |

#### Burden: insured prima facie then insurer proves exception

| Stage | Who | What |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stage 1 | Insured/beneficiary | Prove policy, payment of premium, loss within general coverage |
| Stage 2 | Insurer | Prove excepted or excluded risk by evidence |
| Stage 3 | Court | Construe ambiguity against insurer; padded-claim fraud voids policy |
| Costs note | Good-faith resistance | No damages against insurer for good-faith denial |

### Case cards

### Case: *Sun Insurance v. CA*, G.R. No. 92383, July 17, 1992

Facts: The insured pointed a gun he believed was unloaded at his temple and fired. The insurer invoked the needless-peril or intentional-act exception under the accident policy.

Issue: Whether death by own gun believed unloaded is an accident or an excluded intentional or needless-peril act.

Held: The death was accidental and is therefore covered. The needless-peril exception requires a reckless risk bordering on suicide. Mere negligence is no bar under Sec. 89. The insurer's good-faith resistance did not warrant damages.

*Doctrine: Accident is judged from the victim's standpoint. Negligence does not exonerate the insurer. Only a willful act or connivance of the insured excludes coverage.*

> Exam trap: Do not call self-inflicted gunplay intentional just because the insured pulled the trigger. Ask whether the risk bordered on suicide.

### Case: *Finman General Assurance v. CA*, G.R. No. 100970, September 2, 1992

Facts: The insured was killed by assailants. The insurer denied the accident claim, arguing that murder was intentional and excluded.

Issue: Whether murder or assault is accidental as to the victim under an accident policy with enumerated exclusions.

Held: Covered. Murder is accidental from the victim's standpoint. Expressio unius applies here. The policy listed ten exclusions and murder was not among them. Exclusions are strictly construed.

*Doctrine: Where the policy enumerates exclusions, unlisted perils are covered. The assailant's intent is not the insured's intent.*

> Exam trap: If examiner lists ten exclusions, check expressio unius before importing a willful-act defense.

### Case: *Biagtan v. Insular Life*, G.R. No. L-25579, March 29, 1972

Facts: The insured sustained nine close-range stab wounds. The beneficiary claimed double indemnity for accidental death.

Issue: Whether multiple close-range stab wounds are accidentally or intentionally inflicted within double-indemnity exception.

Held: The injuries were intentionally inflicted so the double-indemnity exception applies. The case is distinguished from *Calanoc* on its facts.

*Doctrine: Number, location, and closeness of wounds can establish intentionally inflicted injury.*

> Exam trap: *Biagtan* is the counterpoint to *Sun* and *Finman*. Same accident policy, opposite result on the facts.

### Case: *Go Tiaoco v. Union Insurance Society of Canton*, G.R. No. 13983, September 1, 1919

Facts: The cargo was damaged through the defective drain pipe of the ship. The claim was made under a marine policy against perils of the sea.

Issue: Whether internal defect (defective pipe) is a peril of the sea.

Held: Not covered. Defective pipe is a peril of the ship or internal vice, not a sea peril.

*Doctrine: Proximate cause analysis distinguishes sea perils from internal defects of vessel.*

> Exam trap: Do not label every loss at sea a sea peril. Trace proximate cause to vessel condition as against sea action.

### Case: *United Merchants v. Country Bankers*, G.R. No. 198588, July 11, 2012

Facts: The fire claim for P50M was contradicted by records showing about P1.9M in stocks. The insurer alleged fraud and a padded claim.

Issue: Whether padded claim voids policy and how burden of proof applies to excepted risk.

Held: The policy is void for fraud. The insured first makes a prima facie case. The insurer then proves the excepted risk or the fraud that voids coverage.

*Doctrine: A fraudulent overclaim voids the policy. The burden shifts to the insurer to prove the exception after a prima facie showing.*

> Exam trap: State the two-step burden sequence explicitly to earn points. Do not collapse it into one sentence.

### Historic Q — 2019 Mercantile Law A.6, contention 2 (tested here as exclusion)

**2019 Bar Q: In January 2016, Mr. H was issued a life insurance policy by XYZ Insurance Co., wherein his wife, Mrs. W, was designated as the sole beneficiary. Unbeknownst to XYZ Insurance Co., however, Mr. H had been previously diagnosed with colon cancer, the fact of which Mr. H had concealed during the entire time his insurance policy was being processed. In January 2019, Mr. H unfortunately committed suicide. Due to her husband's death, Mrs. W, as beneficiary, filed a claim with XYZ Insurance Co. to recover the proceeds of the late Mr. H's life insurance policy. However, XYZ Insurance Co. resisted the claim, contending that: 1. the policy is void ab initio because Mr. H fraudulently concealed or misrepresented his medical condition, i.e., his colon cancer; and 2. as an insurer in a life insurance policy, it cannot be held liable in case of suicide. Rule on each of XYZ Insurance Co.'s contentions. (5%)**

> Scope note: Contention 1 (concealment and incontestability) is answered in §8. This §7 answers contention 2 only (suicide as exclusion).

A: Contention 2 fails. The January 2019 suicide, about three years after the January 2016 issue, falls outside any suicide exclusion.
Under Sec. 183 the insurer in a life contract is liable for suicide committed after the policy has been in force two years from issue or last reinstatement, and exclusions are strictly construed against the insurer, which bears the burden of proving the exception after the beneficiary's prima facie case.
The policy ran from January 2016 to January 2019, about three years, so the two-year suicide threshold is passed and XYZ proved no insanity issue or shorter policy period. Its blanket citation of suicide, without clause or proof, cannot carry an exclusion defense that is construed against it.
Contention 2 is denied and XYZ is liable on this ground, subject to the §8 incontestability analysis that independently bars contention 1.

## 8. Incontestable Clause

### Verbatim Code

> Whenever a right to rescind a contract of insurance is given to the insurer by any provision of this chapter, such right must be exercised previous to the commencement of an action on the contract.

> Source: Sec. 48, Insurance Code (RA 10607), first paragraph.

> After a policy of life insurance made payable on the death of the insured shall have been in force during the lifetime of the insured for a period of two (2) years from the date of its issue or of its last reinstatement, the insurer cannot prove that the policy is void ab initio or is rescindible by reason of the fraudulent concealment or misrepresentation of the insured or his agent.

> Source: Sec. 48, Insurance Code (RA 10607), second paragraph.

### Elements table

| Element/Requisite | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Requisite 1: life policy | Only life insurance; non-life policies never become incontestable |
| Requisite 2: payable on death | Policy made payable on death of the insured |
| Requisite 3: in force 2 years | Continuously in force for two years; lapses break continuity |
| Requisite 4: during insured's lifetime | Two years must run during lifetime of insured |
| Requisite 5: from issue or last reinstatement | Period counts from date of issue or date of last reinstatement |
| Requisite 6: only vs fraud defenses | Bars only proof of void ab initio or rescindibility by fraudulent concealment or misrepresentation |
| Rationale | Force insurer to investigate promptly; give insured security after paying premiums |
| Commencement | Runs from issue or last reinstatement; reinstatement restarts the clock |
| Connivance-with-agent allegation | Insurer cannot evade Sec. 48 by alleging the insured connived with the insurer's own agent, since the bar expressly covers misrepresentation of the insured or his agent |
| Death within 2 years | Insurer may still contest if death occurs within 2-year period and action timely |
| Rescind-before-action | Even within period, right to rescind must be exercised previous to action on contract |

### Distinction tables

#### Contestable vs incontestable

| Basis | Contestable (within 2 years) | Incontestable (after 2 years) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | Insurer may prove fraud, concealment, misrepresentation | Insurer cannot prove void ab initio or rescindibility on those grounds |
| Duty | Insurer must investigate and rescind timely | Insurer deemed to have waived fraud defense |
| Procedure | Rescind previous to action on contract | Styling as nullity action does not evade bar |
| Policy | Protects insurer from fraud | Protects insured and beneficiary repose |

#### When inapplicable

| Situation | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Not life insurance | Sec. 48 does not apply |
| Not payable on death | Requirement fails |
| Not in force 2 years during lifetime | Still contestable |
| Defense other than concealment or misrepresentation | Bar does not cover it; e.g., non-payment of premium |
| Lack of insurable interest of beneficiary where insured took own life | Per *Aban*, insured's own insurable interest in own life suffices; do not invent beneficiary-interest voidness |
| Styling as void ab initio | Does not evade bar per *Manila Bankers*; substance controls |

### Case cards

### Case: *Manila Bankers Life v. Aban*, G.R. No. 175666, July 29, 2013

Facts: The life policy had been in force for 2 years and 7 months. The insurer resisted payment alleging fraudulent concealment or misrepresentation and styled its action as one for void ab initio.

Issue: Whether insurer after 2 years in force may still prove policy void ab initio or rescindible for fraudulent concealment or misrepresentation.

Held: No. Sec. 48 bars such proof after 2 years in force during the insured's lifetime from issue or last reinstatement. The insurer must investigate within the period. Styling the suit as a nullity action does not evade the bar. The insured's own insurable interest in his own life suffices.

*Doctrine: Incontestability bars post-2-year fraud defenses. The form of the action cannot circumvent the substance of Sec. 48.*

> Exam trap: Do not credit void-ab-initio labeling. Check the 2-year clock and the fraud-only scope.

### Historic Q — 2019 Mercantile Law A.6, contention 1 (tested here as incontestability)

**2019 Bar Q: In January 2016, Mr. H was issued a life insurance policy by XYZ Insurance Co., wherein his wife, Mrs. W, was designated as the sole beneficiary. > Scope note: Contention 2 (suicide) is answered in §7. This §8 answers contention 1 only.

A: Contention 1 is barred. XYZ cannot prove void ab initio or rescindibility for fraudulent concealment after 3 years in force.
Sec. 48 bars proof of void ab initio or rescindibility by fraudulent concealment or misrepresentation after a life policy payable on death has been in force during the insured's lifetime for 2 years from issue or last reinstatement, with rescission required before action on the contract.

XYZ must pay as to contention 1. Its remedy was timely investigation and pre-action rescission within 2 years.

#### War exclusion vs war coverage by rider

| Basis | Excluded war risk | Covered war risk by rider |
| --- | --- | --- |

### Case cards

### Case: *Malayan Insurance v. CA (TKC Marketing)*, G.R. No. 119599, 1997

Facts: The cargo policy carried a deleted F.C. and S. clause with added Institute War Clauses. The goods were lost through civil arrest or authority restraint. The insurer invoked the war exclusion.

Issue: Whether civil arrest was excluded or covered given deleted F.C. and S. clause and added war clauses.

Held: The loss is covered. Deletion removed the war exclusion and the Institute War Clauses made civil arrest a covered war risk. War-risk allocation follows the policy as rewritten.

*Doctrine: Deleted exclusions cannot be revived. Added war clauses define coverage.*

> Exam trap: Read the policy as rewritten, not the printed form. Deletion plus rider controls.

### Drill — §9

**Q: Cargo insured under marine policy with F.C. and S. clause deleted and Institute War Clauses attached is seized by civil authority. Insurer denies, citing the war exclusion in the printed form. Rule on the denial.**

A: The insurer is liable because civil arrest is covered under the policy as rewritten. Sec. 88 excepts only perils especially excepted as written, and the deleted F.C. and S. language plus the added Institute War Clauses restore war-risk coverage strictly construed in the insured's favor. Deletion erased the printed war exclusion and the rider affirmatively covered civil arrest, so the insurer cannot revive deleted text and has not proved a surviving exception. The insurer must therefore pay the claim because the war defense fails on the policy as rewritten.

## 10. Defenses of Insured against Revocation

### Verbatim Code

> Whenever a right to rescind a contract of insurance is given to the insurer by any provision of this chapter, such right must be exercised previous to the commencement of an action on the contract.

> Source: Sec. 48, Insurance Code (RA 10607), first paragraph.

Paraphrase note: Sec. 33 recognizes waiver by policy terms or neglect to inquire, and Sec. 74 governs rescission for material breach of warranty. Their verbatim texts appear under §§3 and 5 above. Here only the substance is applied as a defense.

### Elements table

| Element/Requisite | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Timeliness bar | Insurer must rescind previous to action on contract; late rescission barred |
| Incontestability bar | After 2 years life in force, fraud defenses barred per Sec. 48 second paragraph |
| Waiver | Intentional relinquishment of known right, e.g., collecting premiums with knowledge |
| Estoppel | Insurer by conduct induces reliance; cannot contradict prior position |
| Guaranteed insurability | Optional benefit allowing added coverage without new evidence of insurability |
| Knowledge requirement | Waiver and estoppel need insurer knowledge of facts at time of conduct |
| Premium-collection signal | Continued collection after knowledge signals waiver |

### Distinction tables

#### Waiver vs estoppel vs timeliness bar

| Basis | Waiver | Estoppel | Timeliness bar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Intentional relinquishment by insurer | Conduct inducing reliance by insured | Sec. 48 time limit |
| Focus | Insurer's intent and knowledge | Insured's reliance and prejudice | Procedural timing |
| Example | Collecting premiums knowing concealment | Assuring coverage then denying after loss | Suing first then rescinding |
| Effect | Right relinquished | Insurer barred from contradiction | Rescission ineffective |

### Case cards

### Case: *Qua Chee Gan*, G.R. No. L-4611, 1955

Facts: Insurer with knowledge of facts continued to treat policy as subsisting and accepted benefits, then sought to deny liability.

Issue: Whether insurer that by conduct recognized policy is estopped or deemed to have waived forfeiture.

Held: Barred. Insurer's conduct with knowledge estops denial and evidences waiver.

*Doctrine: Insurer that affirms policy with knowledge cannot later contradict that position.*

> Exam trap: Name the defense precisely. Waiver is intent while estoppel is reliance.

### Case: *La O v. Yek Tong Lin*, G.R. No. 33131, 1930

Facts: Insurer collected premiums despite knowledge of ground for forfeiture, then invoked forfeiture after loss.

Issue: Whether premium collection with knowledge waives forfeiture.

Held: Waived. Collection with knowledge is intentional relinquishment of forfeiture right.

*Doctrine: Knowing premium collection waives known forfeiture.*

> Exam trap: Knowledge at collection time is the trigger. Later discovery does not retroactively waive.

### Case: *Manila Bankers Life v. Aban*, G.R. No. 175666, July 29, 2013

Facts: Insurer delayed fraud challenge until after 2 years 7 months in force and after action commenced.

Issue: Whether late rescission survives timeliness and incontestability bars.

Held: No. Both bars apply. The insurer must rescind previous to action, and no post-2-year fraud proof is allowed.

*Doctrine: Timeliness and incontestability are independent defenses of insured.*

> Exam trap: Plead both bars separately. Passing one does not excuse failing the other.

### Drill — §10

**Q: Insurer learns of concealment in Year 1, keeps collecting premiums for a year, then after loss and after beneficiary sues, declares rescission. The insured pleads waiver, estoppel, and timeliness. Rule on the defenses.**

A: All three defenses succeed, so rescission is barred and the insurer must pay. Knowing premium collection waives forfeiture, conduct inducing reliance estops contradiction, and Sec. 48 requires rescission previous to action on the contract. One year of post-knowledge collection shows intentional relinquishment and induced reliance, and the post-suit rescission violates the previous-to-action rule, with incontestability as an added bar if the life-policy and 2-year requisites are met. Rescission is therefore ineffective because the insurer is barred by waiver, estoppel, and timeliness.

## Exam Traps

1. Concealment needs no fraud and no intent. Representation needs material falsity, so do not import fraud into Sec. 27.
2. Materiality is judged by probable influence on underwriting, never by whether the concealed fact caused the loss (Sec. 31).
3. Causation between the concealed fact and the loss is not an element. Sec. 88 but-for causation belongs to exclusions, not concealment.
4. Agent knowledge is insurer knowledge: an approving officer who knew the illness destroys concealment (2023 Q10).
5. Sec. 30 matters still compel answers to direct inquiries. Silence is excused only absent inquiry.
6. Judgment and opinion need never be disclosed even upon inquiry (Sec. 35). Facts must be.
7. A representation cannot qualify an express policy term but may qualify an implied warranty (Sec. 40).
8. A representation withdrawn or altered before insurance is effected is gone. After effectivity it sticks (Sec. 41).
9. Promissory representation breached later rescinds from when it becomes false, not from inception (Sec. 45).
10. Sec. 70 formality: an express warranty outside the policy and outside an incorporated signed instrument is not an express warranty.
11. Breach without fraud is exoneration or non-attachment, not voidness ab initio (Sec. 76). Do not upgrade it.
12. Triple-identity all-or-nothing: same building with different interest is La O, no breach, not Multi-Ware.
13. Knowing premium collection waives forfeiture. Pre-knowledge collection waives nothing, so date the knowledge.
14. Six-month suit limits are void under Sec. 63. The suit proceeds under the lawful period.
15. Accident is judged from the victim's view (Finman). The assailant's intent does not make it intentional as to the victim, except where the clause asks about infliction, and nine close-range wounds satisfy it (Biagtan).
16. Negligence never exonerates under Sec. 89. Only willful act or connivance does, so Sun Insurance's gunplay stayed an accident.
17. Void-ab-initio styling never evades Sec. 48. Count the 2-year clock from issue or last reinstatement (Aban and 2019 A.6).
18. Death inside 2 years keeps contestability. Suicide inside 2 years is excluded but insanity suicide always pays (Secs. 48, 183).
19. Deleted exclusions stay deleted: read war cover from the rider, not the printed form (Malayan/TKC).
20. Rescind before suing or being sued on the contract. Post-action rescission is dead even within 2 years (Sec. 48(1)).
