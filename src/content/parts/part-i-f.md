## 1. Defined; Governing Law and State Policy; Insurance Commission

> "Pre-need plans" are contracts, agreements, deeds or plans for the benefit of the planholders which provide for the performance of future service/s, payment of monetary considerations or delivery of other benefits at the time of actual need or agreed maturity date, as specified therein, in exchange for cash or installment amounts with or without interest or insurance coverage and includes life, pension, education, interment and other plans, instruments contracts or deeds as may in the future he determined by the Commission.
>
> — Sec. 4(b), RA 9829 (verbatim from LawPhil full text, where "he" for "be" is the source page's own rendering)

> It is the policy of the State to regulate the establishment of pre-need companies and to place their operation on sound, efficient and stable basis to derive the optimum advantage from them in the mobilization of savings and to prevent and mitigate, as far as practicable, practices prejudicial to public interest and the protection of planholders. The State shall hereby regulate, through an empowered agency, pre-need companies based on prudential principles to promote soundness, stability and sustainable growth of the pre-need industry.
>
> — Sec. 2, RA 9829

> Any doubt in the interpretation and implementation of any provision in this Code shall be interpreted in favor of the rights and interests of the planholder.
>
> — Sec. 3, RA 9829

> All pre-need companies, as defined under this Act, shall be under the primary and exclusive supervision and regulation of the Insurance Commission.
>
> — Sec. 5, par. 1, RA 9829

### Case: *Visitacion Gavina Gaw v. CA, RTC Pasay Br. 113, Pacific Plans, Inc.*, G.R. No. 147748, Apr. 19, 2006

Facts: In 1982 Gaw bought a Provincial Memorial Plan (Agreement No. 93945-5). Her mother died the morning of July 9, 1996. Her brother at once engaged Funeraria Baluyot, which embalmed the body and supplied a casket. Only that evening did Gaw notify Pacific Plans of her intent to assign the plan to her mother. The company refused memorial services. Gaw later dealt with accredited Funeraria Tolete, sought a replacement casket, sold a P150,000.00 farm lot for P50,000.00, and incurred P23,500.00 funeral expenses. MeTC ruled for her. RTC reversed. CA affirmed the dismissal.

Issue: Whether the provider is liable for damages for refusing the memorial services.

Held: Petition denied. The plan is the law between the parties. Clear terms control literally. Par. III gave Pacific the sole and exclusive right to arrange services with a mortuary of its choice, and notice directly to and acknowledged by Pacific before acting was imperative, time being of the essence. Evening notice after embalming and casketing preempted that prerogative and breached the plan. The casket upgrade likewise breached par. I(A): the plan delivered the pre-selected casket, and the different casket's cost is Gaw's alone since Pacific never consented and contracts bind only parties. The plan is a contract of adhesion but as binding as any contract since adherence is rejectable. Courts make no new contracts to avoid hardship.

Doctrine: A pre-need plan's clear stipulations control literally. Delayed notice that preempts the provider's sole arrangement right, combined with off-plan substitution, releases the provider. Adhesion binds.

> Exam trap: Sec. 3 pro-planholder construction does not rewrite clear plan terms — Gaw enforces the literal stipulation (immediate acknowledged notice, a company-chosen mortuary, and the pre-selected casket) even while calling the plan an adhesion contract.

Historic Bar question: gap. No verbatim pre-need question appears in the 2016-2025 Bar file (search returns no memorial, plan, or RA 9829 item, since Quiz 1 reproduces only insurance-code questions). If the examiner asks the hospital-share problem, answer with the drill below.

**Q: A hospital gives subscribers, their spouses, and dependents free or discounted board, procedures, and drugs as subscriber incentives. Decide whether these perks are pre-need plans or HMO contracts.**

A: The perks are neither pre-need plans nor HMO contracts. They are mere shareholder incentives.
The controlling rules are Sec. 4(b) of RA 9829, which requires a contract for future service, money, or benefit at actual need or agreed maturity in exchange for cash or installment amounts. EO 192 s. 2015 defines an HMO contract as pre-agreed health care services for a fixed prepaid fee over a period through a provider network.
Applied here, the perks ride on stock subscription payments rather than plan consideration and promise no benefit at actual need or agreed maturity. They likewise involve no fixed prepaid fee and no provider network.
The perks therefore fall outside both Sec. 4(b) and the HMO definition and stand as mere shareholder incentives.

## 2. Parties; Other Regulated Persons; Suspension/Revocation

> "Pre-need company" refers to any corporation registered with the Commission and authorized/licensed to sell or offer to sell pre-need plans. The term "pre-need company" also refers to schools, memorial chapels, banks, nonbank financial institutions and other entities which have also been authorized/licensed to sell or offer to sell pre-need plans insofar as their pre-need activities or business are concerned.
>
> — Sec. 4(c), RA 9829

> "Planholder" refers to any natural or juridical person who purchases pre-need plans from a pre-need company for whom or for whose beneficiaries' benefits are to be delivered, as stipulated and guaranteed by the pre-need company. The term includes the assignee, transferee and any successor-in-interest of the planholder.
>
> — Sec. 4(d), RA 9829

> "Beneficiary" refers to the person designated by the planholder as the recipient of the benefits in the pre-need plan.
>
> — Sec. 4(e), RA 9829

> "Sales counselors" refers to natural persons who are engaged in the sale of, or offer to sell, or counsel of prospective planholders for the purpose of selling, whether or not on commission basis, pre-need plans upon the authority of the pre-need company.
>
> — Sec. 4(h), RA 9829

> If the Commission is of the opinion, upon examination or other evidence, that any pre-need company is in an unsound condition, or that it has failed to comply with the provisions of law or regulations, or that its condition or method of business is such as to render its proceedings hazardous to the public or to its planholders, or that its paid-up capital stock is impaired or deficient, the Commission is authorized to suspend or revoke all certificates of authority granted to such pre-need company, its officers and agents, after due notice or hearing. No new business shall thereafter be done by such company or for such company by its agent in the Philippines.
>
> — Sec. 48, par. 1, RA 9829

> The Commission may not lift the order of suspension or revocation of the said authority until the concerned pre-need company shall have submitted a viable business plan showing the company's estimated receipts and disbursements, as well as the basis therefor for the next succeeding three (3) years.
>
> — Sec. 48, par. 2, RA 9829

Historic Bar question: gap for §2. Use the drill below.

**Q: A company offers pension plans online with bank-deposit payment instructions but holds no license. Decide whether the offer is actionable.**

A: The online offer is actionable, and a cease-and-desist order against it is proper.
The controlling rules are Sec. 10, which bars operation as a pre-need company without a Commission license. Sec. 18 requires prior Commission approval of advertising. Sec. 53(c) fines unauthorized sales on a 30%/40%/license-loss ladder. Sec. 54(a)-(b) punishes unregistered selling with one (1) year of imprisonment and triple-price fines.
Applied here, the Primamanila-pattern facts — a website offer of pension plans with bank-deposit payments and no license — breach both the licensing gate and the advertising gate.
The Commission may therefore issue a cease-and-desist order, and fines plus criminal exposure attach.

Historic Bar question: gap for §3. Use the drill below.

**Q: After a planholder's mother dies, the family at once engages an outside funeral home that embalms the body and supplies a casket, notifies the provider only that evening, and later substitutes a different casket. Decide whether the provider owes memorial services.**

A: The provider owes no memorial services, and the dismissal of the claim stands.
The controlling rules are that the plan is the law between the parties so its clear stipulations control literally, and that Sec. 3 liberality for the planholder cannot rewrite those stipulations.
Applied here, the sole-and-exclusive arrangement right and the imperative acknowledged-notice requirement were preempted by the evening notice after embalming and casketing, and the pre-selected-casket term was breached by the upgrade whose cost falls on the family alone.
There is therefore no liability for refusing the services, and the CA dismissal stands.

**Q: A planholder misses six months of installments after receiving only one notice, and the company cancels the plan. Decide whether the cancellation is valid.**

A: The cancellation is invalid, and the company must accept reinstatement or reimburse the late payments.
The controlling rule is Sec. 23, which requires a 60-day grace period, a 2-year reinstatement window from grace lapse with no cancellation inside it, and two written 30-day notices — one after grace expiry and one after reinstatement-period expiry.
Applied here, six months of nonpayment still falls inside the reinstatement window, and a single notice fails the two-notice rule.
Cancellation is therefore barred until the window closes with both notices given. The company must accept reinstatement or reimburse.

**Q: A pre-need company offers to buy out plans at 120 percent of termination value without Commission approval, and some planholders object. Decide whether the offer may proceed.**

A: The offer cannot proceed as to the objecting planholders without Commission approval.

**Q: A matured education plan pays 60 days late without any fraud. State what the beneficiary collects.**

A: The beneficiary collects twice-legal-interest for the delay period plus Sec. 28 damages on suit.
The controlling rules are Sec. 26, which prices scheduled-plan delay at twice legal interest from day 16 where the planholder complied with documents. Sec. 28 imposes actual damages, attorney's fees, and legal interest from claim date on unreasonable withholding, with a missed Sec. 26 clock as prima facie proof of unreasonable delay.
Applied here, the 60-day delay yields 45 days of delay interest, and the missed clock supplies the prima facie case for litigation damages if unreasonableness is found.
The beneficiary therefore recovers twice-legal-interest for the delay period plus Sec. 28 damages on a successful suit.

### Case: *SEC v. Laigo (Legacy Consolidated Plans)*, G.R. No. 188689, Sept. 2, 2015 (book-cited)

Facts (as discussed in Aquino 2024): Legacy bound itself in trust recitals to manage collections for the benefit and account of planholders through LBP as trustee. On distress the question was whether the company or the planholders owned the fund.

Issue: The issue was whether the trust fund belonged to the company or to the planholders on the company's distress.

Held (as discussed): the manifest intention makes planholders the beneficiaries. Beneficial ownership lies in planholders, legal ownership lies in the trustee, and none is left in the trustor. Sec. 16's spirit (now Sec. 30's letter) makes planholders exclusive beneficiaries, a design RA 9829 cemented after the industry chaos.

Doctrine: trust-fund assets answer only to planholders, never to the company or its general creditors.

> Exam trap: Laigo predates full RA 9829 litigation but states the Sec. 30 rule — cite Sec. 30 as the controlling text, Laigo as the ownership illustration.

Distinction table: suspend vs conserve vs receive vs liquidate.

| Point | Suspend/revoke (Sec. 48) | Conserve (Sec. 49) | Receive (Sec. 50) | Liquidate (Secs. 50-52) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Four opinion triggers on examination or evidence | Continuing inability or unwillingness to comply | Insolvency or hazardous continuance | Confirmed insolvency with public-interest need |
| Who acts | Commission after notice and hearing | Commission-appointed conservator (may be another company) | Commission-designated receiver after forthwith CDO | Commission-approved liquidator plus court proceedings |
| Fund | Untouched by the order | Preserved under conservator | Taken charge of for planholders and creditors | Distributed exclusively to planholders pro rata; Commission may take custody |

Historic Bar question: gap for §7. Use the drill below.

**Q: General creditors of an insolvent pre-need company seek to attach the trust fund. Decide whether attachment lies.**

A: The attachment fails, and the creditors are confined to non-fund assets. The controlling rules are Sec. 30, which bars diversion of the fund and creditor claims against it notwithstanding any contrary law. Sec. 52 excludes the fund from court insolvency jurisdiction and denies creditors personality in Commission fund proceedings. Laigo vests beneficial ownership of the fund in the planholders. Applied here, the attachment targets fund assets, the precise object of the statutory shield. Attachment therefore fails, and creditors may proceed against non-fund assets only.

## Exam Traps

1. Sec. 4(b) needs a future benefit at need or maturity for plan payments — stockholder perks paid for shares fail both halves.
2. HMO contracts run on fixed prepaid fees through provider networks (EO 192) — different animal, now also IC-supervised but not pre-need.
3. Doubts favor the planholder (Sec. 3) but clear terms are enforced literally (Gaw) — liberality is not rewriting.
4. The Commission's power is primary and exclusive (Sec. 5). Pending SEC cases merely continue to conclusion (Sec. 57).
5. Assignees and transferees are planholders (Sec. 4(d)) — Gaw still had to meet the assignment conditions (accelerated installments, written, approved, 25-km).
6. A counselor must be a licensed natural person with training plus exam. Lending a license to an unlicensed seller kills the license (Sec. 21(g)).
7. Affiliate control is over 25% or officer/director status — lower than the 40% insurance-holding presumption in Part I-E.
8. Lapsed (past grace) is curable. Cancelled (past 2 years unreinstated) is terminal, so classify before advising.
9. Scheduled vs contingent decides the Sec. 26 clock: immediate/15-day vs 30-days-on-documents.
10. Registration runs per plan within 45 days of licensing — company licensing alone never authorizes a sale.
11. Ads need prior approval, but 10 working days of silence means approval — calendar working days, not calendar days.
12. Both Sec. 23 notices are required. One notice or none bars cancelled treatment entirely.
13. Termination by the planholder is a right (written notice anytime). Termination by the company is a proposal needing consent plus three conditions.
14. Twice legal interest (not twice the Monetary Board ceiling of insurance Secs. 248-250) prices scheduled-plan delay — keep the regimes apart.
15. The five Sec. 25 acts each need without-just-cause, but unlike insurance Sec. 247 no business-practice frequency is stated — single acts suffice.
16. Fund deposits are not company income and cannot fund dividends or creditors (Sec. 31) — dividend lock adds the fund as item (c) (Sec. 29).
17. Full-payment floors are 45% life but 51% education-pension — the higher number belongs to the scheduled plans.
18. Remit within 20 days of each reference month. Cure noticed deficiencies in 30 days. They are two different clocks.
19. FRIA does not cover pre-need debtors (RA 10142 Sec. 5) — the Code's own conservatorship-to-liquidation path governs.
20. Sec. 55 finality stops at P100,000.00. Above it, Sec. 56 sends any aggrieved person to the CA by petition for review.
