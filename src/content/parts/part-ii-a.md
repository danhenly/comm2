## 1. Definition; Parties; Elements; Perfection

> "Common carriers are persons, corporations, firms or associations engaged in the business of carrying or transporting passengers or goods or both, by land, water, or air, for compensation, offering their services to the public."
> — Art. 1732, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Person | Any natural or juridical person, firm, or association, including brokers and forwarders that undertake carriage |
| Business of carrying | Transport offered as a business, whether principal or ancillary to another enterprise such as a resort or school |
| For compensation | Fare, freight, or imputed fare inside a package price, since free separate charge is unnecessary |
| To the public | Holding out to whoever offers, though actual patronage may be narrow, occasional, or course-based |

Parties to goods carriage are the shipper who delivers, the carrier who undertakes transport, the consignee named in the bill, and the sub-contractor who actually hauls under the carrier. Parties to passenger carriage are the carrier and the passenger, and a paying passenger, a guest with imputed fare, and a gratuitous rider in a common carrier all ride inside the contract while a stowaway rides outside it.

Perfection follows consent. Air carriage is perfected when the airline accepts the fare and confirms the booking even before any ticket is issued. Bus and jeepney carriage follows the continuing-offer rule where the waiting vehicle offers and the boarding passenger accepts by entry with intent to ride. Train carriage begins when the passenger pays the fare and enters the premises, and from that moment the carrier owes utmost diligence over stations and platforms.

### Case: Torres-Madrid Brokerage, Inc. v. FEB Mitsui Marine Insurance Co., Inc., G.R. No. 194121, July 11, 2016

Facts: Sony hired a brokerage to clear and deliver imported goods. The brokerage owned no trucks and sub-contracted the hauling, and one truck with its cargo was lost to theft.

Issue: The sole question was whether a broker that sub-contracts delivery without its own trucks is a common carrier of the goods.

Held: The Court held that it was. Ancillary carriage for pay to the public is common carriage, and ownership of trucks is unnecessary.

Doctrine: The sub-contract does not relieve the contracting carrier, whose remedy is reimbursement from the sub-contractor while responsibility lasts until delivery under Art. 1736.

### Case: Compañia Maritima v. Insurance Company of North America, G.R. No. L-18965, October 30, 1964

Facts: The shipper orally booked space with written confirmation, and the carrier received hemp in good order aboard its lighter for transshipment to the ocean vessel. The lighter sank before any bill of lading was issued.

Issue: The question was whether a carriage contract was perfected though the goods reached only the carrier lighter and no bill of lading existed.

Held: The Court held that it was perfected. Liability attached from delivery to the carrier or its agent, and lighterage was part of the contracted carriage.

Doctrine: The bill of lading is dispensable under Art. 350 of the Code of Commerce since consent and delivery perfect the contract.

### Case: British Airways v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 92288, February 9, 1993

Facts: A recruiter prepaid fares through a prepaid ticket advice with confirmed bookings for over one hundred workers. The airline accepted the payment instrument and fare, confirmed the seats, then failed to carry the workers as booked.

Issue: The question was whether passenger carriage was perfected though no ticket was ever issued to the workers.

Held: The Court held that it was. Carriage is consensual, and acceptance of fare with confirmed bookings supplied consent, object, and cause.

Doctrine: The unjustified failure to carry after perfection is breach in bad faith and not a mere documentary lapse.

### Case: LRTA v. Navidad, G.R. No. 145804, February 6, 2003

Facts: A passenger paid her token, entered the LRT platform, fell onto the tracks, and was struck by an incoming train.

Issue: The question was when train carriage begins and what presumption follows a passenger death.

Held: The Court held that carriage began at fare payment and entry. The carrier owed the utmost diligence of Art. 1755 over its premises, and death raised the Art. 1756 presumption of fault.

Doctrine: Hiring a security firm does not delegate away the non-delegable safety duty of the carrier.

**2017 Bar Q: Wisconsin Transportation Co., Inc. (WTC) owned and operated an inter-island de luxe bus service plying the Manila-Batangas-Mindoro route. Three friends, namely: Aurelio, Jerome and Florencio rode on the same WTC bus from Manila bound for Mindoro. Aurelio purchased a ticket for himself. Jerome, being a boyhood friend of the bus driver, was allowed a free ride by agreeing to sit during the trip on a stool placed in the aisle. Florencio, already penniless after spending all of his money on beer the night before, just stole a ride in the bus by hiding in the on-board toilet of the bus. During the trip, the bus collided with another bus coming from the opposite direction. The three friends all suffered serious physical injuries. What are WTC's liabilities, if any, in favor of Aurelio, Jerome and Florencio? Explain your answer. (4%)**

A: WTC answers to Aurelio and Jerome as its passengers and owes nothing to Florencio as a stowaway outside the contract.
Aurelio holds a ticket and Jerome rides by the driver permission inside the vehicle, so both entered carriage by consent while Florencio boarded by stealth without the carrier acceptance that perfection requires.
WTC must therefore carry Aurelio and Jerome with the utmost diligence of very cautious persons, and their injuries raise the presumption of fault that yields only to proof of extraordinary diligence.
Florencio cannot invoke the contract since no meeting of minds ever bound him to the carrier, so he recovers only if he proves the ordinary negligence that the law demands of every person.

## 2. Common Carriers: Tests, Characteristics, Ancillary Business, Exclusions

> "Common carriers, from the nature of their business and for reasons of public policy, are bound to observe extraordinary diligence in the vigilance over the goods and for the safety of the passengers transported by them, according to all the circumstances of each case."
> — Art. 1733, par. 1, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Test or Trait | Content |
|---|---|
| Public holding-out | The decisive test is whether carriage for pay is offered to the public as a business and not by special private contract |
| Regularity immaterial | Occasional, seasonal, or single-trip carriage for the public still qualifies |
| Purpose immaterial | Ancillary carriage inside a resort, school, or construction business still qualifies where a fare is charged or imputed |
| Means immaterial | Trucks, vessels, aircraft, pipelines, and even no owned vehicle at all still qualify where the undertaking is carriage |
| Clientele immaterial | Limited, enrolled, or course-based patronage still qualifies since the test is the nature held out and not the headcount |
| Brokers and forwarders | Arrangers answer for their own negligence only, but those that undertake delivery or issue bills of lading answer as carriers |
| TNVS and TNC | App-based vehicles offered to the riding public perform common carriage and need a franchise |
| Excluded by law | Carriage by special contract under customer control, private use by permission, and carriage the law reserves to private hands stay outside |

| Claimed Carrier | Common | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Resort ferry for package guests | Yes | Imputed fare inside the package and public offering through tours (G.R. No. 186312, 2010) |
| School van for enrolled pupils for a fee | Yes | Nature held out controls over number served (G.R. No. 157917, 2012) |
| Pipeline for hire | Yes | Land transport for pay needs no vehicle (G.R. No. 125948, 1998) |
| Broker issuing its own bill of lading | Yes | Undertaking delivery binds as carrier (G.R. No. 166250, 2010) |
| Trucker hired per trip under customer control | No | Special contract plus control makes use by permission (G.R. No. L-15122, 1920) |
| Chartered vessel serving select charterers only | No | No public holding-out, so charter rules govern (G.R. No. 112287, 1997) |

### Case: National Steel Corporation v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 112287, December 12, 1997

Facts: Steel was loaded aboard a vessel working under voyage charter for select charterers. The cargo was wetted and rusted in transit.

Issue: The question was whether the vessel was a common or a private carrier and which law governed the claim.

Held: The Court held that it was a private carrier. It never held itself out to the public, so the charter with Code of Commerce rules governed and the shipper carried the burden of proving willful negligence.

Doctrine: The Art. 1732 test turns on public holding-out as a business, and charter service to chosen clients fails that test.

### Case: U.S. v. Tan Piaco, G.R. No. L-15122, March 10, 1920

Facts: The operator ran rented trucks on Leyte routes where each trip rested on a special contract and the customer directed the use of the vehicle.

Issue: The question was whether special-contract carriage made the operator a public utility common carrier.

Held: The Court held that it did not. Public use by public right differs from use by private permission, and customer control marked the engagement as private.

Doctrine: Contract terms plus control decide character, and neither trucks on routes nor trips for pay suffice without a public offer.

### Case: Sps. Cruz v. Sun Holidays, Inc., G.R. No. 186312, June 29, 2010

Facts: A resort ferried package-tour guests to its island without a separate boat charge. The boat sailed into a storm and capsized.

Issue: The question was whether ancillary carriage for a narrow set of guests was common carriage.

Held: The Court held that it was. Art. 1732 draws no line between principal and ancillary business or between general and narrow clientele, and the package price carried the imputed fare.

Doctrine: Sailing into a known storm breached the extraordinary diligence the character imposed.

### Case: Luzon Stevedoring Co., Inc. v. Public Service Commission, G.R. No. L-5458, September 16, 1953

Facts: The company hauled cargo by tug and barge between Manila and Negros for regular commercial clients for pay with its own crew and control.

Issue: The question was whether limited clientele kept the operation outside regulation.

Held: The Court held that it did not. The statute reached general or limited clientele, and crew plus control marked the work as transport rather than lease.

Doctrine: Limited patronage never defeats the public-service character of carriage for pay.

### Case: Sps. Pereña v. Sps. Nicolas, G.R. No. 157917, August 29, 2012

Facts: A school van carrying pupils for a fee was struck by a train at a non-crossing point. The operator claimed private and limited character.

Issue: The question was whether a fee van serving enrolled pupils only was a common carrier.

Held: The Court held that it was. Fee transport over routes offered to whoever enrolls is carriage to the public under Arts. 1732, 1733, and 1755, and the Art. 1756 presumption applied.

Doctrine: The headcount served is irrelevant, since character follows the nature held out.

### Case: FPIC (First Philippine Industrial Corporation) v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 125948, December 29, 1998

Facts: The company ran a pipeline from Batangas to Sucat and Pandacan for hire and claimed it was no carrier since it owned no vehicle.

Issue: The question was whether pipeline transit without a vehicle was common carriage.

Held: The Court held that it was. Art. 1732 demands only land, water, or air carriage for pay to the public, and a pipeline moves petroleum by land.

Doctrine: The petroleum law confirms the character, and the confirmed common carrier kept its local-tax exemption.

### Case: Westwind Shipping Corp. v. UCPB General Insurance Co., Inc., G.R. Nos. 200289 and 200314, November 25, 2013

Facts: A broker withdrew nearly two hundred skids from arrastre in good order and delivered several more skids damaged.

Issue: The question was whether a broker-deliverer without trucks answered as a common carrier.

Held: The Court held that it did. Good-order receipt followed by bad-order delivery raised prima facie negligence under Art. 1735, and ancillary character with no trucks was immaterial.

Doctrine: The broker answers for the cargo and preserves reimbursement against whoever actually caused the damage.

### Case: Unsworth Transport International (Phils.), Inc. v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 166250, July 26, 2010

Facts: A freight forwarder issued its own bill of lading and undertook delivery of the shipment. One drum arrived punctured and five drums short.

Issue: The question was whether a forwarder that merely arranges transport becomes a carrier by issuing a bill of lading.

Held: The Court held that it did. A mere arranger answers for its own negligence only, but issuing a bill and undertaking delivery binds the forwarder as carrier under Arts. 1733 to 1735.

Doctrine: The document plus the undertaking, and not the forwarder label, fixes the character.

### Case: LTFRB v. Valenzuela, G.R. No. 242860, March 11, 2019

Facts: A motorcycle ride-hailing app operated without any franchise, and the trial court enjoined regulators from interfering with it.

Issue: The question was whether app-based TNVS and TNC service was common carriage subject to regulation.

Held: The Court treated the service as carriage in concept. Online offering to the app public is offering to the public under Art. 1732, and private motorcycles hired for pay were already barred by statute.

Doctrine: Concept does not equal authority, since the service still needs the franchise the law demands.

**2019 Bar Q: LMN, Inc. operates a beach resort in a secluded island off the coast of Puerto Princesa City, Palawan. It operates three (3) motorized boats to ferry its guests from the city proper to the island resort and vice-versa. During one rainy morning, the guests were informed that the ferry services for that day were cancelled due to a storm forecast. In order to appease the apparent dismay of most of the guests who will miss their flight back to Manila, the boat captain of one of LMN, Inc.'s motorized boats decided to push through with its trip back to the city. Shortly after the boat sailed, the storm hit and the winds and waves became stronger, causing engine trouble to the boat. Unfortunately, the boat capsized and sank, resulting in the death of one of the passengers, Mr. X. This prompted Mr. X's heirs to file a complaint for damages against LMN, Inc., which they alleged to be a common carrier. In its defense, LMN, Inc. maintained that it is not a common carrier because its boats are not available to the general public but only ferry resort guests and employees. (a) May LMN, Inc. be considered a common carrier? Explain. (3%) (b) Assuming LMN, Inc. is a common carrier, may it be absolved from liability on the ground of fortuitous event? Explain. (2%)**

A: LMN, Inc. is a common carrier and it is not absolved by the storm, since it sailed into a known danger and the law imputes the package fare as compensation.
Its boats carry guests by water for the imputed fare inside the resort package, and Art. 1732 reaches ancillary and narrow-client carriage so long as the service is offered to whoever books the resort.
The storm defense fails because the forecast preceded the sailing and the captain chose to brave it, which keeps the human fault concurrent with the waves and defeats the fortuity claim.
Death therefore raises the presumption of fault, and only proof of extraordinary diligence could rebut it, which a voluntary voyage into a forecasted storm cannot supply.

## 3. Effect of Charter Party

| Charter | Possession and Control | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Voyage charter | Owner keeps vessel, crew, and command for the trip | Common carrier |
| Time charter | Owner keeps vessel, crew, and command for the period | Common carrier |
| Bareboat or demise | Charterer takes vessel, crew, and command as owner pro hac vice | Private carrier |

A time or voyage charter is a contract of affreightment that hires carrying space while the owner stays carrier. A bareboat or demise charter hires the vessel itself, and the charterer who mans and commands her becomes the carrier for that venture. The owner therefore keeps Art. 1733 responsibility under the first two forms and sheds it under the third.

### Case: Planters Products v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 101503, September 15, 1993

Facts: Bulk urea moved under time charter while the owner kept the vessel, crew, and command. Shortage at outturn was tied to free-in-and-out stowed unloading.

Issue: The question was whether the time charter turned the owner into a private carrier.

Held: The Court held that it did not. Owner retention of possession and command kept the common character, and only a demise transfer would change it.

Doctrine: The presumption of fault applied in form, but the carrier rebutted it here by proving seaworthiness, sealed hatches, and cargo character with agreed unloading terms.

**2017 Bar Q: Onassis Shipping, Inc. (Onassis) operated passenger vessels and cargo trucks, and offered its services to the general public. In line with its vision and mission to protect the environment, Go-Green Asia (Go-Green), an NGO affiliated with Greenpeace, entered into a contract with Onassis whereby Go-Green would operate with its own crew the MN Dolphin, an ocean-going passenger vessel of Onassis. While on its way to Palawan carrying Go-Green's invited guests who were international and local observers desirous of checking certain environmental concerns in the area, the MN Dolphin encountered high waves and strong winds caused by a typhoon in the West Philippine Sea. The rough seas led to serious physical injuries to some of the guests. Discuss the liabilities of Onassis and Go-Green to the passengers of the M/V Dolphin. Explain briefly your answer. (3%)**

A: Go-Green answers as the carrier for the voyage while Onassis keeps owner exposure under its public franchise, since the contract transferred crew and command to Go-Green.
The transfer of operation with its own crew marks a demise in substance, which makes Go-Green the private operator for the invited guests and lifts the voyage out of Onassis common-carrier performance.
Go-Green therefore bears the diligence of the carrier in command, and the typhoon excuses only if it stood alone without concurrent fault in braving known rough seas.
Onassis remains reachable on registered ownership and franchise responsibility even as Go-Green carries the primary voyage liability to the injured guests.

## 4. Common vs Private Carrier; vs Towage, Arrastre, Stevedoring, Travel Agency

| Pair | Carrier Side | Other Side |
|---|---|---|
| Common vs private | Holds out to the public for pay and answers with extraordinary diligence and presumed fault | Serves by special contract under customer control and answers on proved fault under charter and Code of Commerce rules |
| Vs towage | Takes cargo or passengers aboard for transport | Merely pulls or pushes another vessel without receiving the goods |
| Vs arrastre | Carries to destination with transit custody | Receives and holds goods on the pier for loading or turnover |
| Vs stevedoring | Undertakes the voyage with cargo in charge | Loads, stows, or discharges as hired labor without the voyage undertaking |
| Vs travel agency | Undertakes carriage itself over its routes | Arranges bookings, tickets, and hotels without operating the carriage |

The dividing line is custody plus transport under a public undertaking. Towage moves without receiving, arrastre receives without voyaging, stevedoring handles without undertaking, and a travel agency arranges without operating, so each answers on its own fault and never with the carrier presumption.

**2025 Bar Q: To celebrate her 70th birthday, A hired F Travel & Tours to arrange and facilitate her booking, ticketing, and accommodation for a two-week foreign tour, which included visits to Italy and Greece, for the total amount of PHP 145,000.00. On Wednesday, August 14, 2024, B, an employee of F Travel & Tours, went to A's house to deliver the travel documents and plane ticket. She told A to be at the Ninoy Aquino International Airport (NAIA) on Saturday, August 17, 2024 two hours before her flight schedule. Without checking her travel documents, A went to NAIA on Saturday, August 17, 2024. When she got there, she was informed that the flight she was supposed to take had already left the previous day. A demanded that F Travel & Tours refund her payment. She claimed that F Travel & Tours did not observe the standard of care required of a common carrier when its employee, B, informed her of the wrong flight schedule. Is F Travel & Tours liable as a common carrier? Reason(s).**

A: F Travel & Tours is not liable as a common carrier, though it may answer for its own fault as an agent.
It never undertook carriage over any route and never held itself out as transporting the public, since it only arranged bookings, tickets, and hotels for the celebrant.
The extraordinary diligence and presumed fault of Arts. 1733 and 1735 to 1756 therefore never attached, because those burdens follow the carrier undertaking and not the arranging work.
Its employee gave the wrong flight date, so liability if any rests on agency fault proved by the traveler and not on the carrier presumption she invoked.

## 5. Tramp vs Line Service; Governing Laws; Nature of Business

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Tramp service | Vessels take whatever cargo offers without fixed schedule or route and commonly work under charter |
| Line service | Vessels keep fixed schedules and routes for whoever books and plainly hold out to the public |
| Civil Code | Governs common carriage first through Arts. 1732 to 1766 on diligence, defenses, and remedies |
| Code of Commerce | Governs suppletorily on vessel, charter, average, and collision matters such as Arts. 361, 362, and 587 |
| Public interest | Carriage for the public is impressed with public interest, which justifies regulation, franchises, and the extraordinary standard |

Scheduling does not decide character by itself. A liner serving the public is ordinarily common, while a tramp serving only its charterers is ordinarily private, yet each case still turns on the Art. 1732 holding-out test rather than on the timetable alone.

**Q: A liner keeps a fixed Manila-Cebu schedule for whoever books, while a tramp takes whatever bulk cargo offers under voyage charters to chosen charterers. A shipper asks which vessel answers as a common carrier if its cargo is lost. How do you advise?**

A: The liner answers as the common carrier while the tramp answers as the private carrier on these facts.
The liner holds scheduled carriage out to the public for pay, which meets Art. 1732 without need of further proof of regularity or purpose.
The tramp serves chosen charterers by special contract without a public offer, which places it under charter and Code of Commerce rules with fault to be proved.
The shipper therefore sues the liner under the presumption of fault and sues the tramp on proved willful negligence, since schedules only evidence and never replace the holding-out test.

## 6. Registered Owner Rule and Kabit System; Boundary System

The registered owner of a vehicle answers to third persons for its operation under his franchise, even where the real owner is another or the transfer was never registered. Registration is the public notice of ownership and operation, so victims need not look beyond the registry for the party answerable in damages.

The kabit system binds a vehicle to another franchise without transfer or approval. Between the parties the tie-up is void for illegality, yet the registered franchise holder still answers to injured third persons since the pari delicto bar runs only between the wrongdoers and never against the victim. Aircraft and vessels follow the same policy through their own registries and operating authorities.

The boundary system is the arrangement where the driver remits a fixed daily amount to the operator and keeps the excess. It does not make the driver an independent contractor, since the operator keeps control through routes, schedules, and discipline, and the operator therefore answers as employer for the crew torts.

| Device | Effect Between Parties | Effect on Third Persons |
|---|---|---|
| Registered ownership | Title and franchise evidence | Owner on record answers for operation |
| Kabit tie-up | Void and unenforceable between operators | Franchise holder still answers the victim |
| Pari delicto | Bars recovery between the wrongdoers | Never bars the injured passenger or stranger |
| Boundary remittance | Internal division of earnings | Operator keeps employer liability |

**Q: A bus is sold but the sale is never registered, and the buyer ties the unit to the seller franchise under a kabit arrangement with the driver on boundary. A passenger is hurt when the bus collides with a truck. From whom may the passenger recover?**

A: The passenger may recover from the registered franchise holder, who cannot hide behind the unregistered sale or the illegal tie-up.
Registration tells the public who operates the vehicle, so the seller on record answers although beneficial ownership already passed to the buyer.
The kabit pact is void between the operators, yet that voidness never travels to the victim since pari delicto bars only the wrongdoers against each other.
The boundary setup keeps the operator as employer, so the driver fault in the collision is the operator fault that the presumption of carrier negligence already provisionally establishes.

## 7. Formalities: Bill of Lading

> "The shipper as well as the carrier of the merchandise or goods may mutually demand that a bill of lading be issued."
> — Art. 350, Code of Commerce

| Facet | Content |
|---|---|
| Definition | A written acknowledgment that the carrier received stated goods for transport to a stated destination |
| Kinds | Clean or foul on exceptions, on-board or received for shipment on loading state, straight or order or bearer on negotiability, and through or multimodal on connecting carriers |
| Nature | Receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract terms, and document of title that transfers constructive possession |
| Effectivity | Takes effect on issuance and delivery of the goods description, though the underlying contract already lives by consent |
| As contract | Embodies the voyage terms that bind shipper and carrier, read with the Code where stipulations are silent |
| Parties | Shipper who delivers, carrier who issues, consignee named as recipient, and endorsee who takes by negotiation |
| Adhesion | Pre-printed carrier terms are read strictly against the carrier with obscurities resolved against the drafter |
| Parol evidence | The written bill controls over prior oral terms, except where fraud, mistake, or a valid side agreement is proved |
| Evidence | Clean receipt in good order followed by short or bad-order delivery raises prima facie carrier fault |
| Actionable document | The lawful holder may sue on the bill as the title representative of the goods |
| Shipment terms | Freight, free-in-and-out stowed, and loading and discharge duties allocate handling between ship and shore |
| Stipulations | Parties may set handling, notice, and forum terms within the Code limits on carrier responsibility |
| Electronic documents | Electronic bills carry the same functions where the law recognizes the data message as writing |
| Prohibited stipulations | Total exemption from fault, reduction of diligence below extraordinary, and unconscionable caps fall as void |
| As receipt | Admits quantity and apparent condition at loading and estops denial against good-faith holders |
| As document of title | Endorsement and delivery pass constructive possession so the holder may claim the goods at destination |

**Q: A carrier issues a clean bill of lading for bagged fertilizer in apparent good order, and the consignee receives short and hardened bags at discharge. The carrier points to a printed clause exempting it from all liability for any loss. Is the bill conclusive and is the clause valid?**

A: The clean bill proves prima facie fault for the shortage and the blanket exemption is void.
The bill admits receipt in apparent good order, so short and deteriorated delivery raises the presumption that the carrier failed in extraordinary diligence.
The printed total-exemption clause asks less than the diligence the law commands, and stipulations that erase carrier responsibility fall as contrary to public policy.
The carrier therefore answers for the shortage unless it proves an excepted cause with extraordinary care, since the bill fixes the prima facie case and the void clause contributes nothing to the defense.

## Exam Traps

1. A broker with no trucks is still a carrier where it undertakes delivery, since means are immaterial under Art. 1732.
2. A forwarder that only arranges is not a carrier, but issuing its own bill of lading converts it into one.
3. Package-tour transport carries imputed fare, so free-separate-ride language never defeats the compensation element.
4. Narrow, enrolled, or occasional patronage never defeats public offering, since character follows the nature held out.
5. A pipeline with no vehicle still carries by land for pay, so the no-vehicle defense fails.
6. App-based TNVS service is carriage in concept yet illegal in operation without a franchise.
7. Time and voyage charters keep the owner common, while only bareboat or demise transfers make the operator private.
8. Special-contract hauling under customer control is private even on regular routes with trucks for pay.
9. Towage pulls without receiving, so the tower is no carrier of the towed cargo.
10. Arrastre receives without voyaging, so custody ends at turnover and the carrier presumption never attaches.
11. A travel agency arranges without operating, so the carrier standard never follows the agency fee.
12. A tramp schedule alone proves nothing, since holding-out decides and timetables only evidence it.
13. The registered owner answers although he already sold the unit, since registration is public notice.
14. The kabit victim recovers from the franchise holder although the tie-up is void between the operators.
15. The boundary driver stays an employee for third-party purposes despite keeping the daily excess.
16. The bill of lading is dispensable for perfection, though it remains the best evidence of receipt and terms.
17. A clean bill admits apparent good order and estops denial against good-faith holders.
18. A total exemption from fault is void although printed in an adhesion bill accepted by the shipper.
