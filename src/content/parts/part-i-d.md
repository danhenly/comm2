## 1. Reinsurance

> A contract of reinsurance is one by which an insurer procures a third person to insure him against loss or liability by reason of such original insurance.
>
> — Sec. 97, Insurance Code (RA 10607) (old Sec. 95)

> Where an insurer obtains reinsurance, except under automatic reinsurance treaties, he must communicate all the representations of the original insured, and also all the knowledge and information he possesses, whether previously or subsequently acquired, which are material to the risk.
>
> — Sec. 98, Insurance Code (RA 10607) (old Sec. 96)

> A reinsurance is presumed to be a contract of indemnity against liability, and not merely against damage.
>
> — Sec. 99, Insurance Code (RA 10607) (old Sec. 97)

> The original insured has no interest in a contract of reinsurance.
>
> — Sec. 100, Insurance Code (RA 10607) (old Sec. 98)

| Element/Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Parties | Ceding company (original insurer, reinsured) cedes; reinsurer (assuming insurer) assumes a portion of the risk for a proportionate premium share |
| Subject | The ceding insurer's own liability under the original policy, not the original insured's property |
| Good faith disclosure (Sec. 98) | Ceding insurer must communicate the original insured's representations plus all material knowledge, before or after acquisition; automatic treaties excepted |
| Presumption (Sec. 99) | Indemnity against liability, not merely against damage: reinsurer pays when the ceding insurer's liability attaches |
| Stranger rule (Sec. 100) | Original insured has no interest in, and no action on, the reinsurance contract |
| Functions | Spread and cap exposure; stabilize surplus; allow underwriting beyond retention; regulatory: Sec. 221 (non-life single-risk retention capped at 20 percent of net worth, reinsurance ceded deducted); Sec. 224 (cede excess to authorized local insurers first) |
| Kinds | Facultative (per-risk offer and acceptance) vs treaty: obligatory (automatic cession of a defined book) and facultative-obligatory (cedent may cede, reinsurer must accept) |
| Regulatory controls | Sec. 222 (accept only risks otherwise authorized to insure); Sec. 223 (no cession abroad to unauthorized foreign insurer except through a registered resident agent); Sec. 225 (Commissioner-set terms; report any cancellation or termination in writing within 30 days); Sec. 226 (report treaty particulars within 3 months of effectivity); Sec. 227 (no credit as admitted asset unless reinsurance payable on ceding insurer's liability without diminution for its insolvency, to the ceding insurer or its liquidator); Sec. 228 (no life company may reinsure its whole risk on any life or substantially all business without the Commissioner's written permission) |

### Case: *Fieldmen's Insurance Co. v. Asian Surety & Insurance Co.*, G.R. No. L-23447, July 31, 1970

Facts: Asian Surety (ceding company) and Fieldmen's (reinsurer) entered seven reinsurance treaties (Apr. 11, 1960-Jan. 9, 1961), each terminable on at least three months' registered-mail notice effective Dec. 31 of the notice year. Fieldmen's gave notice Sept. 19, 1961 for Dec. 31, 1961. A GSIS risk reinsured under Cession No. 61-87 (Facultative-Obligatory Treaty-Fire, policy July 1, 1961-July 1, 1962) burned Feb. 16, 1962. Fieldmen's sought declaratory relief that all treaties and cessions ended Dec. 31, 1961, alternatively rescission for alleged violations.

Issue: The issues were whether treaty cancellation ipso facto terminated liability under cessions made before cancellation and whether rescission lay in declaratory relief.

Held: No to both. Article 10 of the Facultative-Obligatory Treaty-Fire (and Art. VI(4) of the Personal Accident Treaty) kept current cessions in full force until natural expiry, so Fieldmen's answered for the Feb. 1962 fire. Rescission could not be granted. No grounds were pleaded and no substantial breach was found. It was also the wrong remedy.

Doctrine: Cessions are governed by the treaty's express stipulations. Cancellation ends future cessions, not current ones where survival is stipulated. Rescission needs a proper action and a substantial breach.

> Exam trap: A Dec. 31 treaty cut-off kills future cessions, not a still-running cession with a survival clause; check the treaty's continuing-liability article before releasing the reinsurer.

### Case: *Malayan Insurance v. Philippines First Insurance (Wyeth/Reputable)*, G.R. No. 184300, July 11, 2012 (cross-listed from Part I-C §6)

Facts (per Batch-1 digest): Two policies covered the same goods exposed to the same peril. They were taken for two different persons with distinct interests, namely the owner and the carrier.

Issue: The issue was whether the two policies constituted double insurance so that the other-insurance and ratable-contribution clauses applied.

Held: They did not constitute double insurance. Each insurer answered on its own contract.

Doctrine: Policies over the same subject for different persons with distinct interests are not double insurance. The other-insurance and ratable-contribution clauses do not apply to them. By the same logic, the original insured remains a stranger to a reinsurance contract under Sec. 100.

Held relevance here: the reinsurance boundary is that the carrier's own SR-policy insurer (Malayan) answers the carrier on that contract, while the cargo owner's insurer answers the owner. Distinct persons and interests defeat any pooling of the covers. That is the same logic that keeps the original insured a stranger to a reinsurance contract under Sec. 100.

**2025 Bar Q: X obtained an insurance policy for her Porsche Carrera GT from XYZ Insurance Company. The same car was insured by her creditor-mortgagee, Y, with ABC Insurance Company. When the car was damaged because of an accident, X filed a claim with XYZ Insurance Company. The latter denied the claim since it discovered that the car was also insured with ABC Insurance Company. According to XYZ Insurance Company, double insurance is prohibited by law. Decide with reason(s).**

A: Denial of the claim is improper because X and Y are different persons with distinct interests — owner and creditor-mortgagee — so the two policies are not double insurance, which exists only where the same person is insured by several insurers separately in respect to the same subject and interest.
The controlling rule is Sec. 95, which defines double insurance by the same-person, same-subject, same-interest identities, as applied in Malayan v Phil First: the same goods exposed to the same peril but insured for different persons with distinct interests is not double insurance, and double cover is regulated by deduction and contribution under Sec. 96, not prohibited.
Applied here, XYZ answers X's ownership interest while ABC answers Y's mortgage-credit interest, so each insurer pays on its own contract without deduction from the other.
XYZ is therefore liable on X's claim, and neither an other-insurance clause nor Sec. 96 contribution defeats recovery.

## 2. Fire Insurance

> As used in this Code, the term fire insurance shall include insurance against loss by fire, lightning, windstorm, tornado or earthquake and other allied risks, when such risks are covered by extension to fire insurance policies or under separate policies.
>
> — Sec. 169, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> An alteration in the use or condition of a thing insured from that to which it is limited by the policy made without the consent of the insurer, by means within the control of the insured, and increasing the risks, entitles an insurer to rescind a contract of fire insurance.
>
> — Sec. 170, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> An alteration in the use or condition of a thing insured from that to which it is limited by the policy, which does not increase the risk, does not affect a contract of fire insurance.
>
> — Sec. 171, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> A contract of fire insurance is not affected by any act of the insured subsequent to the execution of the policy, which does not violate its provisions, even though it increases the risk and is the cause of the loss.
>
> — Sec. 172, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> If there is no valuation in the policy, the measure of indemnity in an insurance against fire is the expense it would be to the insured at the time of the commencement of the fire to replace the thing lost or injured in the condition in which it was at the time of the injury; but if there is a valuation in a policy of fire insurance, the effect shall be the same as in a policy of marine insurance.
>
> — Sec. 173, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> No policy of fire insurance shall be pledged, hypothecated, or transferred to any person, firm or company who acts as agent for or otherwise represents the issuing company, and any such pledge, hypothecation, or transfer hereafter made shall be void and of no effect insofar as it may affect other creditors of the insured.
>
> — Sec. 175, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

| Element/Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Concept (Sec. 169) | Cover for loss by fire, lightning, windstorm, tornado, earthquake and allied risks by extension or separate policy; indemnity contract |
| Property covered | Buildings, merchandise, household effects; loss payable to mortgagee to the extent of its interest does not displace the insured as proper plaintiff (La'O) |
| Alteration rescinding (Sec. 170) | Without insurer consent, by means within insured's control, and increasing the risk: insurer entitled to rescind |
| Alteration harmless (Sec. 171) | Same alteration not increasing the risk: contract unaffected |
| Subsequent act (Sec. 172) | Post-execution act not violating policy provisions: contract unaffected even if it increased the risk and caused the loss |
| Measure, unvalued (Sec. 173) | Replacement expense at commencement of fire in condition at time of injury; cost price is competent evidence of value (La'O) |
| Measure, valued/appraised (Sec. 174) | Appraised value fixed by independent appraiser binds; total loss pays the stated amount, partial loss pays in full, pro rata among co-insurers, never above the stated amount |
| Co-insurance (Galian clause 17) | Property of greater value than cover at outbreak: insured is his own insurer for the difference, bears a ratable proportion |

### Case: *Multi-ware Manufacturing v. Cibeles Insurance*, G.R. No. 230528, Feb. 1, 2021

Facts: Multi-ware took fire policies from Western Guaranty (P10M) and Cibeles (P7M) on machinery and equipment, then Prudential Guarantee policies on the same Building 1 machinery. After the Apr. 21, 2000 fire it claimed on all. Cibeles and Western denied for violation of Policy Condition No. 3 (other insurance), No. 15 (fraud), and No. 21 (arson). Non-disclosure of the Prudential policies was established.

Issue: Whether Condition No. 3 covers machinery and equipment and whether non-disclosure forfeits benefits.

Held: Yes to both. "Property" is generic and includes machinery and equipment. The undisclosed policies were on the same matter with the same interest and risk, so all benefits were forfeited.

Doctrine: The other-insurance clause prevents over-insurance and fraud inducement. Identity of subject matter and interest triggers forfeiture.

### Case: *Emilio Gonzalez La O v. Yek Tong Lin Fire & Marine Insurance*, G.R. No. 33131, Dec. 13, 1930

Facts: La O stored leaf tobacco insured against fire with four companies (P290,000 total, P100,000 with defendant) in a warehouse the defendant itself owned and partly leased to others. After the Jan. 11, 1928 fire, defendant invoked warranties A and G and the undeclared-other-insurance condition (Art. 3).

Issue: The issues were whether the insured may sue though the policy is payable to the mortgagee bank and whether the other-insurance condition voids the policies.

Held: The insured may sue as the contracting party, especially where the mortgagee's interest is less than full recovery. The policies were not void. The typewritten clause plus the attached "other insurances" sheet gave the defendant notice. The other policies covered different interests in a stock far exceeding total cover. The defendant, knowing of them, collected increased premiums instead of rescinding. That was waiver.

Doctrine: Other-insurance forfeiture needs the same subject matter and interest. The insurer's knowledge plus continued collection of premium waives annulment.

### Case: *Qua Chee Gan v. Law Union and Rock Insurance*, G.R. No. L-4611, Dec. 17, 1955

Facts: Warehouses and copra/hemp stocks (P370,000, eight policies) burned July 1940. Insurer pleaded breach of the hydrant/hose/pressure/brigade memo-of-warranty, the hemp warranty (gasoline stored), refusal of books, and fraudulent overclaim, plus arson (acquitted July 9, 1941).

Issue: Whether breach of warranties and conditions voids the policies.

Held: No. The insurer issued the policies knowing only two own plus two municipal hydrants existed against an eleven-hydrant warranty and collected premiums. It was therefore estopped from asserting the breach. Gasoline storage was incidental to the business under an ambiguous warranty. The books were substantially produced. The overclaim was an innocent misstatement, not wilful fraud.

Doctrine: Knowledge at issuance plus premium collection waives inconsistent conditions. Ambiguities construe against the insurer. False swearing voids only if wilful with intent to defraud.

### Case: *Francisco Galian v. State Assurance*, G.R. No. L-8405, Feb. 10, 1915

Facts: The policy was an open fire policy for P3,000 on household effects. The post-fire statement listed P4,512. The insurer pleaded fraudulent overclaim. The trial court fixed value at P1,500 on the insurer's compromise offer.

Issue: The issues were the competence of the insured's value testimony, the proof of fraud, and the effect of the coinsurance clause.

Held: The insured and his brother were competent on ordinary household values. Preponderance sustained the list, and fraud was not proved. Clause 17 was valid. The property (P4,512) exceeded the cover (P3,000), so the insurer paid only 3,000/4,512 of the actual loss (P4,391.60 after P120.40 salvage) = P2,919.92.

Doctrine: The ratable-proportion (coinsurance) clause is enforceable. Partial loss is computed on actual loss less salvage, scaled by cover over value.

**2024 Bar Q: Flavio obtained from Safe Assurance Corporation (SAC), through its agent Laureen, a fire insurance policy over his residential house for a period of one year from August 25, 2024 to August 24, 2025. Flavio delivered on August 26, 2024 a check representing premium payment to Laureen who then issued an acknowledgment receipt. On August 27, 2024, a fire completely razed Flavio's house. Applied here, Flavio paid through Laureen before the fire razed the house, so SAC's August 28 bank-credit date is a mere internal matter that does not move the payment date, and the denial on the no-premium ground fails.

## 3. Life Insurance

> Life insurance is insurance on human lives and insurance appertaining thereto or connected therewith.
>
> — Sec. 181, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> The insurer in a life insurance contract shall be liable in case of suicide only when it is committed after the policy has been in force for a period of two (2) years from the date of its issue or of its last reinstatement, unless the policy provides a shorter period: Provided, however, That suicide committed in the state of insanity shall be compensable regardless of the date of commission.

>

### Case: *Manila Bankers Life Insurance v. Aban*, G.R. No. 175666, 715 Phil. 404, July 29, 2013

Facts: Delia Sotero took a P100,000 life policy naming her niece Aban beneficiary. It was in force over two years and seven months at death (Apr. 10, 1996). The insurer denied on post-claim findings (illiteracy, sickness since 1990, no financial capacity, forged application) and sued for rescission/annulment.

Issue: Whether the 2-year incontestability bar (Sec. 48) applies, including against a void-ab-initio theory.

Held: Barred. Sec. 48 gives the insurer two years from effectivity while the insured lives to prove void ab initio or rescindible for fraud or concealment. After that it must pay even if fraud existed. Sotero had insurable interest in her own life and could name anyone. Aban's lack of interest is immaterial.

Doctrine: Incontestability covers void-ab-initio recasts. Insurers must investigate within two years, not after the claim.

### Case: *Biagtan v. Insular Life Assurance*, G.R. No. L-25579, Mar. 29, 1972

Facts: P5,000 policy plus P5,000 Accidental Death Benefit Clause covering death "directly from bodily injury effected solely through external and violent means," excepting injury "intentionally inflicted by another party." Robbers stabbed the insured nine times (five mortal). Insurer paid the base P5,000 but refused the rider.

Issue: Whether nine close-range stab wounds were "intentionally inflicted" within the exception.

Held: Yes. Intent refers to the act of inflicting injury, not the purpose (homicidal or not). Nine wounds at close range cannot be innocent. That distinguished the case from Calanoc's single distant shot. The complaint was dismissed.

Doctrine: Accidental-death riders except intentional third-party injury. Manner and proximity prove intent.

**2019 Bar Q: In January 2016, Mr. H was issued a life insurance policy by XYZ Insurance Co., wherein his wife, Mrs. W, was designated as the sole beneficiary. Unbeknownst to XYZ Insurance Co., however, Mr. H had been previously diagnosed with colon cancer, the fact of which Mr. H had concealed during the entire time his insurance policy was being processed. In January 2019, Mr. H unfortunately committed suicide. Due to her husband's death, Mrs. W, as beneficiary, filed a claim with XYZ Insurance Co. to recover the proceeds of the late Mr. H's life insurance policy. However, XYZ Insurance Co. resisted the claim, contending that: 1. the policy is void ab initio because Mr. H fraudulently concealed or misrepresented his medical condition, i.e., his colon cancer; and 2. as an insurer in a life insurance policy, it cannot be held liable in case of suicide. Rule on each of XYZ Insurance Co.'s contentions. (5%)**

A: Both of XYZ's contentions fail because by January 2019 the policy had been in force for three years, so the Sec. 48 two-year incontestability bar kills the concealment defense and the Sec. 183 two-year suicide clock makes the suicide compensable.
The controlling rules are Sec. 48, under which the insurer is barred after two years from proving void ab initio or rescission for fraud or concealment even on a void-ab-initio recast (Manila Bankers), and Sec. 183, which makes the insurer liable for suicide committed after the policy has been in force two years from issue or last reinstatement.
Applied here, three years in force exceeds both clocks, so the concealed colon cancer can no longer be proved to avoid the policy and the sane suicide after the period is a covered risk.
XYZ must therefore pay Mrs. W the proceeds of the policy.

**2019 Bar Q: On June 21, 2008, Yate took out a life insurance policy on her life in the amount of PhP 10 million and named her husband Vandy and daughter as joint irrevocable beneficiaries. Before the policy was issued and the premiums were paid, Yate underwent a medical checkup with a physician accredited by the insurer, and the only result found was that she was suffering from high blood pressure. Yate was previously diagnosed by a private physician of having breast cancer which she did not disclose to the insurer in her application, nor to the insurer's accredited physician because by then, she was told that she was already cancer-free after undergoing surgery which removed both her breasts. She was later diagnosed with psychotic tendency that graduated into extreme despondency. She was found dead hanging in her closet 36 months after the issuance of the policy. The police authorities declared it to be a case of suicide. The policy did not include suicide as an excepted risk. (a) Can the insurer raise the issue of failure to disclose that she had cancer as a cause for denying the claim of the beneficiaries? (2.5%) (b) Are the beneficiaries entitled to receive the proceeds of the life insurance notwithstanding the fact that the cause of death was suicide? (2.5%)**

A: (a) No, the insurer cannot raise the non-disclosure of the breast cancer because thirty-six months in force exceeds the two-year incontestability period, so that defense is barred. (b) Yes, the beneficiaries are entitled to the proceeds because suicide thirty-six months after issue is compensable under Sec. 183, the policy did not except suicide, and insanity-graded despondency reinforces compensability.
The controlling rules are Sec. 48 as construed in Manila Bankers and the Sec. 183 suicide text quoted above.
Applied here, the insurer's own accredited examination found only hypertension, so the cancer history, even if material, is time-barred, and the 36-month suicide falls outside any exclusion window.
Vandy and the daughter therefore recover the P10M as joint irrevocable beneficiaries.

**2018 Bar Q: Shortly after Yin and Yang were wed, they each took out separate life insurance policies on their lives, and mutually designated one another as sole beneficiary. Both life insurance policies provided for a double indemnity clause, the cost for which was added to the premium rate. During the last 10 years of their marriage, the spouses had faithfully paid for the annual premiums over the life policies from both their salaries. Unfortunately, Yin fell in love with his officemate, Vessel, and they carried on an affair. After two years, their relationship bore them a daughter named Vinsel. Without the knowledge of Yang, Yin changed the designation of the beneficiary to an "irrevocable designation" of Vinsel and Vessel jointly. When Yang learned of the affair, she was so despondent that, having chanced upon Yin and Vessel on a date, she rammed them down with the car she was driving, resulting in Vin's death and Vessel's complete loss of mobilization. Yang was sued for parricide, and while the case was pending, she filed a claim on the proceeds of the life insurance of Yin as irrevocable beneficiary, or at least his legal heir, and opposed the claims on behalf of Vessel and her daughter Vinsel. Yang claimed that her designation as beneficiary in Vin's life insurance policy was irrevocable, in the nature of one "coupled with interest," since it was made in accordance with their mutual agreement to designate one another as sole beneficiary in their respective life policies. She also claimed that the beneficiary designation of Vessel and the illegitimate minor child Vinsel was void being the product of an illicit relationship, and therefore without "insurable interest." (a) Is Yang correct in saying that her designation as beneficiary was irrevocable? (2.5%) (b) Do Vessel and Vinsel have "insurable interest" on the life of Yin? (2.5%)**

A: (a) No, Yang is not correct because a beneficiary designation is revocable unless expressly made irrevocable in the policy, a private mutual agreement does not create irrevocability "coupled with interest," and Yin's later express irrevocable designation of Vinsel and Vessel displaced her. Her killing of Yin additionally disqualifies her as beneficiary. (b) Vessel and Vinsel need no insurable interest on Yin's life because Sec. 184 lets a life policy pass to any person and Sec. 10 gives Yin insurable interest in his own life to support naming whom he pleases, so their illicit origin does not void the designation itself.
The controlling rules are Secs. 184-185, which allow transfer to any person without need of notice, together with the revocability default and the killer-beneficiary disqualification.
Applied here, ten years of faithful premium payment created no vested irrevocability, so the written irrevocable change controls and Vessel and Vinsel take as designated transferees.
Yang's irrevocability claim therefore fails, and Vessel and Vinsel may recover as irrevocable beneficiaries, subject to the parricide consequence against Yang.

**2016 Bar Q: X insured his life for P20 million. X, plays golf and regularly exercises everyday, hence is considered in good health. He did not know, however, that his frequent headache is really caused by his being hypertensive. In his application form for a life insurance for himself, he did not put a check to the question if he is suffering from hypertension, believing that because of his active lifestyle, being hypertensive is a remote possibility. While playing golf one day, X collapsed at the fairway a [question continues in the official release on hypertension concealment and materiality]. [a] Will the beneficiary of X be entitled to the proceeds of the life insurance under the circumstances, despite the non-disclosure that he is hypertensive at the time of application? (2.5%) [b] If X died in an accident instead of a heart attack, would the fact of X's failure to disclose that he is hypertensive be considered as material information? (2.5%)**

A: [a] The beneficiary's right to the proceeds depends on good faith and materiality at application, because unknowing non-disclosure without fraudulent intent does not avoid the policy where the applicant genuinely believed himself healthy, but hypertension that caused the very death is material and avoids the policy if it is proved to have been known or fraudulently concealed. [b] No, the undisclosed hypertension is not a ground to deny the accidental-death claim because immateriality to accidental death means it would not have affected the risk assumed.
The controlling rules are that concealment avoids only when fraudulent and material to the risk, that incontestability under Sec. 48 (Manila Bankers) bars late proof after two years, and that Biagtan distinguishes accidental from non-accidental death measures.
Applied here, X's good-faith omission plus the insurer's own medical-examination opportunity weigh against avoidance, and death by accident severs any causal link to hypertension.
The insurer must therefore pay where the omission was innocent or time-barred, and may deny only on proved fraudulent material concealment within the contestable period.

## 5. Casualty Insurance

> Casualty insurance is insurance covering loss or liability arising from accident or mishap, excluding certain types of loss which by law or custom are considered as falling exclusively within the scope of other types of insurance such as fire or marine. It includes, but is not limited to, employer's liability insurance, motor vehicle liability insurance, plate glass insurance, burglary and theft insurance, personal accident and health insurance as written by non-life insurance companies, and other substantially similar kinds of insurance.

> — Sec. 176, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

| Element/Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Loss or liability arising from accident or mishap |
| Carve-out | Losses falling by law or custom exclusively within fire or marine are excluded |
| Non-exhaustive kinds | Employer's liability; motor vehicle liability; plate glass; burglary and theft; personal accident and health as written by non-life companies; other substantially similar kinds |
| Accident meaning (Sun; Finman) | That which happens by chance or fortuitously, without intention or design, unexpected, unusual, unforeseen; judged from the insured's standpoint |
| Exclusion discipline (Finman) | Liability exclusions are enumerated and exclusive; murder and assault not listed are covered (expressio unius) |
| Motor boundary (Villacorta; Perla; Palermo) | Theft and accident are distinct risks; the Authorized Driver clause governs accident, the theft clause governs unlawful taking |

Distinction table: casualty vs fire vs marine.

| Point | Casualty (Sec. 176) | Fire (Sec. 169) | Marine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peril | Accident or mishap generally | Fire, lightning, windstorm, tornado, earthquake and allied risks | Perils of the sea and navigation |
| Boundary | Yields where law or custom reserves the loss to fire or marine | Yields to marine where the risk is transit by sea | Yields to fire where the risk is fire on land |
| Example kinds | Personal accident, burglary/theft, plate glass, employer liability, motor liability | Building and contents fire cover | Cargo, hull, freightage |

Distinction table: accident vs willful exposure vs negligence (Sun).

| Concept | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Accident | Fortuitous, unintended, unforeseen happening (e.g., gun believed unloaded fires) | Covered |
| Willful exposure to needless peril | Reckless risking bordering on suicidal intent | Excepted where the policy so provides |
| Mere negligence of insured | Contributory carelessness without design | Does not bar recovery absent a policy provision |

### Case: *Sun Insurance Office v. CA (Lim)*, G.R. No. 92383, July 17, 1992

Facts: Felix Lim Jr. held a P200,000 Personal Accident Policy. Two months later he died after pointing at his temple a gun whose magazine he had removed and which he believed to be unloaded. The gun fired. The insurer conceded there was no suicide but denied that any accident occurred.

Issue: Whether the self-inflicted gunshot death was an accident or a willful exposure to needless peril.

Held: The death was accidental and covered. The firing was the additional, unexpected, independent, unforeseen happening. Lim believed the act was safe, so the needless-peril exception failed. Negligence did not bar recovery because no policy provision gave it that effect. Damages and attorney's fees were deleted. The insurer had litigated in good faith a debatable issue of first impression.

Doctrine: Accident carries its ordinary meaning. A deliberate act still yields an accident where an unforeseen happening produces death. The needless-peril exception imports recklessness bordering on suicidal intent.

> Exam trap: Do not treat a mistaken belief of safety as willful exposure; the exception needs conscious reckless risking, and negligence alone never implies it.

### Case: *Finman General Assurance v. CA (Surposa)*, G.R. No. 100970, Sept 2, 1992

Facts: The policy was a Teachers Protection Plan personal accident policy. The insured was stabbed to death without provocation by unidentified men on his way home from a festival. The insurer denied the claim. It said murder and assault were not within coverage.

Issue: Whether death by murder or assault is covered or excluded.

Held: The claim was covered. The death was pure accident on the insured's part. The policy enumerated ten excluding circumstances and murder and assault were not among them, so expressio unius keeps the insurer liable. Ambiguities are construed against the insurer under Art. 1377.

Doctrine: Third-party criminal violence unforeseen by the insured is accident as to him. Enumerated exclusions are exclusive.

> Exam trap: Murder is intentional as to the killer but accidental as to the victim; coverage is judged from the insured's standpoint against the exclusion list.

### Case: *Palermo v. Pyramid Insurance*, G.R. No. L-36480, May 31, 1988

Facts: The policy was Private Car Comprehensive Policy MV-1251 with P20,000 own damage and P10,000 TPL. Palermo's new car was wrecked head-on by a fire engine while he drove with an expired license. The insurer invoked the Authorized Driver clause.

Issue: Whether driving with an expired license removed the insured from the Authorized Driver clause.

Held: No. The insured himself is always an authorized driver. The licensing proviso applies only to the second class of persons driving on his order or with his permission. The Motor Vehicle Law infraction draws penal sanctions. It does not work a forfeiture of cover.

Doctrine: The Authorized Driver clause polices other drivers. It does not police the insured owner. An expired license alone does not void a comprehensive motor claim.

> Exam trap: Check who drove before checking the license; the proviso never disqualifies the insured himself.

**Q: A personal accident policy excludes ten listed causes. The insured dies in a stabbing by strangers, with no provocation. The insurer denies the claim. It cites an unlisted peace-and-order exclusion it says is implied. Decide whether the insurer must pay.**

A: The insurer must pay the face amount because the death is accidental as to the insured under the Section 176 trigger, murder by strangers falls outside the ten listed causes so the exclusive enumeration closes against adding an implied peace-and-order exclusion, and ambiguities construe against the insurer.

## 6. Compulsory Motor Vehicle Liability Insurance (CMVLI)

> It shall be unlawful for any land transportation operator or owner of a motor vehicle to operate the same in the public highways unless there is in force in relation thereto a policy of insurance or guaranty in cash or surety bond issued in accordance with the provisions of this chapter to indemnify the death, bodily injury, and/or damage to property of a third-party or passenger, as the case may be, arising from the use thereof.

> — Sec. 387, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> Any claim for death or injury to any passenger or third-party pursuant to the provisions of this chapter shall be paid without the necessity of proving fault or negligence of any kind: Provided, That for purposes of this section: (a) The total indemnity in respect of any person shall not be less than Fifteen thousand pesos (P15,000.00); (b) The following proofs of loss, when submitted under oath, shall be sufficient evidence to substantiate the claim: (1) Police report of accident; and (2) Death certificate and evidence sufficient to establish the proper payee; or (3) Medical report and evidence of medical or hospital disbursement in respect of which refund is claimed; (c) Claim may be made against one motor vehicle only. In the case of an occupant of a vehicle, claim, shall lie against the insurer of the vehicle in which the occupant is riding, mounting or dismounting from. In any other case, claim shall lie against the insurer of the directly offending vehicle. In all cases, the right of the party paying the claim to recover against the owner of the vehicle responsible for the accident shall be maintained.

> — Sec. 391, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

### Case: *Villacorta v. Insurance Commission (Empire)*, G.R. No. L-54171, Oct 28, 1980

Facts: The policy was a Private Car Policy with P35,000 own damage and P30,000 theft and P30,000 TPL. The car was entrusted to Sunday Machine Works for check-up. It was taken by shop residents and employees. It was driven to Montalban and crashed into a parked truck. The driver and the passenger died and the car was totally smashed. The insurer and the Commission denied the claim. They cited an unauthorized driver and a mere temporary joy ride and no theft.

Issue: Whether the repair-shop diversion violated the Authorized Driver clause, and whether the taking was theft.

Held: Recovery was allowed on both grounds. Entrustment to a shop implies permission to its owner and employees for legitimate checking and road-testing. Diversion to an illicit purpose by a licensed employee does not breach the clause. This is the same as a family driver's joy ride. Independently, unlawful taking without consent partakes of theft under Art. 308 even if temporary because use itself is gain. The theft clause applies and the driver clause does not. The subrogee claim against the shop was preserved.

Doctrine: Implied permission covers shop employees for legitimate purposes. Unauthorized diversion keeps authorized-driver status. Temporary unlawful taking is theft for the theft clause.

> Exam trap: Run the theft clause first where consent to the taking is absent; the driver clause only decides accident-risk cases.

### Case: *Perla Compania de Seguros v. CA (Lim)*, G.R. No. 96452, 208 SCRA 487, May 7, 1992

Facts: The policy was Comprehensive Policy PC/41PP-QCB-43383 on a mortgaged Ford Laser. The car was carnapped while parked. The loss was promptly reported to the police and the LTC and the highway patrol. The claim was filed two days later. The insurer denied the claim. It said driver Evelyn Lim's license had expired in violation of the Authorized Driver clause.

Issue: Whether the Authorized Driver clause applies to a theft-clause claim.

Held: No. Theft as intent to gain and accident as event without the actor's will are distinct risks. The driver clause contemplates accident and not theft. No causal link exists between license validity and carnapping, and reading one in would make car insurance a sham. The mortgage note survives as the principal obligation. Interest and liquidated damages and attorney's fees were excused because of the sham denial. Bad-faith refusal draws moral/exemplary damages and fees.

Doctrine: On a theft taking, only the theft clause decides. License status is irrelevant. The mortgage debt stands apart from the accessory chattel mortgage.

> Exam trap: An expired license never travels from the accident risk to the theft risk; keep the two clauses in separate lanes.

### Case: *Shafer v. RTC Olongapo (Makati Insurance)*, G.R. No. L-78848, Nov 14, 1988

Facts: The policy was private car TPL policy GA No. 0889. Shafer was criminally charged with reckless imprudence for a collision injuring a third party. The trial court dismissed his third-party complaint against the TPL insurer as premature pending conviction.

Issue: Whether the insured may implead the TPL insurer before final judgment on his own liability.

Held: Yes. TPL under then Sec. 374 and now Chapter VI insures directly against liability. The insurer's liability accrues on the injury's occurrence and not on final judgment. The victim may sue the insurer directly, and the insured may use the third-party complaint to avoid multiplicity. No-action clauses yield to the Rules of Court.

Doctrine: Occurrence and not conviction triggers TPL accrual. A third-party complaint is proper where a second suit would otherwise follow.

> Exam trap: Prematurity pleas fail against TPL; the insurer is in from occurrence, and no-action clauses do not block the third-party complaint.

**Q: A pedestrian is hit by a private car. Sworn police report and hospital receipts for P40,000 are filed with the offending car's insurer. The insurer refuses any payment until fault is proved and demands a full quitclaim for P15,000. The victim sues eight months after denial. Decide liability, amount floor, quitclaim, and prescription.**

A: The insurer must pay no-fault indemnity of at least P15,000.00 on the sworn papers without proof of fault, and it may not condition payment on a full quitclaim, while the balance above the floor is litigated on fault and the suit filed eight months after denial is within the Sec. 397 one-year period.
The controlling rules are Sec. 391(a)-(c) on the no-fault track, the Sec. 398 first paragraph barring quitclaim compulsion, and Sec. 397 on the six-month notice and one-year-from-denial suit.
Applied here, the sworn police report plus medical and disbursement proof satisfies Sec. 391(b), Sec. 391(c) points the claim at the directly offending vehicle, Sec. 398 bars the forced quitclaim, and eight months from denial is inside the one-year limit.
The insurer must therefore pay no-fault now, litigate the excess on fault, and the action is timely.

### Case: *La Razon Social Go Tiaoco y Hermanos v. Union Insurance Society of Canton*, G.R. No. 13983, Sept 1, 1919

Facts: Cargo of rice shipped Saigon to Cebu on the Hondagua. 1,473 sacks were damaged by seawater entering through a defective drain pipe. The hole predated the voyage from ordinary wear and tear. The cement-and-iron-patch repair was slovenly. The pipe vent sat below sea level when laden. The trial court dismissed the case. It was affirmed on appeal.

Issue: Whether a loss from a pre-voyage defect and negligent repair is a peril of the sea, and whether the implied warranty of seaworthiness bars recovery.

Held: No to coverage on both grounds. General words (all other perils, losses, misfortunes) cover only risks of like kind (ejusdem generis) with enumerated perils. A loss flowing from wear and tear or negligent unequipment is a peril of the ship, not of the sea. The implied warranty (then Act No. 2427, Sec. 106. Now Sec. 115) was breached: the ship was unseaworthy for the cargo at inception.

Doctrine: Insurer answers for accidents that may happen, not events that must happen. A ship seaworthy for hull purposes may be unseaworthy for cargo purposes.

> Exam trap: A defect known and badly patched before sailing sounds in unseaworthiness and peril of the ship; do not call every seawater entry a peril of the sea.

**Q: A time-policy hull insurer denies a mid-period voyage loss because the ship, seaworthy at inception of the period, developed unseaworthiness during an earlier voyage that the master unreasonably delayed repairing. The cargo owner separately claims although the hull was sound for hull purposes. Decide both claims.**

A: The hull claim fails for losses arising from the unrepaired defect because Sec. 117(a) demands seaworthiness at the commencement of every voyage and Sec. 120 exonerates the insurer on unreasonable delay in repairing unseaworthiness, while the cargo claim depends on Sec. 121, under which fitness for hull purposes does not decide fitness for cargo.
The controlling rules are Secs. 115-121 as quoted and tabled above.
Applied here, the master's unreasonable delay in repair triggers the Sec. 120 exoneration on the resulting hull loss, and cargo seaworthiness is separately measured at the inception of the risk against equipment, lading, and manning.
The denial is therefore proper on the resulting hull loss, and cargo recovery turns on cargo-specific unseaworthiness.

## 8. Suretyship

> A contract of suretyship is an agreement whereby a party called the surety guarantees the performance by another party called the principal or obligor of an obligation or undertaking in favor of a third party called the obligee. It includes official recognizances, stipulations, bonds or undertakings issued by any company by virtue of and under the provisions of Act No. 536, as amended by Act No. 2206.

> — Sec. 177, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> The liability of the surety or sureties shall be joint and several with the obligor and shall be limited to the amount of the bond. It is determined strictly by the terms of the contract of suretyship in relation to the principal contract between the obligor and the obligee.

> — Sec. 178, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> The surety is entitled to payment of the premium as soon as the contract of suretyship or bond is perfected and delivered to the obligor. No contract of suretyship or bonding shall be valid and binding unless and until the premium therefor has been paid, except where the obligee has accepted the bond, in which case the bond becomes valid and enforceable irrespective of whether or not the premium has been paid by the obligor to the surety: Provided, That if the contract of suretyship or bond is not accepted by, or filed with the obligee, the surety shall collect only a reasonable amount, not exceeding fifty percent (50%) of the premium due thereon as service fee plus the cost of stamps or other taxes imposed for the issuance of the contract or bond: Provided, however, That if the nonacceptance of the bond be due to the fault or negligence of the surety, no such service fee, stamps or taxes shall be collected. In the case of a continuing bond, the obligor shall pay the subsequent annual premium as it falls due until the contract of suretyship is cancelled by the obligee or by the Commissioner or by a court of competent jurisdiction, as the case may be.

> — Sec. 179, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> Pertinent provisions of the Civil Code of the Philippines shall be applied in a suppletory character whenever necessary in interpreting the provisions of a contract of suretyship.

> — Sec. 180, Insurance Code (RA 10607)

> By guaranty a person, called the guarantor, binds himself to the creditor to fulfill the obligation of the principal debtor in case the latter should fail to do so. If a person binds himself solidarily with the principal debtor, the provisions of Section 4, Chapter 3, Title I of this Book shall be observed. In such case the contract is called a suretyship.

> — Art. 2047, Civil Code

> The guarantor cannot be compelled to pay the creditor unless the latter has exhausted all the property of the debtor, and has resorted to all the legal remedies against the debtor.

> — Art. 2058, Civil Code

| Element/Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Contract and parties | Surety guarantees principal's performance to obligee; three parties: surety, principal (obligor), obligee (Sec. 177; Art. 2047) |
| Three statutory characters | Accessory to the principal contract; joint and several with the obligor; limited to the amount of the bond (Sec. 178) |
| Guaranty vs surety | Guarantor pays only on debtor's failure after exhaustion (Art. 2058); surety binds solidarily so creditor may proceed directly against either (Art. 2047, second paragraph; Art. 2059(2)) |

### Case: *First Lepanto-Taisho Insurance (FLT Prime) v. Chevron (Caltex)*, G.R. No. 177839, Jan 18, 2012

Facts: Fumitechniks got Surety Bond FLTICG (16) No. 01012 for P15,700,000 to secure a fuel-credit line with Caltex/Chevron. Bond recited payment in accordance with the terms of the parties' agreement and referred to a written agreement to be attached. Fumitechniks defaulted (dishonored P11,461,773.10 check. P15,084,030.30 claimed). No written distributorship or credit agreement existed or was relayed to the surety. RTC dismissed the case. CA reversed and held the surety liable. Supreme Court reinstated dismissal.

Issue: Whether the surety is liable where the bond expressly refers to a written principal agreement that was never communicated to it.

Held: No. Suretyship is ancillary and presupposes a principal contract. Liability is strictly by bond terms in relation to the principal contract (then Secs. 175-176. Now Secs. 177-178) and cannot be extended by implication. The creditor's non-communication of recited terms bars it from demanding performance, though the bond itself is not voided.

Doctrine: Recital controls: bond terms communicated to the surety fix its risk. It is strictly construed against the creditor. Doubts are resolved in favor of the solidary debtor.

> Exam trap: Non-communication of the recited principal terms bars the creditor's demand but does not void the bond; plead bar to performance, not nullity.

### Case: *Stronghold Insurance v. Tokyu Construction*, G.R. Nos. 158820-21, June 5, 2009

Facts: Gabriel subcontracted NAIA Terminal 2 SDS/STP works (P56,507,752 total), backed by Stronghold surety bonds (advance repayment) and performance bonds (due and timely performance), each one year. Gabriel defaulted. Scope was revised down and Tico bonds were later taken. Gabriel abandoned at partial completion. CIAC and CA held Stronghold liable. Supreme Court affirmed with modification.

Issue: Whether advance issuance, scope modification without notice, replacement bonds, or expiry defeats the surety's liability.

Held: Liable as modified. Ancillary does not mean the bond is void if issued ahead of the principal contract. Only a material alteration imposing a new obligation, taking one away, or changing legal effect releases. The scope revision did not make the obligation more onerous. Default had already fixed liability before the Tico bonds. But one-year performance bonds cap solidary liability to cost overrun and liquidated damages accruing during effectivity.

Doctrine: Modification without added onerosity does not release. Bond period caps the solidary reach.

> Exam trap: Expiry and replacement do not erase liability already fixed by default; cap recovery to the bond's effectivity window.

### Case: *Capital Insurance and Surety v. Del Monte Motor Works*, G.R. No. 159979, Dec 9, 2015

Facts: To discharge a preliminary attachment, a CISCO counterbond (Bond No. 00011-00005/JCL(3), June 10, 1997) was filed and approved. RTC later held defendants jointly and severally liable for P11,835,375.50 and enforced against the counterbond. Capital disowned the bond via internal limits (P5,000,000 authority), alleged forgery, lost records, and unpaid premium. RTC and CA sustained the bond. Supreme Court sustained validity but reversed levy on the Sec. 203 security deposit.

Issue: Whether internal authority limits, lost records, or unpaid premium defeat the counterbond, and whether the Sec. 203 deposit answers for it.

Held: Bond valid and enforceable. Deposit is exempt. Apparent authority binds the insurer as against the obligee. Unsubstantiated internal rules and bare non-payment claims do not discharge the proved bond. But Sec. 203 expressly exempts the security deposit from levy by any judgment creditor or claimant. It is a contingency fund for all policyholders.

Doctrine: Insurer proves invalid issuance or pays. Single claimants take no preference from the statutory deposit.

> Exam trap: Win the bond, lose the deposit levy; enforce against ordinary assets, never the Sec. 203 fund.

One-line cross-reference: *Malayan Insurance v. Philippines First Insurance (Wyeth/Reputable)*, G.R. No. 184300 (2012) — two policies for two persons with distinct interests is not double insurance Full card is in §1 reviewer's Part I-C ∩ §1 of this series. It is not re-carded here.

**2019 Bar Q: EFG, Inc. is indebted to Bank Y in the amount of P50,000,000.00. The loan was secured by a suretyship agreement issued by Z Insurance Co. Due to EFG, Inc's default, Bank Y filed a case against Z Insurance Co. as surety. There is also a pending criminal case for violation of the Bouncing Checks Law against the President of EFG, Inc., Mr. P, who signed the check as signatory for the company. Unable to meet its obligations as they fell due, EFG, Inc. filed a petition for rehabilitation. Finding the petition sufficient in form and substance, the court issued a Commencement Order, which was thereafter published. (a) Should the case filed against Z Insurance Co. be suspended in light of the Commencement Order? Explain. (2.5%) (b) Should the criminal case filed against Mr. P be suspended in light of the Commencement Order? Explain. (2.5%)**

A: (a) No, the case against Z Insurance Co. should not be suspended because the surety's liability is joint and several with the obligor under Sec. 178 and solidary under Art. 2047, second paragraph, so Bank Y may proceed directly against the surety while the rehabilitation stay covers only EFG, with the solidary surety answering independently up to the bond amount. (b) No, the criminal case against Mr. P should not be suspended because rehabilitation suspends only enforcement of civil claims against the debtor, while his criminal liability for the Bouncing Checks Law violation is personal to him as signatory and is not a claim against EFG's estate.
The controlling rules are Sec. 178 on joint-and-several liability within the bond cap and strict bond terms, Art. 2047 on solidary suretyship, and Arts. 2059(2) and 2084 on the absence of exhaustion where the obligation is solidary or the bond is judicial.
Applied here, Bank Y sues on the surety's independent solidary promise and not on EFG's stayed obligation, and Mr. P is prosecuted for his own act of issuing the worthless check and not for EFG's debt.
Neither case is therefore suspended: Bank Y collects from Z up to the bond amount, and the State prosecutes Mr. P.

**2018 Bar Q: Yellow Fin Tuna Corporation (Yellow Fin), a domestic corporation, applied for a credit facility in the amount of PhP 50 million with Yengzi Financial Corporation (YFC). The application was approved and the Credit Agreement was signed and took effect. Ysko and Yuan, Yellow Fin Chairman and President, respectively, executed a Continuing Suretyship Agreement in favor of YFC wherein they guaranteed the due and full payment and performance of Yellow Fin's guarantee obligations under the credit facility. YFC soon discovered material inconsistencies in the financial statements given by Yellow Fin, drawing YFC to conclude that Yellow Fin committed misrepresentation. Under the Credit Agreement, any misrepresentation by Yellow Fin or its sureties will constitute an event of default. YFC thus called an event of default and filed a complaint for sum of money against Yellow Fin, Ysko, and Yuan. Immediately thereafter, Yellow Fin filed a petition for rehabilitation. The court suspended the proceedings in YFC's complaint until the rehabilitation court disposed of the petition for rehabilitation. YFC posits that the suspension of the proceedings should only be with respect to Yellow Fin but not with respect to Ysko and Yuan. Is YFC correct? (2.5%)**

A: Yes, YFC is correct because a continuing suretyship guarantees full payment and performance of present and future obligations under the facility under Art. 2053, and sureties bind themselves solidarily under Sec. 178 and Art. 2047, so YFC may enforce directly against Ysko and Yuan despite the stay on Yellow Fin, with the solidary sureties answering on their own promise and remaining suable while retaining reimbursement against the debtor later under Arts. 2066-2067.
The controlling rules are Art. 2053 on future debts secured, Sec. 178 and Art. 2047 on joint-and-several and solidary liability, and Art. 2059(2) on the absence of excussion where the obligation is solidary.
Applied here, Ysko and Yuan signed a continuing solidary guarantee of the whole facility, so YFC's declaration of default on misrepresentation triggered their independent liability, unaffected by Yellow Fin's rehabilitation stay.
Suspension is therefore proper as to Yellow Fin only, and the complaint proceeds against Ysko and Yuan.

## Exam Traps

1. Double insurance is regulated and not prohibited. The same person plus the same subject plus the same interest triggers order of claiming and contribution. Denial is never automatic. See 2025 Q5 and Malayan and Secs. 95 to 96.
2. The original insured has no interest in the reinsurance contract. He cannot sue the reinsurer. See Sec. 100.
3. Reinsurance is presumed to be indemnity against liability. It is not merely indemnity against damage. See Sec. 99.
4. Disclosure to the reinsurer is required. The exception is an automatic reinsurance treaty. See Sec. 98.
5. Facultative reinsurance is risk by risk cession. Treaty reinsurance is automatic cession of a defined book. See Fieldmen's.
6. Fire alteration avoids coverage only with increased risk plus lack of consent. Harmless alteration does not affect the policy. Benign subsequent acts also do not affect it. See Secs. 170 to 172.
7. The Sec. 175 pledge prohibition targets transfer to one acting as the insurer's agent. Do not extend it to ordinary mortgagee loss payable clauses. See Que Chee Gan.
8. Open policy value is fixed at loss. Valued policy value is fixed on the face. Fire open policies like Galian construe coinsurance against the insurer on ambiguity.
9. Suicide after two years pays. Insanity suicide pays at any time. A shorter contractual period binds. See Sec. 183.
10. Concealment bars only within the two-year contestable period. After that the insurer cannot prove void ab initio. See 2019 H and 2019 Yate and Sec. 48.
11. A life beneficiary designation is revocable by default. Only an express irrevocable designation made with legal formalities binds. Even then the killer beneficiary and interest rules still apply. See 2018 Yin Yang.
12. A life policy passes to any transferee regardless of insurable interest. Do not import property insurance interest rules into life transfers. See Sec. 184.
13. Life annuity needs living determinate lives at constitution. It pays only on proof that the measuring life subsists. See Arts. 2022 and 2027.
14. Nonpayment of annuity income never recovers the capital absent stipulation. The remedy is arrears plus security only. See Art. 2024.
15. Casualty classification follows the Sec. 176 enumeration. Motor liability sits inside casualty. But CMVLI procedure follows Secs. 386 to 402 and not Sec. 176 alone. See Palermo and Villacorta and Perla.
16. No-fault pays on the sworn papers with no negligence proof. The floor is a minimum and not a ceiling. No quitclaim may be exacted for the balance. See Secs. 391 and 398.
17. CMVLI cancellation is void without the 15-day prior notice to owner and operator and LTO. Confiscation of plates follows uncured cancellation. See Sec. 393.
18. Change of ownership or engine needs no new policy until next registration. Endorsement plus LTO filing continues cover. See Sec. 395.
19. Improper deviation cuts liability only for subsequent loss. Loss before deviation stays covered. See Sec. 128.
20. Seaworthiness is judged per voyage stage and per subject. Hull seaworthy does not imply cargo seaworthy. See Secs. 117 to 121.
21. Constructive total loss without timely explicit abandonment still recovers actual loss. Abandonment perfects the total loss claim. It does not forfeit all recovery. See Sec. 157.
22. The surety is sued directly with no exhaustion. The guarantor is not sued directly. Excussion comes first for guaranty. See Sec. 178 and the Civil Code on guaranty.
23. Continuing suretyship covers future debts under the credit line until revoked. The principal rehabilitation stay does not stop the creditor suit against the solidary surety. See 2018 Q V and 2019 B.12.
24. Bond premium is due on perfection and delivery of the bond. It is not due on the principal default. See Sec. 179.
25. A counterbond surety answers as judicial security on the bond terms. Forgery or nonissuance defenses need proof and not mere allegation. See Capital.
26. Criminal liability of the corporate president is personal. It survives the corporation rehabilitation stay. See 2019 B.12(b).
