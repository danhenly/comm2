## 2. Learning Objectives

1. Sort every pleaded excuse into its proper kind, whether a goods defense under Art. 1734, a passenger defense under Arts. 1756 to 1763, or a procedural bar of notice or prescription.
2. Run proximate-cause problems under Arts. 1739 to 1741 and Art. 1762, separating sole causes that excuse from concurrent and merely contributing faults that keep or reduce liability.
3. State the exclusivity of goods defenses against the open rebuttal structure of passenger defenses, and decide when a private-carriage stipulation displaces both.
4. Defeat false fortuity claims on fire, forecasted storms, hijacking, defective machinery, and blown tires by testing carrier participation and diligence before, during, and after the occurrence.
5. Apply the public-enemy, goods-character, and public-authority excuses with their strict proof, including lawful power for official orders and forestalling diligence for vicious goods.
6. Divide passenger losses among employees, fellow passengers, strangers, and the passenger himself under Arts. 1759 to 1763 without imputing driver fault to riders.
7. Time notices and suits under Art. 366 and COGSA Sec. 3(6), and bind subrogated insurers to the same periods as the shipper they stepped into.
8. Judge limiting stipulations under Arts. 1744 to 1752 and COGSA Sec. 4(5), voiding what erases diligence while keeping reasonable declared values and strike-delay terms.

## 1. Kinds of Defenses; Proximate Causation

> "Common carriers are responsible for the loss, destruction, or deterioration of the goods, unless the same is due to any of the following causes only: (1) Flood, storm, earthquake, lightning, or other natural disaster or calamity; (2) Act of the public enemy in war, whether international or civil; (3) Act or omission of the shipper or owner of the goods; (4) The character of the goods or defects in the packing or in the containers; (5) Order or act of competent public authority."
> — Art. 1734, Civil Code of the Philippines

> "In order that the common carrier may be exempted from responsibility, the natural disaster must have been the proximate and only cause of the loss. However, the common carrier must exercise due diligence to prevent or minimize loss before, during and after the occurrence of flood, storm or other natural disaster in order that the common carrier may be exempted from liability for the loss, destruction, or deterioration of the goods. The same duty is incumbent upon the common carrier in case of an act of the public enemy referred to in article 1734, No. 2."
> — Art. 1739, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Kinds by cargo | Goods defenses close on the five Art. 1734 causes, passenger defenses open on diligence rebuttal, and procedural bars of notice and prescription can defeat either claim without reaching fault |
| Kinds by source | Some excuses erase liability where proved, some stipulations trim its measure, and some lapses of the claimant forfeit the remedy though fault existed |
| Proximate and only cause | The excepted cause must explain the whole loss by itself, since a concurring carrier fault keeps liability alive under Art. 1739 |
| Diligence around the occurrence | Due care must run before with during with after the calamity or enemy act, since diligence only at one stage never satisfies Art. 1739 |
| Negligent delay | Delay through carrier fault keeps liability alive though a later calamity strikes, under Art. 1740 |
| Mere contribution by the cargo side | Where the shipper or owner merely contributed while carrier negligence stayed the proximate cause, the carrier answers with damages equitably reduced under Art. 1741 |
| Mere contribution by the passenger | Where passenger negligence merely contributed while carrier negligence stayed the proximate cause, recovery stands with damages equitably reduced under Art. 1762 |
| Absence of causation | Where the pleaded cause did not touch the loss at all, as with cargo already discharged before the storm, the defense fails for want of causal link |

Kind sorts the fight before proof begins. A storm pleaded against drowned passengers runs through Arts. 1755 and 1756 with utmost diligence, while the same storm pleaded against soaked cargo runs through the closed list of Art. 1734 with the Art. 1735 presumption. Regional Container Lines v. Netherlands Insurance (G.R. No. 168151, 2009) adds that the presumption needs no express negligence finding, so the carrier that names an excepted cause must still prove it stood alone. Ganzon v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. L-48757, 1988) shows the cost of mis-sorting, since the carrier that pleaded authority below could not switch to fortuity on appeal.

| Cause Status | Effect on Liability |
|---|---|
| Excepted cause as proximate and only cause with full diligence | Carrier freed |
| Excepted cause concurring with carrier negligence or delay | Carrier liable in full under Arts. 1739 and 1740 |
| Cargo-side fault merely contributing with carrier negligence proximate | Carrier liable with damages equitably reduced under Arts. 1741 and 1762 |
| Pleaded cause with no causal touch on the loss | Defense fails for absence of causation |

**2019 Bar Q: LMN, Inc. operates a beach resort in a secluded island off the coast of Puerto Princesa City, Palawan. It operates three (3) motorized boats to ferry its guests from the city proper to the island resort and vice-versa. During one rainy morning, the guests were informed that the ferry services for that day were cancelled due to a storm forecast. In order to appease the apparent dismay of most of the guests who will miss their flight back to Manila, the boat captain of one of LMN, Inc.'s motorized boats decided to push through with its trip back to the city. Shortly after the boat sailed, the storm hit and the winds and waves became stronger, causing engine trouble to the boat. Unfortunately, the boat capsized and sank, resulting in the death of one of the passengers, Mr. X. This prompted Mr. X's heirs to file a complaint for damages against LMN, Inc., which they alleged to be a common carrier. In its defense, LMN, Inc. maintained that it is not a common carrier because its boats are not available to the general public but only ferry resort guests and employees. (a) May LMN, Inc. be considered a common carrier? Explain. (3%) (b) Assuming LMN, Inc. is a common carrier, may it be absolved from liability on the ground of fortuitous event? Explain. (2%)**

A: Yes, LMN, Inc. is a common carrier, but it is not absolved because its voluntary sailing into a forecasted storm concurs with the waves and defeats fortuity.
Under Art. 1732 of the Civil Code, one who offers carriage by water to the public for compensation is a common carrier, and an ancillary resort ferry with an imputed fare inside the package price satisfies the test even for a narrow clientele as Sps. Cruz v. Sun Holidays (G.R. No. 186312, 2010) holds. Under Art. 1739 of the Civil Code, a natural disaster excuses only where it stood as the proximate and only cause with due diligence before and during and after the occurrence, and eleven-mile winds meeting a cracked lighter prove unseaworthiness rather than calamity under Compañia Maritima v. Insurance Company of North America (G.R. No. L-18965, 1964).
Here, LMN operated three motorized boats that ferried guests between the city proper and its island resort with the fare carried inside the package price. The trips for that rainy morning stood cancelled because of a storm forecast, yet the captain pushed through with the return trip to appease guests who would miss their Manila flights. Shortly after sailing the forecasted storm struck with stronger winds and waves, the boat developed engine trouble mid-voyage, and it capsized and sank with the death of Mr. X. Reading narrow clientele as private carriage mistakes headcount for holding out, while reading a forecasted blow met by voluntary sailing as fortuity ignores the concurring fault that breaks the proximate and only cause.
LMN therefore answers as a common carrier under the Art. 1756 presumption of fault for the death of Mr. X, and the fortuity defense fails, so the heirs recover unless LMN proves utmost diligence no voyage into a forecasted storm can supply.

## 2. Defenses in Carriage of Goods in Their Exclusivity and in Carriage of Passengers

> "In all cases other than those mentioned in Nos. 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 of the preceding article, if the goods are lost, destroyed or deteriorated, common carriers are presumed to have been at fault or to have acted negligently, unless they prove that they observed extraordinary diligence as required in article 1733."
> — Art. 1735, Civil Code of the Philippines

> "In case of death of or injuries to passengers, common carriers are presumed to have been at fault or to have acted negligently, unless they prove that they observed extraordinary diligence as prescribed in articles 1733 and 1755."
> — Art. 1756, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Exclusivity for goods | The carrier answers unless the loss fits one of the five causes and no other excuse counts, since Art. 1734 says only |
| Presumption for goods | Outside the five, fault is presumed and only proof of extraordinary diligence rebuts it under Art. 1735 |
| Open rebuttal for passengers | Any proof of utmost diligence rebuts, since Art. 1756 names no closed list and asks only for the diligence of Arts. 1733 and 1755 |
| Employee channel for passengers | Employee negligence and wilful acts bind the carrier though beyond scope or against orders under Art. 1759, with no stipulation escape under Art. 1760 |
| Stranger channel for passengers | Fellow-passenger and stranger harm binds where good-family diligence of the crew could have prevented or stopped it under Art. 1763 |
| Private carriage contrast | Where the vessel serves select charterers alone, stipulation with Arts. 361 to 362 of the Code of Commerce governs and the Civil Code presumption does not run, as National Steel Corporation v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 112287, 1997) holds |

Exclusivity is the goods rule that has no passenger twin. A carrier that loses sugar to an unlisted cause like warehouse rats or stevedore pilferage cannot invent a sixth excuse, since the Code closed the door with the word only. The passenger carrier faces no such door, since it may rebut with any convincing proof of utmost care, from working brakes to a timely refusal to sail. Planters Products v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 101503, 1993) shows the goods door working properly, since the line escaped only by proving an actual listed cause in goods character with extraordinary care. National Steel Corporation v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 112287, 1997) shows the door disappearing entirely, since private tramp service answers on charter terms with shipper-side risk under Arts. 361 to 362.

| Goods Defense | Passenger Defense |
|---|---|
| Closed list of five causes under Art. 1734 | Open proof of utmost diligence under Art. 1756 |
| Unlisted cause triggers presumed fault under Art. 1735 | Any cause tests what diligence could have prevented |
| Shipper act excuses only as Art. 1734(3) with Art. 1741 reduction | Passenger fault reduces under Art. 1762 where carrier fault stays proximate |
| Employee fault never excuses and never stipulates away under Art. 1745(5) | Employee fault binds even beyond scope under Arts. 1759 to 1760 |

**Q: A vessel loses bagged coffee to warehouse rats during a tariff stop while a passenger on the same voyage is hurt by a falling hatch cover. The carrier pleads unlisted vermin for the coffee and talks of open rebuttal for the passenger. Which plea follows the correct structure?**

A: The carrier likely pays for the coffee lost to vermin while it keeps a fighting chance on the passenger fall, because rats fit no closed goods excuse and the hatch injury turns on utmost diligence.
Under Art. 1734 of the Civil Code, the carrier answers for goods loss unless it fits one of the five exclusive causes, and unlisted vermin or pilferage triggers the Art. 1735 presumption of fault that yields only to proof of extraordinary diligence. Under Art. 1756 of the Civil Code, death or injury of a passenger is presumed to be carrier fault, yet the passenger carrier may rebut with any convincing proof of the utmost diligence of Arts. 1733 and 1755. Under Planters Products v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 101503, 1993), the goods carrier escapes only by proving an actual listed cause with extraordinary care over the cargo.
Here, the vessel lost bagged coffee to warehouse rats during a tariff stop, which names no flood or storm or public enemy or shipper act or goods character or authority order and therefore leaves the Art. 1735 presumption standing. The same voyage hurt a passenger with a falling hatch cover, which is open rebuttal territory where inspection records and maintenance logs can show the utmost care. Reading vermin as goods character mistakes a preventable custody failure for inherent vice, while reading every falling object as automatic liability mistakes a rebuttable presumption for absolute insurance.
The carrier therefore answers for the coffee unless it proves extraordinary diligence over the stored bags, while it defeats the passenger claim where it proves utmost diligence over the hatch, so the coffee claim likely succeeds and the fall turns on the maintenance proof.

## 3. Fortuitous Event with Carrier Participation, Rationale, Fire, Storm, Hijacking, Mechanical Defects, Tire Blowouts, and Other Invalid Defenses

> "Except in cases expressly specified by the law, or when it is otherwise declared by stipulation, or when the nature of the obligation requires the assumption of risk, no person shall be responsible for those events which could not be foreseen, or which, though foreseen, were inevitable."
> — Art. 1174, Civil Code of the Philippines

> "If the common carrier negligently incurs in delay in transporting the goods, a natural disaster shall not free such carrier from responsibility."
> — Art. 1740, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Fortuity defined | An event unforeseeable or though foreseen inevitable excuses only where law, stipulation, or assumed risk does not say otherwise under Art. 1174 |
| Carrier participation defeats | Unseaworthiness, unroadworthiness, or negligent navigation concurring with the calamity keeps liability, since the cause no longer stands alone under Art. 1739 |
| Rationale | Carriage is impressed with public interest, so the carrier insures with its diligence rather than with the weather, and fortuity stays narrow |
| Fire | Fire appears in none of the five Art. 1734 causes, so the carrier stays under the Art. 1735 presumption and must prove extraordinary diligence with the fire origin and spread fully accounted for |
| Storm | Storm excuses only as the proximate and only cause with due diligence before with during with after, and sailing into a forecasted blow or dawdling into its path defeats the claim under Arts. 1739 and 1740 |
| Hijacking | Armed seizure by lawless men fits no goods cause by itself, so the carrier must still prove extraordinary precautions with route, guards, and seals, while passenger hijack harm tests preventability under Art. 1763 |
| Mechanical defects | Cracked hulls, tired engines, and worn brakes are carrier-side wants of fitness rather than calamities, so breakdown mid-voyage proves the opposite of diligence |
| Tire blowouts | A burst tire on worn treads with skipped inspection is want of roadworthiness, and only a roadworthy unit meeting a truly extraneous road hazard keeps the defense arguable |
| Acquittal of crew | Criminal acquittal of driver or crew never bars the civil carrier suit, since contract liability stands on its own proof as settled in Part II-B |
| Pointing at handlers | Blaming stevedores, arrastre men, or subcontractors never rebuts by itself, since cargo duty is non-delegable as Regional Container Lines v. Netherlands Insurance (G.R. No. 168151, 2009) holds |
| Mere difficulty | Hardship in performance is no force majeure, since the obligation stayed possible as Ganzon v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. L-48757, 1988) holds |

Fortuity asks two questions in strict order. It asks whether the event qualifies, and only then whether the carrier kept its hands clean. Compañia Maritima v. Insurance Company of North America (G.R. No. L-18965, 1964) failed at both levels, since eleven-mile winds were no storm under wind-velocity measure and the cracked lighter with open manholes was unseaworthy besides. Phil-Am General Insurance v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 116940, 1997) failed the same way, since improper deck loading made the vessel unseaworthy before ordinary swells arrived. The Nautica and LMN storm problems apply the identical sorting to passengers, and the 2016-VI Nautica answer in Part II-B §5 works through bulkhead modification with monsoon rains in full. Sps. Cruz v. Sun Holidays (G.R. No. 186312, 2010) with Luzon Stevedoring v. Public Service Commission (G.R. No. L-5458, 1953) confirm that narrow resort and clientele service still faces this full fortuity test as common carriage.

| Valid Fortuity | False Fortuity |
|---|---|
| Unforeseen typhoon striking a fit ship that took precautions before with during with after | Forecasted storm voluntarily sailed into with engine trouble mid-voyage |
| Latent defect no inspection could reveal on a maintained unit | Cracked hull, worn treads, and skipped inspections meeting ordinary voyage stress |
| Fire from a truly extraneous source met with full diligence and spread control | Fire of unaccounted origin in carrier custody with no diligence proved |
| Overwhelming armed attack despite guards, seals, and route care | Predictable pilferage at a known hotspot with no precautions taken |

**Q: A warehouse holding consigned sugar burns from an unknown origin at night while a bus with bald tires blows one on the highway and topples. The warehouseman pleads fire and the bus line pleads blowout. Which plea has a path and what must each prove?**

A: The warehouseman keeps a narrow path on the fire where it proves extraordinary diligence, while the bus line keeps almost none on the blowout because bald treads prove want of roadworthiness.
Under Art. 1735 of the Civil Code, loss outside the five Art. 1734 causes is presumed to be carrier fault, and fire appears in none of those causes, so the carrier must prove extraordinary diligence with the origin and spread of the fire fully accounted for. Under Art. 1739 of the Civil Code, a calamity excuses only as the proximate and only cause with diligence before and during and after, and carrier-side unfitness concurring with the event keeps liability alive. Under Compañia Maritima v. Insurance Company of North America (G.R. No. L-18965, 1964), a cracked hull meeting ordinary winds proves unseaworthiness rather than fortuity.
Here, the warehouse holding consigned sugar burned from an unknown origin at night, so the warehouseman carrier must show watchmen and wiring and extinguishers and segregation of the stored sugar. The bus ran on bald treads with skipped inspection, blew one tire on the highway, and toppled, so the blowout grew out of want of roadworthiness rather than out of an extraneous road hazard. Reading an unaccounted fire as automatic fortuity ignores the unlisted-cause rule, while reading a blowout on worn treads as calamity mistakes carrier-side unfitness for an inevitable event.
The sugar claim therefore turns on whether the warehouseman proves extraordinary diligence over custody, while the bus claim fails on the treads alone, so the bus line answers under the presumption and the sugar carrier survives only on full diligence proof.
## 4. Public Enemy; Nature of Goods and Improper Packing; Order of Public Authority

> "If through the order of public authority the goods are seized or destroyed, the common carrier is not responsible, provided said public authority had power to issue the order."
> — Art. 1743, Civil Code of the Philippines

> "Even if the loss, destruction, or deterioration of the goods should be caused by the character of the goods, or the faulty nature of the packing or of the containers, the common carrier must exercise due diligence to forestall or lessen the loss."
> — Art. 1742, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Public enemy defined | Organized force in war whether international or civil excuses under Art. 1734(2), while common thieves and robbers never qualify as the enemy |
| Enemy diligence | The same before with during with after diligence of Art. 1739 applies to enemy acts, since the carrier must still guard, reroute, and salvage what war threatens |
| Nature of goods | Inherent vice, solubility, ferment, and perishability excuse under Art. 1734(4) only where the carrier proves the goods character truly caused the loss |
| Improper packing | Weak crates, thin sacks, and leaking drums excuse under the same cause only where the defect truly produced the deterioration |
| Acceptance with knowledge | Taking goods with apparent packing defects never relieves by itself, since the carrier that accepts must still handle with the diligence the known condition demands |
| Forestalling duty | Even where goods character or packing caused the loss, the carrier must exercise due diligence to forestall or lessen it under Art. 1742 |
| Handling allocation | Agreed free-in-and-out terms put stevedoring on the cargo interests, so loss in consignee-side unloading stays there as Planters Products v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 101503, 1993) holds |
| Competent authority | Seizure or destruction by order excuses only where the authority had power to issue the order under Art. 1743 |
| Proof of power | The carrier must show the official power with the lawfulness of the order with issuance under legal process, since Ganzon v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. L-48757, 1988) demands all three |
| Lawless orders | Shakedown orders, ultra vires seizures, and armed coercion without process never qualify, and obedience to them is carrier fault rather than obedience to law |
| No change of theory | A carrier that pleaded authority below cannot switch to fortuity on appeal, since Ganzon v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. L-48757, 1988) bars the shift |

Three strict excuses share one strict method. The carrier names the cause within Art. 1734, then proves each statutory qualifier, then shows its own diligence on top. Planters Products v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 101503, 1993) models the goods-character win, since sealed hatches with intact seals met urea solubility with consignee-side rainy unloading under free-in-and-out terms, so the shortage stayed a shipper-side risk. Ganzon v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. L-48757, 1988) models the authority loss, since an acting mayor with armed men and no power over the scrap ordered dumping as part of a shakedown, so the carrier that obeyed answered with actual with exemplary damages.

| Excuse | What Must Be Proved | What Defeats It |
|---|---|---|
| Public enemy in war | Organized hostile force with due diligence before with during with after | Common thieves, predictable raids met with no guards, surrender without resistance where resistance was feasible |
| Character of goods and packing | Inherent vice or defect as true cause with forestalling diligence under Art. 1742 | Acceptance of apparent defects without added care, stowage that aggravates the vice, consignee-side handling blamed on the ship |
| Order of competent authority | Power with lawfulness with legal process under Art. 1743 | Shakedown or ultra vires orders, orders without process, obedience where refusal and unloading ashore stayed possible |

**Q: Scrap iron rides a carrier lighter when an acting mayor with armed men orders the crew to dump it into the sea as part of a shakedown of the shipper. The carrier obeys and pleads competent authority. Does the excuse stand?**

A: No, the excuse fails, because a shakedown order from an acting mayor without power over the goods is no competent authority and obedience to it is carrier fault.
Under Art. 1743 of the Civil Code, seizure or destruction by order excuses only where the public authority had power to issue the order. Under Ganzon v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. L-48757, 1988), the carrier must prove official power with a lawful order issued under legal process, and an acting mayor with armed men pressing a money demand over goods the town never owned supplies none of the three. The same decision bars switching from authority below to fortuity on appeal.
Here, scrap iron rode a carrier lighter when an acting mayor with armed men ordered the crew to dump it into the sea as part of a shakedown of the shipper. The mayor held no power over the scrap, the order carried no lawful process, and the scraps could have been unloaded ashore and delivered after the dispute instead of dumped. Reading obedience to armed coercion as obedience to law confuses submission with authority, while reading difficulty in the dispute as fortuity ignores that possible performance with unloading ashore defeats impossibility.
The line therefore answers under the Art. 1735 presumption with no diligence proved, and it bears the actual with exemplary exposure that Ganzon imposes, so the authority defense fails and the carrier pays for the dumped scrap.

**Q: Bulk urea sails in sealed holds with intact seals and arrives short with part contaminated by sand after eleven days of rainy unloading by consignee labor under free-in-and-out terms. The consignee sues for shortage and pollution. Who bears the loss?**

A: The consignee bears the loss, because inherent urea solubility with shipper-side unloading under free-in-and-out terms caused the shortage despite a diligent voyage.
Under Art. 1734 of the Civil Code, the character of the goods excuses the carrier where the inherent vice truly caused the loss. Under Art. 1742 of the Civil Code, even where goods character caused the loss, the carrier must still exercise due diligence to forestall or lessen it. Under Planters Products v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 101503, 1993), sealed hatches with intact seals and clean holds prove the extraordinary care the voyage demanded, and loss in consignee-side unloading under free-in-and-out terms stays a shipper-side risk.
Here, bulk urea sailed in sealed holds with intact seals, arrived short with part polluted by sand, and spent eleven days in rainy unloading handled by consignee labor under free-in-and-out terms. The sealed voyage shows the forestalling care, while the rainy open-air handling with sandy hauling sits inside the cargo interests stevedoring rather than inside carrier custody. Reading soluble cargo handled in rain by the consignee as carrier fault mistakes agreed allocation for breach, while reading shortage alone as breach ignores the proven inherent vice.
The Art. 1735 presumption therefore stands rebutted and the claim fails, so the consignee absorbs the shortage and the pollution that its own unloading produced.

## 5. Defenses in Carriage of Passengers; Acts of Employees, of Other Passengers and Third Persons, and of Shipper or Passenger

> "Common carriers are liable for the death of or injuries to passengers through the negligence or wilful acts of the former's employees, although such employees may have acted beyond the scope of their authority or in violation of the orders of the common carriers. This liability of the common carriers does not cease upon proof that they exercised all the diligence of a good father of a family in the selection and supervision of their employees."
> — Art. 1759, Civil Code of the Philippines

> "A common carrier is responsible for injuries suffered by a passenger on account of the wilful acts or negligence of other passengers or of strangers, if the common carrier's employees through the exercise of the diligence of a good father of a family could have prevented or stopped the act or omission."
> — Art. 1763, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Passenger defense structure | Death or injury presumes fault under Art. 1756, and the carrier escapes only by proving the utmost diligence of Arts. 1733 and 1755 |
| Acts of employees | Negligence and wilful acts of crew bind the carrier though beyond scope or against orders under Art. 1759, with selection diligence no defense |
| No stipulation escape | Employee responsibility cannot be eliminated or limited by stipulation, notices, or ticket statements under Art. 1760 |
| No duty to inquire | The passenger need not investigate fitness, licensing, or regulatory compliance before boarding, since vigilance is the non-delegable duty of the carrier |
| Rationale | The carrier chooses with commands with controls its crew, so the law pins their fault on the enterprise that profits from their work |
| Theft by employees | Pocketed watches, pilfered baggage, and extorted fares are wilful employee acts under Art. 1759, and Art. 1745(6) voids any bill term dispensing with ordinary-thief liability |
| Acts of fellow passengers | Assaults and negligence by co-riders bind where crew with good-family diligence could have prevented or stopped them under Art. 1763 |
| Acts of strangers | Rock-throwing outsiders, robbers, and track intruders bind on the same preventability test, while truly unforeseeable and unstoppable attacks break the chain |
| No absolute insurer | The carrier answers for preventable harm rather than for every harm, since the 2018-IV Yatco answer in Part II-B §4 applies diligence rather than insurance |
| Contributory negligence of shipper | Mere contribution keeps carrier liability with equitable reduction where carrier negligence stays proximate under Art. 1741 |
| Contributory negligence of passenger | Mere contribution keeps recovery with equitable reduction where carrier negligence stays proximate under Art. 1762, with self-care measured by Art. 1761 |
| Causation sorting | Sole passenger or shipper fault that truly caused the loss frees the carrier, while concurrent carrier fault keeps it liable in full or reduced |
| Avoidable consequences | The harmed party must take reasonable steps to avoid aggravating the loss, since neglect that enlarges damages counts in the equitable sorting |
| Assumption of risk | Knowingly choosing the peril, like the open truck back in Lara v. Valencia (G.R. No. L-9907, 1958), weighs against recovery where it caused or aggravated the harm |
| Last clear chance | The party with the final opportunity to avert the casualty bears it, so the train operator with working brakes and sight distance answers though the motorist stalled first, as the 2016-VII PNR answer in Part II-B §6 applies it |

Passenger defenses divide the human actors while keeping the diligence question single. The crew channel binds almost always, the stranger channel binds where prevention was feasible, and the victim channel only reduces where carrier fault stayed proximate. British Airways v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 92288, 1993) with LRTA v. Navidad (G.R. No. 145804, 2003) fix when the duty opens, Sps. Pereña v. Sps. Nicolas (G.R. No. 157917, 2012) with LTFRB v. Valenzuela (G.R. No. 242860, 2019) fix who counts as a carrier, and Lara v. Valencia (G.R. No. L-9907, 1958) fixes what the rider owes himself. The 2018-IV Yatco rock-throwing problem with its fellow-passenger stabbing variant, worked in Part II-B §4, remains the model application for stranger and co-rider harm, and the 2016-VII crossing problem, worked in Part II-B §6, remains the model for last clear chance with non-imputation to riders.

| Harm Source | Rule | Carrier Escapes Where |
|---|---|---|
| Employee negligence or wilful act | Binds even beyond scope under Arts. 1759 to 1760 | Never by diligence in hiring or by stipulation, only by disproving the act or the harm |
| Fellow passenger or stranger | Binds where preventable with good-family diligence under Art. 1763 | Attack unforeseeable and unstoppable despite proper crew vigilance |
| Passenger or shipper fault alone as sole cause | Frees the carrier for want of carrier causation | Victim fault truly explains the whole loss |
| Victim fault concurring with carrier fault | Recovery stands reduced equitably under Arts. 1741 and 1762 | Reduction never reaches zero while carrier negligence stays proximate |

**Q: A conductor pockets a sleeping passenger watch while a stranger at a traffic stop hurls a rock through the window and blinds another rider. The line pleads stranger fortuity for both losses. How does liability divide?**

A: The line pays for the pocketed watch as of course, while the rock blinding turns on whether good-family crew vigilance could have prevented or stopped the attack.
Under Art. 1759 of the Civil Code, the carrier answers for the negligence and wilful acts of its employees though they acted beyond scope or against orders, and diligence in selection and supervision is no defense. Under Art. 1760 of the Civil Code, that employee responsibility cannot be eliminated or limited by stipulation or ticket statement. Under Art. 1763 of the Civil Code, the carrier answers for injuries from fellow passengers or strangers only where its employees through the diligence of a good father of a family could have prevented or stopped the act. Under LRTA v. Navidad (G.R. No. 145804, 2003), the safety duty behind these articles is non-delegable from fare payment with entry.
Here, a conductor pocketed a sleeping passenger watch, which is a wilful employee act inside Art. 1759 though done against orders, so no hiring care or ticket term escapes it. A stranger at a traffic stop hurled a rock through the window and blinded another rider, which is stranger harm inside Art. 1763 where closed windows and guards and route care decide the outcome. Reading the theft as outside scope mistakes the very channel Art. 1759 closes, while reading every thrown rock as automatic liability mistakes preventability for insurance.
Theft therefore pays as of course with no diligence defense available, while the blinding pays only where the prevention proof fails, so the line answers for the watch and reaches the merits on the rock.

## 6. Notice of Claim and Prescription in Overland and Coastwise Shipping, in International Carriage by Sea, and in Subrogation

> "Within twenty-four hours following the receipt of the merchandise, the claim against the carrier for damage or average which may be found therein upon opening the packages, may be made, provided that the indications of the damage or average which gives rise to the claim cannot be ascertained from the outside part of such packages, in which case the claim shall be admitted only at the time of receipt. After the periods mentioned have elapsed, or the transportation charges have been paid, no claim shall be admitted against the carrier with regard to the condition in which the goods transported were delivered."
> — Art. 366, Code of Commerce, as quoted in New Zealand Insurance Co. v. Choa Joy (G.R. No. L-7311, September 30, 1955)

> "If the loss or damage is not apparent, the notice must be given within three days of delivery. The notice in writing need not be given if the state of the goods has at the time of their receipt been the subject of joint survey or inspection. In any event the carrier and the ship shall be discharged from all liability in respect of loss or damage unless suit is brought within one year after delivery of the goods or the date when the goods should have been delivered; Provided, That, if a notice of loss or damage, either apparent or concealed, is not given as provided for in this section, that fact shall not affect or prejudice the right of the shipper to bring suit within one year after the delivery of the goods or the date when the goods should have been delivered. In the case of any actual or apprehended loss or damage, the carrier and the receiver shall give all reasonable facilities to each other for inspecting and tallying the goods."
> — COGSA Sec. 3(6), as quoted in Belgian Overseas Chartering v. Philippine First Insurance (G.R. No. 143133, June 5, 2002)

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Purpose of notice | Prompt notice lets the carrier survey, trace, and defend while evidence is fresh, so delay forfeits what diligence can no longer test |
| Overland and coastwise notice | Concealed damage found on opening must be claimed within twenty-four hours from receipt, while apparent damage claimable from the outside must be made at receipt itself under Art. 366 |
| Notice cutoff | Lapse of the period or payment of freight without protest bars the condition claim under the second paragraph of Art. 366 |
| No-delivery limit | Art. 366 applies only where goods were actually received by the consignee, so total non-delivery or loss before destination needs no twenty-four-hour notice as New Zealand Insurance Co. v. Choa Joy (G.R. No. L-7311, 1955) holds |
| International sea notice | Concealed loss or damage needs written notice within three days from delivery, apparent damage needs notice at receipt, and joint survey at receipt excuses written notice under COGSA Sec. 3(6) |
| One-year suit period | International sea suits must be brought within one year after delivery or the date delivery was due, and late notice alone never shortens that year under the same section |
| Suppletory reach | The one-year limitation applies suppletorily to transit cargo claims where neither the Civil Code nor the Code of Commerce states a specific period, as the Loadstar ruling quoted in Belgian Overseas holds |
| Overland suit prescription | With no special Code of Commerce suit period, the Civil Code framework governs by action type, namely ten years on a written contract, six on an oral one, and four on quasi-delict under Arts. 1144 to 1146 with the saving clause of Art. 1148 |
| Subrogation standing | The insurer that pays the shipper steps into the shipper shoes and sues the carrier as assignee, as Compañia Maritima v. Insurance Company of North America (G.R. No. L-18965, 1964) holds |
| Subrogation periods | The one-year period binds shipper with consignee with insurer and every bill holder alike, so the subrogee inherits the same clock as its subrogor |

Notice and prescription are procedural bars that decide cases before fault is reached. The overland consignee that opens crates on the third day and complains on the fifth has already lost the condition claim under Art. 366, though the no-delivery consignee whose cargo never arrived never needed that notice at all. The international consignee plays a longer clock, since three days give way to a full year for suit, and joint inspection at receipt excuses the writing. Westwind Shipping Corporation v. UCPB (G.R. Nos. 200289 and 200314, 2013) shows the related stipulation side, since a fifteen-day gate-pass claim term barred the arrastre claim there. Republic v. Lorenzo Shipping Corporation (G.R. No. 153563, 2005) with Unsworth Transport v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 166250, 2010) fix who receives and who sues, which is where every notice and prescription analysis must start.

| Regime | Notice Rule | Suit Period |
|---|---|---|
| Overland and coastwise under Art. 366 | Apparent damage at receipt, concealed damage within twenty-four hours from receipt, barred after lapse or freight payment without protest | Civil Code by action type with ten, six, and four years under Arts. 1144 to 1146 |
| International by sea under COGSA Sec. 3(6) | Apparent damage at receipt, concealed damage within three days, excused by joint survey, with mutual inspection facilities | One year from delivery or due date, unshortened by late notice |
| Subrogation claims | Insurer inherits shipper notice position from payment | Same clock as the shipper with consignee with bill holder |

**Q: A consignee receives crates by truck with intact exteriors, opens them after three days to find water damage, and complains on the fifth day. A second consignee receives container cargo by foreign vessel, joins the survey at receipt, then sues ten months after discharge. Which claims live?**

A: The truck claim dies on late notice under Art. 366, while the vessel claim reaches the merits because joint survey excused the writing and suit came inside the one-year period.
Under Art. 366 of the Code of Commerce, concealed damage found on opening must be claimed within twenty-four hours from receipt, apparent damage at receipt itself, and lapse of the period or payment of freight without protest bars the condition claim. Under New Zealand Insurance Co. v. Choa Joy (G.R. No. L-7311, 1955), Art. 366 applies only where goods were actually received, so total non-delivery needs no such notice. Under Sec. 3(6) of COGSA, concealed sea loss needs written notice within three days from delivery, joint survey at receipt excuses the writing, and suit must come within one year after delivery or the due date, which late notice never shortens, as Belgian Overseas Chartering v. Philippine First Insurance (G.R. No. 143133, 2002) holds.
Here, the consignee received crates by truck with intact exteriors, opened them after three days to find water damage, and complained on the fifth day, which is concealed damage notified beyond twenty-four hours. The second consignee received container cargo by foreign vessel, joined the survey at receipt, and sued ten months after discharge, which excuses the COGSA writing and sits inside the one-year suit period. Reading a fifth-day truck complaint as timely mistakes days for hours, while reading want of earlier shipper notice as fatal to the sea suit mistakes notice for prescription.
The first consignee therefore loses procedurally without reaching fault, while the second reaches the merits on loss with the carrier fault presumption, so only the vessel claim survives the bars.

**Q: An insurer pays the shipper in full eleven months after discharge, takes a subrogation receipt, and sues the foreign line in the twelfth month without any prior shipper notice. The line pleads prescription and want of notice. Does the suit prosper?**

A: Yes, the suit prospers past both bars, because the subrogated insurer inherits the shipper clock and files inside the one-year sea period that want of notice never shortens.
Under Compañia Maritima v. Insurance Company of North America (G.R. No. L-18965, 1964), the insurer that pays the shipper steps into the shipper shoes and sues the carrier as assignee by subrogation. Under Sec. 3(6) of COGSA, the international sea suit must be brought within one year after delivery or the date delivery was due, the one-year period binds shipper with consignee with bill holder with insurer alike, and failure to give notice of loss never affects the right to sue within that year, as Belgian Overseas Chartering v. Philippine First Insurance (G.R. No. 143133, 2002) holds.
Here, the insurer paid the shipper in full eleven months after discharge, took a subrogation receipt, and sued the foreign line in the twelfth month without any prior shipper notice. Payment with assignment puts the insurer in the shipper shoes rather than in the place of a stranger, and filing in the twelfth month satisfies the one-year period. Reading the insurer as a new claimant with its own clock mistakes subrogation for assignment of a fresh right, while reading want of notice as prescription mistakes a procedural aid for the suit period itself.
Prescription and want of notice therefore both fail as bars, so the suit reaches the merits where the insurer must still prove the loss with the carrier fault presumption behind it.

## 7. Prohibited and Limiting Stipulations with Purpose, Presumption, Reduced Diligence, Fixed Recovery, Riots and Strikes, and COGSA with Its Limits

> "A stipulation between the common carrier and the shipper or owner limiting the liability of the former for the loss, destruction, or deterioration of the goods to a degree less than extraordinary diligence shall be valid, provided it be: (1) In writing, signed by the shipper or owner; (2) Supported by a valuable consideration other than the service rendered by the common carrier; and (3) Reasonable, just and not contrary to public policy."
> — Art. 1744, Civil Code of the Philippines

> "Neither the carrier nor the ship shall in any event be or become liable for any loss or damage to or in connection with the transportation of goods in an amount exceeding $500 per package lawful money of the United States, or in case of goods not shipped in packages, per customary freight unit, or the equivalent of that sum in other currency, unless the nature and value of such goods have been declared by the shipper before the shipment and inserted in bill of lading. By agreement between the carrier, master or agent of the carrier, and the shipper another maximum amount than that mentioned in this paragraph may be fixed; Provided, That such maximum shall not be less than the figure above named. In no event shall the carrier be liable for more than the amount of damage actually sustained. Neither the carrier nor the ship shall be responsible in any event for loss or damage to or in connection with the transportation of the goods if the nature or value thereof has been knowingly and fraudulently misstated by the shipper in the bill of lading."
> — COGSA Sec. 4(5), as quoted in Belgian Overseas Chartering v. Philippine First Insurance (G.R. No. 143133, June 5, 2002)

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Purpose of limits | Agreed valuation lets the carrier price risk with rate, route, and guard decisions, so the shipper that declares low value pays low freight and recovers low loss |
| Three requisites | Signed writing with separate valuable consideration with reasonableness keeps a below-extraordinary limit valid under Art. 1744 |
| Presumption survives | Even with a valid limit, fault stays disputably presumed on loss under Art. 1752, so the carrier must still prove the agreed diligence |
| Void stipulations | Owner-risk terms, total exemptions, zero diligence, below-good-family care, employee-act freedom, ordinary-thief freedom, and defective-equipment excuses are void under Art. 1745 |
| Coerced agreement | A limit extracted by refusing carriage unless signed may be annulled by the shipper under Art. 1746 |
| Delay and deviation | Unjust delay or route change forfeits the limiting contract for the ensuing loss under Art. 1747 |
| Fixed recovery | Bill-value limits with declared-value option bind under Art. 1749, and fairly agreed reasonable fixed sums bind under Art. 1750 with no-competitor lines weighing reasonableness under Art. 1751 |
| Destination law | Liability for goods in transit follows the law of the destination country under Art. 1753 |
| Riots and strikes | Delay limits on account of strikes or riots are valid under Art. 1748, though only delay caused by the stoppage sits inside them |
| Passenger ban | Safety responsibility can never be dispensed with or lessened by stipulation, notices, or ticket statements under Art. 1757, save the gratuitous-rider negligence limit that never reaches wilful or gross fault under Art. 1758 |
| Employee ban | Employee responsibility can never be stipulated away under Art. 1760 |
| COGSA cap | Five hundred dollars per package or customary freight unit caps sea liability absent declared higher value in the bill, with agreed higher maxima allowed and fraudulent overstatement forfeiting all under Sec. 4(5) |
| Cap as bill term | The limitation rides inside every bill as though written there, binding accepting shippers and consignees to the declared maximum |
| When the cap cannot be invoked | No cap term in the bill, declared higher value inserted, delay or deviation forfeiting stipulations under Art. 1747, coerced assent annullable under Art. 1746, and knowing fraudulent misstatement by the shipper all defeat the invocation |

Stipulation law protects the bargain without selling the franchise. National Steel Corporation v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 112287, 1997) shows the free end, since private tramp parties may stipulate down to willful-negligence terms with Arts. 361 to 362 allocating risk. Lu Do and Lu Ym Corporation v. Binamira (G.R. No. L-9840, 1957) shows the valid middle, since a lawful customs-cutoff bill term bound the consignee once authorities took custody. Belgian Overseas Chartering v. Philippine First Insurance (G.R. No. 143133, 2002) shows the cap in operation, since the five-hundred-dollar limit supplements the Code where the bill carries no term and the shipper declares no higher value, while a cited letter of credit alone declares nothing. Commonwealth Act No. 65 of 1936, approved October 22, 1936, makes that whole scheme applicable to sea carriage to and from Philippine ports in foreign trade without repealing the Code of Commerce.

| Stipulation | Valid | Void |
|---|---|---|
| Agreed diligence below extraordinary with signed writing, separate consideration, and reasonableness | Yes under Art. 1744 | No where any requisite fails |
| Bill value with declared higher-value option and fixed reasonable sums freely agreed | Yes under Arts. 1749 to 1750 | No where coerced under Art. 1746 or forfeited by delay or deviation under Art. 1747 |
| Strike and riot delay limits | Yes under Art. 1748 for stoppage-caused delay | No beyond delay into loss from carrier fault |
| Owner-risk, total exemption, employee-act freedom, thief freedom, defective-equipment excuse | Never under Art. 1745 | Always void as contrary to public policy |
| Passenger safety waivers and employee responsibility waivers | Never under Arts. 1757 and 1760, save gratuitous-rider negligence limits | Always void against paying riders |
| COGSA five-hundred-dollar package cap | Yes absent higher declaration under Sec. 4(5) | No where higher value declared, no term carried, or invocation otherwise defeated |

**Q: A shipper signs a bill limiting recovery to invoice value for lower freight, then a strike delays the voyage for a week and the line deviates to an unscheduled port where theft guts the container. The line invokes the invoice cap and the strike clause. What survives?**

A: The strike clause stays valid for the week of stoppage delay, but the invoice cap cannot shelter the theft after the deviation, so the post-deviation loss recovers outside the limit.
Under Art. 1744 of the Civil Code, a limit below extraordinary diligence stands only with signed writing and separate valuable consideration and reasonableness, and a signed invoice-value limit for lower freight satisfies those requisites on its face. Under Art. 1747 of the Civil Code, unjust delay or route change forfeits the limiting contract for the ensuing loss. Under Art. 1748 of the Civil Code, delay limits on account of strikes or riots are valid, though only delay caused by the stoppage sits inside them, and valid bill terms bind only inside their own terms as Lu Do and Lu Ym Corporation v. Binamira (G.R. No. L-9840, 1957) shows.
Here, the shipper signed a bill limiting recovery to invoice value for lower freight, then a strike delayed the voyage for a week and the line deviated to an unscheduled port where theft gutted the container. The signed invoice limit with lower freight met the Art. 1744 face test for ordinary voyage loss, and the strike clause covers the week the stoppage itself delayed. Reading deviation as covered delay mistakes carrier-created routing for stoppage time, while reading the theft at the off-route port as stoppage loss ignores that the deviation forfeited the limit for what followed.
The line therefore keeps the strike-delay shelter for the week of stoppage but pays the theft after deviation outside the invoice cap, so the cap survives only for loss the deviation did not produce.

**Q: A foreign-going bill carries no package-limit term and the shipper declares no higher value, then ten packages land short by twenty thousand dollars. The line invokes the five-hundred-dollar cap per package. Does it hold?**

A: Yes, the cap holds and cuts recovery to five thousand dollars, because COGSA supplies the five-hundred-dollar package limit where the bill carries no term and the shipper declared no higher value.
Under Sec. 4(5) of COGSA, neither the carrier nor the ship shall be liable beyond five hundred dollars per package or customary freight unit unless the nature and value of the goods were declared by the shipper and inserted in the bill of lading. Under Belgian Overseas Chartering v. Philippine First Insurance (G.R. No. 143133, 2002), the limitation rides inside every foreign sea bill as though written there, binding accepting shippers and consignees to the declared maximum, while a letter of credit alone declares nothing. The section never pays beyond damage actually sustained, and knowing fraudulent overstatement forfeits all.
Here, the foreign-going bill carried no package-limit term, the shipper declared no higher value, and ten packages landed short by twenty thousand dollars. No higher declaration in the bill means no higher valuation binds, and no deviation or coercion attends the invocation on these facts. Reading silence in the bill as no limit mistakes omission for freedom, while reading actual loss above the cap as recoverable mistakes ceiling for floor.
The line therefore pays five hundred dollars per lost package with interest and costs as the law allows, so ten packages yield five thousand dollars and the excess above the cap goes unrecovered.

## Exam Traps

1. Goods defenses close on five causes while passenger defenses stay open on diligence, so an unlisted excuse never frees goods though rebuttal may free passengers.
2. The excepted cause must be proximate and only, since concurring carrier fault keeps liability in full.
3. Diligence must run before with during with after the calamity, since one stage never satisfies Art. 1739.
4. Negligent delay forfeits fortuity, since sailing into a forecasted storm is fault concurrent with the waves.
5. Fire is no Art. 1734 cause, so the carrier stays presumed at fault until extraordinary diligence is proved.
6. Eleven-mile winds are no storm, and a cracked hull meeting them proves unseaworthiness rather than calamity.
7. Worn treads and skipped inspections make blowouts and breakdowns carrier fault rather than fortuity.
8. Criminal acquittal of crew never kills the civil carrier suit, since contract liability stands on its own proof.
9. Pointing at stevedores or arrastre men never rebuts, since cargo duty is non-delegable.
10. Mere difficulty is no force majeure, since possible performance with unloading ashore defeats impossibility.
11. Common thieves are no public enemy, since Art. 1734(2) means organized force in war.
12. Acceptance of visibly defective packing never relieves by itself, since the carrier must still handle with fitting care and forestall under Art. 1742.
13. An illegal mayor order is no competent authority, since power with lawfulness with legal process must all be proved.
14. Switching from authority below to fortuity on appeal is barred as change of theory.
15. Employee fault binds beyond scope and against orders, with hiring diligence and ticket terms no defense.
16. The passenger need not investigate carrier fitness before boarding, since vigilance is non-delegable.
17. Stranger harm binds where preventable, so the carrier is diligent rather than an absolute insurer.
18. Victim fault merely contributing only reduces while carrier negligence stays proximate, and driver fault never travels to riders.
19. Art. 366 needs actual receipt, so total non-delivery never triggers the twenty-four-hour notice.
20. Joint survey excuses the COGSA writing, and late notice never shortens the one-year suit period that binds insurer with shipper alike.
21. A below-extraordinary limit needs signed writing with separate consideration with reasonableness, or it is void.
22. Coerced assent may be annulled while unjust delay or deviation forfeits the limit for the ensuing loss.
23. Strike clauses cover stoppage delay only, never theft at an off-route port.
24. The five-hundred-dollar cap rides in every foreign sea bill, yet declared higher value, deviation, and coercion defeat its invocation.
