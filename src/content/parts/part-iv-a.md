## 1. Competition and Importance; Constitutional Provisions; Policies and Rationale

> "The State shall regulate or prohibit monopolies when the public interest so requires. No combinations in restraint of trade or unfair competition shall be allowed."

> "The goals of the national economy are a more equitable distribution of opportunities, income, and wealth; a sustained increase in the amount of goods and services produced by the nation for the benefit of the people; and an expanding productivity as the key to raising the quality of life for all, especially the underprivileged. The State shall promote industrialization and full employment based on sound agricultural development and agrarian reform, through industries that make full and efficient use of human and natural resources, and which are competitive in both domestic and foreign markets. However, the State shall protect Filipino enterprises against unfair foreign competition and trade practices. In the pursuit of these goals, all sectors of the economy and all regions of the country shall be given optimum opportunity to develop. Private enterprises, including corporations, cooperatives, and similar collective organizations, shall be encouraged to broaden the base of their ownership."

> "The efficiency of market competition as a mechanism for allocating goods and services is a generally accepted precept. The State recognizes that past measures undertaken to liberalize key sectors in the economy need to be reinforced by measures that safeguard competitive conditions. The State also recognizes that the provision of equal opportunities to all promotes entrepreneurial spirit, encourages private investments, facilitates technology development and transfer and enhances resource productivity. Unencumbered market competition also serves the interest of consumers by allowing them to exercise their right of choice over goods and services offered in the market. Pursuant to the constitutional goals for the national economy to attain a more equitable distribution of opportunities, income, and wealth; a sustained increase in the amount of goods and services produced by the nation for the benefit of the people; and an expanding productivity as the key to raising the quality of life for all, especially the underprivileged and the constitutional mandate that the State shall regulate or prohibit monopolies when the public interest so requires and that no combinations in restraint of trade or unfair competition shall be allowed, the State shall: (a) Enhance economic efficiency and promote free and fair competition in trade, industry and all commercial economic activities, as well as establish a National Competition Policy to be implemented by the Government of the Republic of the Philippines and all of its political agencies as a whole; (b) Prevent economic concentration which will control the production, distribution, trade, or industry that will unduly stifle competition, lessen, manipulate or constrict the discipline of free markets; and (c) Penalize all forms of anti-competitive agreements, abuse of dominant position and anti-competitive mergers and acquisitions, with the objective of protecting consumer welfare and advancing domestic and international trade and economic development."

| Element or Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Competition and its importance | Competition allocates goods and services efficiently, disciplines liberalized sectors, spreads opportunity with investment and technology, and lets consumers choose freely among offers |
| Constitutional anchor | Section 19 of Article XII states the anti-trust principle of the Constitution and makes competition the underlying policy of the charter |
| Monopoly rule | The Constitution never bans every monopoly at once but commands regulation or prohibition when the public interest so requires |
| Combination rule | Every combination in restraint of trade and every unfair competition stands prohibited without need for a further showing of public interest |
| Goals of the national economy | Equitable distribution of opportunities, income, and wealth comes first, sustained increase of goods and services for the people comes second, and expanding productivity for the quality of life of all especially the underprivileged comes third |
| Goals of competition law | Economic efficiency with free and fair competition comes first, prevention of undue economic concentration comes second, and punishment of the three prohibited forms for consumer welfare comes third |
| Deregulation lesson | A deregulation law defeats itself when its tariff, inventory, and pricing rules build walls that only the entrenched oligopoly can climb |
| Old rule replaced | Article 186 of the Revised Penal Code once punished monopolies and combinations, and Section 55(a) of the Act now repeals it for later conduct |

Competition protects the process while the Constitution protects the nation, so the Act punishes conduct that closes markets and never punishes size alone. Goals of the economy state where the nation must go while goals of competition law state how markets must behave on the way there. A monopoly endures where public interest tolerates it while a combination in restraint of trade falls at once because the ban is absolute.

### Case: *Tatad v. Secretary of DOE*, G.R. No. 124360, November 5, 1997

Facts: Congress passed the Downstream Oil Industry Deregulation Act of 1996 with a three percent tariff on imported crude and seven percent on imported refined products, an inventory burden on new players, and a predatory pricing rule, while only Petron, Shell, and Caltex held refineries and storage. Petitioners assailed the tariff differential, the inventory and pricing rules, and the executive order that ordered full deregulation on a ground outside the statute.

Issue: The issue was whether the tariff differential, the inventory requirement, and the predatory pricing rule erect unconstitutional barriers to entry under Section 19 of Article XII and whether the whole deregulation law must fall with them.

Held: The Court held the whole law unconstitutional, because the three provisions favored the entrenched oligopoly and barred new entrants from a truly competitive market, the offending parts so permeated the statute that Congress could not have passed it without them, and the executive had rewritten the deregulation standards by adding the depletion of the stabilization fund.

Doctrine: Section 19 of Article XII is anti-trust in history and in spirit and makes competition its underlying principle. Laws that barricade entry into an oligopoly market must fall. Offending provisions that permeate the essence of a statute void the whole law despite a separability clause.

**Q: Congress deregulates the cement industry but keeps a prior-import-volume rule that only the two incumbent importers can meet, and no newcomer can sell a bag without five years of import records. A newcomer assails the rule as an entry barrier. Will the challenge prosper.**

A: Yes, the challenge will prosper and the prior-import-volume rule will be declared void because it perpetuates the two incumbents' control and makes competitive entry by the newcomer impossible despite formal deregulation.
Under 1987 Constitution Article XII Section 19 and R.A. No. 10667 Section 2, approved July 21, 2015, the State must regulate or prohibit monopolies when the public interest so requires and must preserve open entry, as applied in Tatad v. Secretary of DOE (G.R. No. 124360, November 5, 1997) where the Court voided the Downstream Oil Industry Deregulation Act because its three percent crude and seven percent refined tariff differential, inventory burden, and predatory pricing rule entrenched Petron, Shell, and Caltex.
Here, the cement deregulation law retains a five-year prior-import-volume requirement that only the two incumbent importers can satisfy so that no newcomer can sell even one bag, which replicates the Tatad tariff and inventory walls in cement form and cannot be defended as ordinary regulation because it measures fitness by past incumbency rather than present ability to compete safely and efficiently.
The rule must therefore be struck down and entry opened on lawful and even terms, leaving the State free to impose neutral safety, quality, and capacity standards but powerless to condition market access on an incumbency history that newcomers by definition cannot possess.

**Q: A mayor claims that the Constitution bans every monopoly so the city may seize the only ice plant in town without regulation. The owner answers that the plant charges fair prices and serves all buyers. May the city skip regulation and proceed to seizure.**

A: No, the city may not skip regulation and proceed to seizure because the Constitution does not ban every monopoly and the fair-priced and fully serving lone ice plant presents no prohibited monopoly on these facts.
Under 1987 Constitution Article XII Section 19, the State shall regulate or prohibit monopolies only when the public interest so requires while no combinations in restraint of trade or unfair competition shall ever be allowed, as explained in Tatad v. Secretary of DOE (G.R. No. 124360, November 5, 1997) and confirmed in Gios-Samar v. DOTC (G.R. No. 217158, March 12, 2019) where exclusivity alone was held not to constitute a monopoly.
Here, the mayor treats the mere existence of the only ice plant in town as a constitutional violation warranting immediate seizure, while the owner shows fair prices and service to all buyers with no combination in restraint of trade or unfair competition, so the facts establish at most a factual monopoly that the public-interest test may tolerate and regulate rather than a prohibited monopoly that the Constitution condemns outright.
The city must therefore apply neutral regulation first and may prohibit or expropriate only upon a statute, due process, and payment of just compensation if the public interest so requires, so the attempted shortcut to seizure without that foundation must be denied.

## 2. Methods to Deter Anti-competitive Conduct; Tools under the PCA

> "An entity that enters into any anti-competitive agreement as covered by Chapter III, Section 14(a) and 14(b) under this Act shall, for each and every violation, be penalized by imprisonment from two (2) to seven (7) years, and a fine of not less than fifty million pesos (P50,000,000.00) but not more than two hundred fifty million pesos (P250,000,000.00). The penalty of imprisonment shall be imposed upon the responsible officers, and directors of the entity. When the entities involved are juridical persons, the penalty of imprisonment shall be imposed on its officers, directors, or employees holding managerial positions, who are knowingly and willfully responsible for such violation."

> "In any investigation under Chapter III, Sections 14 and 15, and Chapter IV, Sections 17 and 20 of this Act, after due notice and hearing, the Commission shall impose the following schedule of administrative fines on any entity found to have violated the said sections: First offense: Fine of up to one hundred million pesos (P100,000,000.00); Second offense: Fine of not less than one hundred million pesos (P100,000,000.00) but not more than two hundred fifty million pesos (P250,000,000.00). In fixing the amount of the fine, the Commission shall have regard to both the gravity and the duration of the violation."

| Element or Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Criminal method | Section 14(a) and 14(b) agreements draw two to seven years with a P50,000,000 to P250,000,000 fine for each violation, imposed on knowing and willful officers, directors, and managerial employees |
| Administrative method | Sections 14, 15, 17, and 20 violations draw fines after notice and hearing up to P100,000,000 for the first offense and P100,000,000 to P250,000,000 for the second, scaled to gravity and duration |
| Enforcement aids | Refusal to obey a Commission order draws P50,000 to P2,000,000 per violation with daily accrual from the forty-fifth day, and knowingly false filings draw up to P1,000,000 |
| Private method | Any person directly injured may bring a separate and independent civil action after the Commission completes the preliminary inquiry under Section 31 |
| Leniency method | The first reporter before inquiry starts earns immunity on four conditions, and a later reporter earns leniency on three harder conditions with full and continuing cooperation always required |
| Nolo method | An accused entity may plead nolo contendere only up to arraignment and later only with court permission, and the plea never proves liability in the civil suit |
| Consent method | Binding rulings, show cause orders, and consent judgments let a willing entity comply, stop, pay within the fine range, compensate victims, and report compliance before any case is filed |
| Basic-necessities multiplier | Fines triple where the violation moves basic necessities and prime commodities under R.A. No. 7581 as amended |
| Prescription guard | Every action prescribes in five years from discovery for crimes and from accrual for administrative and civil cases |
| First pillar | Section 14 prohibits anti-competitive agreements among competitors with per se and effects-based rules |
| Second pillar | Section 15 prohibits abuse of dominant position through conduct that substantially prevents, restricts, or lessens competition |
| Third pillar | Sections 16 to 23 review mergers and acquisitions and prohibit those that substantially prevent, restrict, or lessen competition in the relevant market |

Crime punishes the cartel while the fine prices the violation, so the same agreement can send officers to prison and bill the entity at once. A private suit compensates the victim while leniency buys the evidence, so the State recovers money and truth together. Per se condemnation needs no market weighing while effects analysis weighs harm against efficiency, so the pillar invoked decides the proof required.

**Q: All four refiners agree to fix pump prices and rotate winning bids for government fuel. The finance manager who signed the minutes seeks immunity and tells everything after the inquiry has started. The Department of Justice charges the presidents. Who goes to jail and what happens to the manager.**

A: Yes, the presidents who knowingly and willfully joined the price-fixing and bid-rotation cartel face imprisonment and fine while the finance manager who disclosed only after the inquiry started cannot claim automatic immunity and keeps at best a chance at leniency.
Under R.A. No. 10667 Sections 14(a) and 14(b), Section 30, and Section 35, every per se price-fixing and bid-rigging agreement draws two to seven years imprisonment on responsible officers and a Fifty Million to Two Hundred Fifty Million Peso fine per violation, as the competition command explained in Tatad v. Secretary of DOE (G.R. No. 124360, November 5, 1997) condemns concerted closure of markets, while late disclosure earns only discretionary leniency for the first full and continuing cooperator.
Here, all four refiners fixed pump prices and rotated winning bids for government fuel through minutes the finance manager signed, so the agreement is complete upon concerted fixing and rotation without need to prove market effects, and the manager came only after the preliminary inquiry had started while the presidents acted knowingly and willfully, so belated candor cannot convert into pre-inquiry immunity that requires prompt exit and pre-inquiry reporting.
The presidents must therefore stand trial for the cartel with imprisonment and fine upon conviction for each violation, while the manager must seek leniency from the Commission and the Department of Justice on full cooperation without assurance, so prosecution proceeds against the leaders and mitigation alone remains for the late discloser.

**Q: A consumer group proves a flour mill abused its dominant position and asks the Commission for P80,000,000 on first offense plus damages for every baker overcharged. The mill argues one forum must choose one remedy. Must the Commission stop at the fine.**

A: No, the Commission need not stop at the fine because the administrative penalty for the State and the civil damages for the overcharged bakers are separate and cumulative remedies that proceed on different tracks after due process.
Under R.A. No. 10667 Section 29, Section 45, and Section 31, the Commission after notice and hearing may impose up to One Hundred Million Pesos on first offense guided by gravity and duration while any person directly injured may bring a separate civil action after the preliminary inquiry closes, a division confirmed in Gios-Samar v. DOTC (G.R. No. 217158, March 12, 2019) where the Court distinguished punishable abuse from mere position and demanded proof of ultimate facts.
Here, the consumer group proved abuse of dominant position by the flour mill and seeks Eighty Million Pesos within the first-offense ceiling plus full baker damages, while the mill wrongly reads singleness of forum into the law because the fine vindicates public competition and the civil suit repairs private overcharge, so one does not consume the other and triple fines apply if flour is a basic necessity under the pricing law.
The Commission must therefore impose the calibrated fine after notice and hearing and let the directly injured bakers file their independent damages action after the preliminary inquiry, so the mill pays the State for the violation and separately compensates victims for proven losses.

## 3. Scope of the PCA; Relevant Market Rule; Single Economic Unit Rule

> "This Act shall be enforceable against any person or entity engaged in any trade, industry and commerce in the Republic of the Philippines. It shall likewise be applicable to international trade having direct, substantial, and reasonably foreseeable effects in trade, industry, or commerce in the Republic of the Philippines, including those that result from acts done outside the Republic of the Philippines. This Act shall not apply to the combinations or activities of workers or employees nor to agreements or arrangements with their employers when such combinations, activities, agreements, or arrangements are designed solely to facilitate collective bargaining in respect of conditions of employment."

> "Relevant market refers to the market in which a particular good or service is sold and which is a combination of the relevant product market and the relevant geographic market, defined as follows: (1) A relevant product market comprises all those goods and/or services which are regarded as interchangeable or substitutable by the consumer or the customer, by reason of the goods and/or services' characteristics, their prices and their intended use; and (2) The relevant geographic market comprises the area in which the entity concerned is involved in the supply and demand of goods and services, in which the conditions of competition are sufficiently homogenous and which can be distinguished from neighboring areas because the conditions of competition are different in those areas."

> "For purposes of determining the relevant market, the following factors, among others, affecting the substitutability among goods or services constituting such market and the geographic area delineating the boundaries of the market shall be considered: (a) The possibilities of substituting the goods or services in question, with others of domestic or foreign origin, considering the technological possibilities, extent to which substitutes are available to consumers and time required for such substitution; (b) The cost of distribution of the good or service, its raw materials, its supplements and substitutes from other areas and abroad, considering freight, insurance, import duties and non-tariff restrictions; the restrictions imposed by economic agents or by their associations; and the time required to supply the market from those areas; (c) The cost and probability of users or consumers seeking other markets; and (d) National, local or international restrictions which limit access by users or consumers to alternate sources of supply or the access of suppliers to alternate consumers."

> "Dominant position refers to a position of economic strength that an entity or entities hold which makes it capable of controlling the relevant market independently from any or a combination of the following: competitors, customers, suppliers, or consumers;"

> "There shall be a rebuttable presumption of market dominant position if the market share of an entity in the relevant market is at least fifty percent (50%), unless a new market share threshold is determined by the Commission for that particular sector. The Commission shall not consider the acquiring, maintaining and increasing of market share through legitimate means not substantially preventing, restricting, or lessening competition in the market such as but not limited to having superior skills, rendering superior service, producing or distributing quality products, having business acumen, and the enjoyment and use of protected intellectual property rights as violative of this Act."

> "An entity that controls, is controlled by, or is under common control with another entity or entities, have common economic interests, and are not otherwise able to decide or act independently of each other, shall not be considered competitors for purposes of this section."

| Element or Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Covered person | Every person, natural or juridical, sole proprietorship, partnership, combination, or association in any form, domestic or foreign, including government owned or controlled entities in economic activity |
| Covered commerce | Every trade, industry, and commerce inside the Republic without sector exception |
| Foreign reach | International trade with direct, substantial, and reasonably foreseeable effects on Philippine trade, industry, or commerce, even from acts done abroad |
| Labor exception | Combinations and activities of workers or employees with employer deals solely for collective bargaining on employment conditions |
| Relevant product market | All goods or services the consumer regards as interchangeable or substitutable by characteristics, prices, and intended use |
| Relevant geographic market | The area of supply and demand where competition conditions stay sufficiently homogenous and differ from neighboring areas |
| Substitution factor | Technological possibility, availability of substitutes to consumers, and time needed to switch |
| Distribution-cost factor | Freight, insurance, duties, non-tariff limits, private restrictions, and time to supply from other areas |
| Switching factor | Cost and probability of users seeking other markets |
| Access-limit factor | National, local, or international limits on alternate supply and alternate customers |
| Control test | Control is presumed past one-half of voting power, and exists at or below one-half through voting pacts, direction of policy, board appointment or removal, majority of board votes, asset ownership or use, or decisive-rights contracts |
| Dominance test | Share with unilateral price or output power, barriers to entry, rival strength, input access, customer switching power, recent conduct, and other Commission criteria |
| Fifty percent presumption | Fifty percent share presumes dominant position subject to rebuttal and to a new sector threshold the Commission may set |
| Legitimate-means shield | Superior skill, service, product, business acumen, and intellectual property never count as violations by themselves |
| Single economic unit | Commonly controlled entities with common interests that cannot decide or act independently are never competitors under Section 14 |
| Conduct determination | The Commission defines the market, measures actual or potential substantial harm against efficiency gains, looks forward and backward, balances enforcement against overdeterrence, and weighs all evidence including reasonable commercial purpose |

Scope asks who must obey while market asks where competition happens, so foreign conduct obeys where local effects land and sister firms escape where independence is gone. Product likeness asks what buyers swap while geographic homogeneity asks where sellers truly meet, so island freight can split one product into two markets. Dominance presumes power from share while abuse proves misuse from conduct, so fifty percent opens the inquiry and never closes it.

### Case: *Gios-Samar v. DOTC*, G.R. No. 217158, March 12, 2019

Facts: The transport department bundled six regional airports into two bid bundles for thirty-year development, operation, and maintenance concessions under the build-operate-transfer law. A taxpayer group sued directly in the Supreme Court to stop the bidding and alleged monopoly, restraint of trade, dummy arrangements, and grave abuse of discretion without pleading market facts or impleaded corporate identities.

Issue: The issue was whether the bundling created a prohibited monopoly and combination in restraint of trade and whether the Court could decide those claims without trial-level proof of the relevant market and dominant position.

Held: The Court dismissed the petition, because constitutional and statutory monopoly claims need ultimate facts on the market, the position, and the abuse that only evidence can supply, an exclusive service concession is never a monopoly by itself, and direct resort to the Court fails where factual questions remain.

Doctrine: Hierarchy of courts binds even transcendental claims, and transcendental importance excuses standing defects but never supplies missing facts. The State punishes abuse of dominant position and never the position itself. Exclusivity inheres in a service concession and public bidding protects the public interest.

**Q: Two shipping lines serve the same island route and charge identical fares. A new entrant proves passengers treat the two lines as fully swappable but proves freight from the rival port costs twice as much with twice the sailing time. The lines claim one national market while the entrant claims an island market. Which market controls.**

A: Yes, the island market controls and the national-market claim fails because homogenous island conditions that differ materially from neighboring areas define a separate relevant geographic market even for an identical service.
Under R.A. No. 10667 Section 4(k) and Sections 24 to 27, the relevant market joins interchangeable products with a homogenous geography tested by substitution possibilities, distribution cost and time, user switching cost and probability, and access limits, as required in Gios-Samar v. DOTC (G.R. No. 217158, March 12, 2019) where the Court refused to find monopoly and restraint without pleaded and proven ultimate facts on market, position, and abuse.
Here, the two shipping lines serve the same island route with identical fares and passengers treat them as fully swappable so the product market is common, but freight from the rival port costs twice as much with twice the sailing time and users cannot practicably seek that distant port, so distribution cost, supply time, and switching probability split the geography and defeat the one-national-market reading.
The Commission must therefore define the island as the relevant geographic market and test dominance and abuse inside that boundary, so market share, power, and conduct are measured where competition actually occurs rather than diluted across a national area the island shipper cannot reach.

**2024 Bar Q: 3. Ernest Bicycle Corporation (EBC) and Max Speed Corporation (MSC) are the leading manufacturers and sellers of electric bicycles in the country. EBC and MSC offer their bestselling models at PHP 25,000.00 per unit. In 2023, Gryffin Pedals, Inc. (GPI) entered the market and sold electric bicycles with features similar to EBC and MSC's bestselling models for only PHP 20,000.00. GPI has no physical stores and offers its products in digital marketplaces. After a year, GPI had 75% share of the market leaving EBC and MSC at 15% and 10% shares, respectively. EBC and MSC filed a complaint before the Philippine Competition Commission and alleged that GPI is fast monopolizing the electric bicycles market because of its dominant position. Will the complaint against GPI for violation of the competition laws prosper? Explain.**

A: No, the complaint against Gryffin Pedals, Inc. will not prosper because its seventy-five percent share at most raises a rebuttable presumption of dominant position and the complainants prove no abusive conduct that substantially prevents, restricts, or lessens competition.
Under R.A. No. 10667 Section 27, Section 15, and Section 4(g), dominance at fifty percent share is only presumed and rebuttable while share won through superior skill, service, product, business acumen, or intellectual property is shielded and only abusive conduct is punished, as affirmed in Gios-Samar v. DOTC (G.R. No. 217158, March 12, 2019) where the Court held that position alone is never unlawful and that market, dominance, and abuse each require proof.
Here, Ernest Bicycle Corporation and Max Speed Corporation sold at Twenty-Five Thousand Pesos while Gryffin Pedals, Inc. sold similar-feature electric bicycles at Twenty Thousand Pesos through no-store digital marketplaces to reach seventy-five percent against fifteen and ten percent, which on these facts shows legitimate price rivalry and efficient distribution rather than below-cost predation or exclusion, so dominance even if presumed is explained by consumer choice and the high-share-equals-violation reading must be rejected.
The complaint must therefore be dismissed for failure to plead and prove the relevant product and geographic markets plus exclusionary abuse inside them, leaving Ernest and Max Speed free to refile only with ultimate facts of predatory or foreclosing conduct and leaving efficient low pricing that benefits consumers fully lawful.

## Exam Traps

1. Competition is the constitutional principle while monopoly is not banned per se, so public interest always mediates prohibition.
2. Combinations in restraint of trade fall at once while monopolies wait for the public-interest test.
3. Economy goals name three ends while competition goals name three market means, and examiners swap them to test recall.
4. Deregulation that builds entry walls defeats itself, so tariff, inventory, and pricing rules decide constitutionality.
5. A separability clause never saves a statute whose lifeblood provisions are void, since permeation beats severance.
6. Price fixing and bid rigging condemn per se while other agreements wait for effects weighing against efficiency gains.
7. Crime needs Section 14(a) or 14(b) only, so abuse and merger cases never send officers to prison.
8. Every fine needs prior notice and hearing, so ex parte punishment voids even a correct finding.
9. Gravity with duration prices every fine, so short and light violations never draw the ceiling.
10. Private suits wait for the preliminary inquiry to close, so a pending inquiry bars the independent action for now.
11. Leniency rewards the first and candid reporter, so second comers and silent leaders earn nothing.
12. Foreign acts answer locally only with direct, substantial, and reasonably foreseeable effects proved together.
13. Labor bargaining stands outside the Act only where employment conditions are its sole object.
14. Product likeness asks what buyers swap while geography asks where sellers meet, so freight can split one good into two markets.
15. Control looks past one-half votes to pacts, policy direction, board power, assets, and decisive rights.
16. Common control without independence ends rivalry, so sister firms never conspire with each other.
17. Fifty percent presumes dominance but never proves abuse, so share opens the case that conduct must close.
18. Skill, service, product, acumen, and intellectual property shield share, so efficiency never counts as abuse by itself.
19. Exclusive concessions never equal monopolies, since bidding itself guards the public interest.
20. Courts never find markets without evidence, so conclusions without ultimate facts state no cause of action.
