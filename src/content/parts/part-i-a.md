## 1. Definition of Insurance Contract

> "A contract of insurance is an agreement whereby one undertakes for a consideration to indemnify another against loss, damage or liability arising from an unknown or contingent event."
> — Sec. 2(a), Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified against LawPhil full text)

> "Any contingent or unknown event, whether past or future, which may damnify a person having an insurable interest, or create a liability against him, may be insured against, subject to the provisions of this chapter."
> — Sec. 3, par. 1, Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Agreement | Meeting of minds on the thing and cause; Code presupposes a valid contract |
| Consideration | The premium; without it there is no undertaking |
| Indemnity undertaking | One party answers for the other's loss, damage, or liability |
| Unknown or contingent event | Fortuity; past events unknown to the parties qualify (Sec. 3 covers past or future) |

Distinction: insurance vs wagering — insurance requires an insurable interest damnifiable by the event (Secs. 13 and 18). A wager manufactures risk for gain. Sec. 4 draws the line inside the Code:

> "The preceding section does not authorize an insurance for or against the drawing of any lottery, or for or against any chance or ticket in a lottery drawing a prize."
> — Sec. 4, Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

Complements: the working contrast is suretyship, deemed insurance only when the surety does an insurance business (Sec. 2(a), par. 2), developed in §8 with First Lepanto-Taisho.

**Q: X pays Y P10,000 so that Y will pay X P1M if a named ship, already lost at sea but unknown to both, failed to arrive. Is this a valid subject of insurance?**

A: Yes, the undertaking is a valid subject of insurance because both parties were ignorant of the loss when the contract was perfected. Section 3 covers past contingent events unknown to the parties. Both parties were ignorant of the loss at perfection, so fortuity subsists. The subject is therefore insurable, subject to insurable interest and the other requisites.

## 2. Principal Object and Purpose Test (HMO Product; Variable Contracts)

> "Doing or proposing to do any business in substance equivalent to any of the foregoing in a manner designed to evade the provisions of this Code."
> — Sec. 2(b)(4), Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified; anti-evasion anchor of the substance test)

| Product           | Test question                                                                                     | Result                                                                    |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| HMO health card   | Does the provider assume fortuitous risk for consideration, or merely sell access to services?    | Risk assumption = insurance business; pure service delivery = not         |
| Variable contract | Is the dominant object investment with insurance riders, or insurance with incidental investment? | Principal object decides; incidental features do not reclassify           |
| Pre-need plan     | Is the promise a fixed future service/benefit on a fixed schedule (Gaw)?                          | Pre-need: plan terms are law between the parties, not Sec. 2(a) indemnity |

### Case: Gaw v. CA (Pacific Plans, Inc.), G.R. No. 147748, April 19, 2006

Facts: The planholder's mother died. The family hired its own mortuary before notifying the pre-need provider. The provider refused memorial services.

Issue: Is the provider liable despite non-notification?

Held: No. The provider is not liable. The family breached the immediate-notice term, and the provider kept the exclusive right to arrange the services.

Doctrine: A pre-need plan is the law between the parties. Clear terms take their literal meaning, and courts cannot make new contracts for them. The provider's exclusive right to arrange services and the planholder's duty of immediate notice control the claim.

> Exam trap: Gaw is a contrast spine, not an insurance holding — never cite it for Sec. 2(a). Cite it to keep pre-need out of insurance.
> Correction to textbook: pre-need is now governed by the Pre-Need Code (RA 9829, 2009) under the Insurance Commission. Book language predating that regime must be read with RA 9829 in force.

**Q: An HMO sells cards guaranteeing doctor visits for a fixed fee, and it absorbs all costs even during epidemics. Is it doing an insurance business?**

A: Yes, the HMO is doing an insurance business in substance and is answerable to regulation. Under the Section 2(b) substance test, absorption of fortuitous cost for consideration is risk assumption. Here, epidemic overuse is the contingent event, and the fixed fee is the consideration. The HMO is therefore doing an insurance business in substance.

## 3. Doing an Insurance Business; Mutual Insurance Companies

> "The term doing an insurance business or transacting an insurance business, within the meaning of this Code, shall include: (1) Making or proposing to make, as insurer, any insurance contract; (2) Making or proposing to make, as surety, any contract of suretyship as a vocation and not as merely incidental to any other legitimate business or activity of the surety; (3) Doing any kind of business, including a reinsurance business, specifically recognized as constituting the doing of an insurance business within the meaning of this Code; (4) Doing or proposing to do any business in substance equivalent to any of the foregoing in a manner designed to evade the provisions of this Code."
> — Sec. 2(b), Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

> "In the application of the provisions of this Code, the fact that no profit is derived from the making of insurance contracts, agreements or transactions or that no separate or direct consideration is received therefor, shall not be deemed conclusive to show that the making thereof does not constitute the doing or transacting of an insurance business."
> — Sec. 2, no-profit sentence (RA 10607, verified)

| Act | Content |
|---|---|
| (1) Insurer contracts | Making or proposing any insurance contract as insurer |
| (2) Vocational suretyship | Surety contracts as a vocation, not merely incidental to another business |
| (3) Recognized business | Any business, including reinsurance, specifically recognized as insurance business |
| (4) Substance equivalent | Evasion-designed equivalents; catches relabeled schemes |

Mutual insurance companies are insurers owned by and for their member-policyholders rather than stockholders. They still transact Sec. 2(b) business and need the certificate of authority like any insurer — member ownership changes governance, not the license requirement.
### Case: Capital Insurance and Surety Co. v. Del Monte Motor Works, Inc., G.R. No. 159979, December 9, 2015

Facts: Del Monte sued its bus-body debtors on unpaid billings and attached buses, land, and bank funds. Debtor Maura Villegas posted CISCO counterbond No. 00011-00005/JCL(3) and the attachment was discharged. After final judgment for P11,835,375.50 enforceable against the counterbond, Capital disowned the bond — unaccounted in its records, signers capped at P5,000,000, witness signature allegedly forged, no premium paid — and resisted execution against its Insurance Commission security deposit under Sec. 203.

Issue: Is the counterbond binding, and is the Sec. 203 deposit leviable?

Held: The bond binds. The deposit does not answer.

Doctrine: the insurer that issued the bond cannot shed it by invoking internal limits unknown to the obligee. The employer of the agent bears the loss. Bare unproved allegations do not discharge the burden of proving invalid issuance. The Sec. 203 security deposit is a contingency fund for all policyholders. It is free from liens and exempt from levy by any single claimant. Each claimant's interest in it is a mere expectancy. The Commissioner as custodian rightly refuses garnishment.

> Exam trap: bond validity and deposit immunity run opposite ways — the insurer pays on the bond from its own funds, never from the Sec. 203 deposit. Letting one claimant levy the deposit creates an illicit preference over all other policyholders.

**Q: A civic club pools member contributions and pays fire losses of members, with no profit and no commission. Is it doing an insurance business?**

A: Yes, the club is doing an insurance business. Section 2(b)(1) governs, together with the no-profit sentence: absence of profit does not negate the business. Here, pooling contributions and paying members' fire losses is indemnity on a contingency, regardless of profit motive. The club therefore needs a certificate of authority, and unlicensed operation is punishable.

**Q: The policy is silent on when a mailed renewal binds. Which law answers?**

A: The Civil Code answers, through the cognition rule in Article 1319. The Civil Code applies suppletorily where the Insurance Code is silent. A mailed acceptance therefore binds only upon the offeror's knowledge. The renewal is effective when the insurer knew of the acceptance.

## 5. Importance of Insurance; Dealing with Risks

| Function | Content |
|---|---|
| Distribution of losses | Fortuitous losses of the few are spread across contributions of the many (element developed in §11) |
| Security for credit and commerce | Mortgagee clauses, TPL, bonds, and cargo cover let credit and carriage move |
| Dealing with risk | Avoidance, retention, transfer, control — insurance is transfer of fortuitous risk for a price |

How insurance deals with risk (TOC 6.01): the insurer prices fortuity by the law of large numbers, pools homogeneous exposures, and pays the stricken few from the common fund. The insured converts an uncertain large loss into a certain small premium.
State it as framework, never as a holding.

**Q: Why can the insurer pay P45M on one fire (cf. Cebu Shipyard) and stay solvent?**

A: The insurer stays solvent through pooling. Insurance rests on the distribution of losses across the pool. Here, the single large loss is funded by the premiums of the many exposures. Solvency therefore rests on spread, pricing, and reserves.

## 6. Characteristics (Indemnity; Not a Wagering Contract)

> "The measure of an insurable interest in property is the extent to which the insured might be damnified by loss or injury thereof."
> — Sec. 17, Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

| Characteristic | Content |
|---|---|
| Indemnity | Recovery capped at the actual loss; insured must not profit (property side; life is valued by agreement) |
| Aleatory | Performance depends on a contingent event; obligations are unequal until the event |
| Uberrimae fidei | Utmost good faith both ways (Qua Chee Gan binds the insurer more strictly) |
| Not wagering | Interest + Sec. 4 lottery bar; the contract answers a real exposure, not a manufactured chance |
| Personal in property | Tied to the insured's interest, so transfer rules (Secs. 20-23) suspend rather than assign freely |

### Case: Galian v. State Assurance Co., G.R. No. L-8405, February 10, 1915, 29 Phil. 413

Facts: Household effects insured for P3,000 but worth P4,512 suffered partial fire loss. The insurer cried fraud and objected to lay value testimony.

Issue: How is value proved, and what is the effect of the coinsurance clause?

Held: Lay testimony is admissible. There was no fraud. Liability was scaled to P2,919.92 under the coinsurance clause.

Doctrine: ordinary persons may testify on common household values. The ratable-proportion clause is valid. The insured is his own insurer for the excess. Partial loss equals actual loss times insured value over value at fire.

> Exam trap: coinsurance punishes underinsurance, not overinsurance — overinsurance triggers ratable return (Sec. 84) and void-for-fraud risk (United Merchants), a different machine.

**Q: A P5M building is insured for P3M and suffers a P1.5M partial fire loss under a 60 percent coinsurance clause. What is the recovery?**

A: Recovery is scaled down. The insured does not get the full P1.5M. Under Galian's ratable-proportion rule and the Section 17 measure, the insurer pays only the insured proportion of the loss. Here, the insured carried part of the risk by underinsuring the P5M building for P3M. The insurer therefore pays only the coinsured proportion of the loss.

## 7. Perfection (Requisites, Cognition, Offer/Acceptance, Unaccepted Application, Stipulation, Tort Liability, Delivery)

> "An acceptance made by letter or telegram does not bind the offerer except from the time it came to his knowledge. The contract, in such a case, is presumed to have been entered into in the place where the offer was made."
> — Civil Code Art. 1319, par. 2 (standard text; cognition rule)

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Requisites | Consent, object, cause — plus insurable interest and (for enforcement) premium exposure |
| Offer and acceptance | Application is the offer; company approval is the acceptance; acceptance must be absolute |
| Cognition theory | Mailed approval binds only when the applicant-offeror knows of it |
| Unaccepted application | No contract; premium paid is refundable; delay alone is not acceptance |
| Effect of stipulation | "Not binding until delivered/approved" clauses are honored as written |
| Tort liability | Inexcusable delay in acting on an application may sound in tort, not in contract |
| Delivery of policy | Policy is evidence, not the contract itself; perfection precedes the paper |

**2016 Bar Q: "Jason is the proud owner of a newly-built house worth PS million. As a protection against any possible loss or damage to his house, Jason applied for a fire insurance policy thereon with Shure Insurance Corporation (Shure) on October 11, 2016 and paid the premium in cash. It took the company a week to approve Jason's application. On October 18, 2016, Shure mailed the approved policy to Jason which the latter received five (5) days later. However, Jason's house had been razed by fire which transpired a day before his receipt of the approved policy. Jason filed a written claim with Shure under the insurance policy. Shure prays for the denial of the claim on the ground that the theory of cognition applies to contracts of insurance. Decide Jason's claim with reasons. (5%)"**

A: Jason's claim must fail on these facts because no contract had yet been perfected when the fire occurred.
Perfection needs absolute acceptance known to the offeror: mailed acceptance binds only from knowledge under Civil Code Article 1319, paragraph 2, and the policy here was still in transit at loss.
Approval was mailed October 18 and was received five days later. The fire came the day before receipt, so at loss no acceptance had reached Jason and no contract yet existed despite premium payment.
Shure is correct to deny. The premium is returnable. Delay alone neither perfects the contract nor, without inexcusable neglect proved, makes a tort case on these facts.

> Exam trap: premium payment is not acceptance — it evidences the offer and earns cover only once the contract is perfected and exposure begins (Sec. 77). Keep 2016-II apart from 2024-14, where a perfected policy plus agent receipt saved the claim.

### Case: Malayan Insurance v. Phil First Insurance (Wyeth / Reputable Forwarder), G.R. No. 184300, July 11, 2012

Facts: Two insurers covered cargo interests on the same shipment chain. Their cover overlapped on the same cargo interest.

Issue: How is liability coordinated between co-insurers over the same cargo interest?

Held: Liability was coordinated under double-insurance principles. Each insurer shares as the law provides for doubly covered interests.

Doctrine: where the same interest is doubly covered, insurers share as the law provides. Disclosure and the identity of subject, interest, and risk decide.
### Case: Multi-Ware Manufacturing Corp. v. Cibeles Insurance Corp., G.R. No. 230528, February 1, 2021

Facts: Multi-Ware held Western Guaranty fire Policy No. 50-118320 (P10,000,000 on machinery in Buildings 1-2) and Cibeles fire Policy No. 80-43032 (P7,000,000 on machinery, stocks, and materials in Building 1), then quietly took two more Prudential Guarantee fire policies on the same Building 1 machinery. After the April 21, 2000 fire, both first insurers denied for breach of Policy Condition No. 3 (other-insurance clause).

Issue: Does the clause reach machinery and equipment, and did non-disclosure forfeit benefits?

Held: Yes on both points. The clause reaches machinery and equipment. The non-disclosure forfeited the benefits.

Doctrine: property in the other-insurance clause is generic and reaches machinery and equipment. Undisclosed co-insurance on the same subject, interest, and risk forfeits all benefits. The clause prevents profitable over-insurance. Read with La O. Different interests (owner versus mortgagee) mean there is no breach.

Accident-coverage pointers (Batch-1 Nos. 10 to 12, one-line ratios from the Batch-1 Summary Table): in Biagtan (G.R. No. L-25579, 1972), the Court treated close-range bladed-weapon wounds are intentionally inflicted, so the accidental-death rider did not pay. In Sun (G.R. No. 92383, 1992), a self-fired shot from a gun the insured believed unloaded was held an accident, with needless peril requiring recklessness bordering on suicide. In Finman (G.R. No. 100970, 1992), murder by third persons was held accidental on the victim's part where the policy does not exclude it.
On the suretyship pair: in First Lepanto-Taisho (G.R. No. 177839, 2012), the surety bore no liability where the referenced written principal contract was never attached or shown. In Stronghold (G.R. No. 158820, 2009), the surety is released only by material alteration increasing its burden, and a bond predating the principal contract is not void.

> Exam trap: double insurance is lawful but never double recovery. "Prohibited by law" (2025-5 insurer's line) misstates it — the prohibition is on profit, enforced through triple identity plus the other-insurance clause.

**Q: Two fire policies cover the same building, with the owner holding one and the lessee holding the other, and both are disclosed. Is there a forfeiture?**

A: No, there is no forfeiture, and both policies stand. Forfeiture needs triple identity of subject, interest, and risk, and La O holds that different interests defeat the clause. Here, the owner's interest and the lessee's interest differ. Both policies therefore stand, and the loss is shared within each policy's measure.

### Case: La O v. Yek Tong Lin Fire and Marine, G.R. No. 33131, December 30, 1930

Facts: Stored tobacco burned. The policies were payable to a mortgagee bank. The insurer invoked undeclared other insurance and lessee-related warranties.

Issue: Who is the proper plaintiff, whether forfeiture attached, and how value is proved.

Held: the mortgagor-insured who contracted is the proper plaintiff. The other policies covered different interests, so there was no breach. The insurer knew the facts and still collected premiums, so it waived the defense.

Doctrine: the contracting insured sues. The other-insurance condition serves the insurer and needs the same subject matter and interest. Knowledge plus premium collection equals waiver. Cost price proves value.

> Exam trap: Sec. 8 keeps the mortgagor as party — loss "payable to the mortgagee" is a payment direction, not a substitution. The mortgagor's pre-loss avoiding acts still avoid. Sec. 9 is the escape: a new assented contract with fresh obligations cuts the mortgagor's acts off.

### Case: Palermo v. Pyramid Insurance, G.R. No. L-36480, May 31, 1988

Facts: The insured himself was driving when the car was damaged. His license had expired at the time.

Issue: Does the expired license of the insured himself defeat cover under the authorized-driver clause?

Held: The claim is covered. The insurer must pay.

Doctrine: the insured is himself an authorized driver. The licensing proviso binds only persons driving on his order or with his permission. Breach of motor-vehicle law alone does not bar recovery.

### Case: Shafer v. Judge, RTC Olongapo, G.R. No. L-78848, November 14, 1988

Facts: The insured faced a reckless-imprudence prosecution. He moved to implead his TPL insurer as third-party defendant.

Issue: May the insured implead the TPL insurer at once without awaiting judgment?

Held: Yes. The third-party complaint is allowed.

Doctrine: TPL liability accrues on injury without awaiting judgment. The victim may sue the insurer directly. No-action clauses yield to the rule against multiplicity of suits. Companion: Tiu v. Arriesgado (G.R. No. 138060, 2004). The impleaded carrier's insurer was held liable with the carrier.

Batch-1 pointers (Nos. 13 and 15, one-line ratios from the Batch-1 Summary Table): in Villacorta (G.R. No. L-54171, 1980), a repair-shop employee entrusted with the car and key is an authorized driver for legitimate purposes, and the unlawful joy-ride taking is theft. In Perla (G.R. No. 96452, 1992), carnapping is governed by the theft clause, so an expired license is no defense to a theft claim. The chattel mortgage answers the note despite the loss, and sham denial of a valid claim is bad faith.

**2025 Bar Q (Q8): "A was a rich businessperson with two wives. He married B, with whom he had three children namely, C, D, and E. While their marriage was subsisting, he met X with whom he madly fell in love. He also married her despite his first marriage. They had a child named Y. In his life insurance policy from XYZ Life Insurance Corp., A designated his second wife, X, and their son, Y, as beneficiaries. Five years later, A died. X filed a claim for the proceeds of the policy for herself and for Y as the designated beneficiaries. At the same time, B also filed a claim for death benefits for herself and her children, C, D, and E, as A's legal wife and legitimate children. Who is/are entitled to the life insurance proceeds? Reason(s)."**

A: Y takes the proceeds while X does not, and B, C, D, and E take only outside the policy through the estate, not as beneficiaries.
Section 11 makes the designation revocable, and the disqualification of illicit-relation beneficiaries extends to the bigamous spouse, while the innocent child keeps an insurable interest in the parent's life.
X's designation is void as fruit of a bigamous union. Y, though illegitimate, holds a Sec. 10 interest and a valid designation. The legal family was never designated and claims only by succession.
The proceeds therefore go to Y (and any other valid co-beneficiaries), never to X.

## 10. The Policy (Form, Basic Provisions, Identification, Adhesion, Interpretation, Indivisibility, Proof, Riders, Cover Notes, Renewal, Reformation)

Section 50 requires seven specifications in substance (standard text). The policy must specify (a) the parties. It must state (b) the amount insured except in open or running policies. It must state (c) the premium. It must identify (d) the property or life insured. It must state (e) the interest of the insured. It must state (f) the risks insured against. Finally, it must state (g) the period of coverage.

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Form; basic provisions | Printed form approved by the Commissioner; Sec. 50 contents mandatory |
| Identification of insured and property | Misidentification fatal only if material; error in name where identity is certain does not avoid |
| Adhesion | Take-it-or-leave-it; construed against the insurer, for the weaker party (Qua Chee Gan; Villacorta; Perla theft-clause limit) |
| Interpretation | Plain meaning first; obscurity against the drafter (CC 1377); riders and endorsements read with the policy |
| Indivisibility | One unit, one premium = indivisible (Oriental); severable only on contrary intent |
| Proof | Policy is the best evidence; loss may still be proved where the policy is lost, on secondary proof |
| Riders | Attached, referred-to, and premium-charged riders become part of the contract |
| Cover notes | Short-term binders pending policy issuance; bind within their stated period and terms |
| Renewal | A new contract on old terms unless varied; premium and acceptance rules rerun |
| Reformation | Instrument reformed to express true intent on clear proof of mistake, fraud, or inequitable conduct |

### Case: Cebu Shipyard v. William Lines, G.R. No. 132607, May 5, 1999

Facts: M/V Manila City burned while docked for repairs under the repairer's exclusive control. The vessel insurer paid P45M and sued the repairer, which invoked a P1M liability cap and co-assurance.

Held: Negligence was found under res ipsa. The insurer was subrogated under Art. 2207. The cap could not shield the repairer from the insurer.

Doctrine: exclusive custody triggers res ipsa. Co-assurance must appear in the policy itself. Unconscionable adhesion stipulations may be disregarded in equity.

### Case: Oriental Assurance v. CA (Panama Sawmill), G.R. No. 94052, August 9, 1991

Facts: 2,000 cubic meters of logs on two barges under a total-loss-only policy. One barge sank (about 41 percent lost). Constructive total loss was claimed.

Held: There is no recovery. The claim for constructive total loss fails on a total-loss-only indivisible policy.

Doctrine: one valuation and one premium make the contract indivisible. Constructive total loss (Sec. 139) needs separate valuation or insurance of the abandoned portion. Under total-loss-only wording, partial loss does not pay.

### Case: Qua Chee Gan v. Law Union and Rock Insurance Co., G.R. No. L-4611, December 17, 1955, 98 Phil. 85

Facts: copra-and-hemp merchant Qua Chee Gan held eight fire policies (P370,000) on Albay bodegas, a hemp press, and contents, loss payable to PNB as mortgagee. After the July 1940 fire gutted Bodegas 1, 2, and 4, the insurer refused payment — breach of the 1939 hydrant, hose, pressure, and brigade warranty riders, gasoline storage, refusal of records, fraudulent claims, and arson (the insured stood acquitted in 1941).

Issue: Did waiver and estoppel, the warranty dispute, and the fraud and arson defenses defeat the claim?

Held: The insurer pays. The defenses fail and the claim stands.

Doctrine: the insurer that issues policies knowing the shortfall against its own warranty, and collects premiums, it waives the condition and is estopped from asserting breach. Insurance is uberrimae fidei on both sides, and more strictly on the insurer. Adhesion ambiguities construe against the insurer to avoid forfeiture. Incidental trade storage is no breach, and only wilful false swearing avoids. (Caption note: the syllabus reads Que, while the official caption reads Qua.)

> Exam trap: adhesion protects the insured on ambiguity, never against clear terms — Gaw's literal-terms rule is the counterweight. Cebu Shipyard's equity disregard needs unconscionability, not mere one-sidedness.

**Q: A policy insures "Building A", and the insurer denies a claim on Building B by invoking an unsigned, unattached, and unreferred-to endorsement. Does that endorsement form part of the contract?**

A: The endorsement is no part of the contract. Riders must be attached to the policy, referred to in it, and supported by premium, and ambiguities are construed against the insurer. Here, the unsigned, unattached, and unmentioned endorsement binds no one. The Building A claim therefore stands on the policy as written.

## 11. Elements (Distribution of Losses; Risk; Insurable Interest)

> "Every person has an insurable interest in the life and health: (a) Of himself, of his spouse and of his children; (b) Of any person on whom he depends wholly or in part for education or support, or in whom he has a pecuniary interest; (c) Of any person under a legal obligation to him for the payment of money, or respecting property or services, of which death or illness might delay or prevent the performance; and (d) Of any person upon whose life any estate or interest vested in him depends."

> "Every interest in property, whether real or personal, or any relation thereto, or liability in respect thereof, of such nature that a contemplated peril might directly damnify the insured, is an insurable interest."
> — Sec. 13, Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

> "An insurable interest in property may consist in: (a) An existing interest; (b) An inchoate interest founded on an existing interest; or (c) An expectancy, coupled with an existing interest in that out of which the expectancy arises."
> — Sec. 14, Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

> "A carrier or depository of any kind has an insurable interest in a thing held by him as such, to the extent of his liability but not to exceed the value thereof."
> — Sec. 15, Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

> "A mere contingent or expectant interest in any thing, not founded on an actual right to the thing, nor upon any valid contract for it, is not insurable."

> "No contract or policy of insurance on property shall be enforceable except for the benefit of some person having an insurable interest in the property insured."
> — Sec. 18, Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

> "An interest in property insured must exist when the insurance takes effect, and when the loss occurs, but need not exist in the meantime; and interest in the life or health of a person insured must exist when the insurance takes effect, but need not exist thereafter or when the loss occurs."

> — Sec. 20, Insurance Code (RA 10607, verified)

|---|---|
| Distribution of losses | Pooling across homogeneous exposures; the economic base of §§5-6 |
| Risk | The contingent or unknown event (Sec. 3); must be fortuitous — inevitable loss and peril of the ship (Go Tiaoco) are not risks |

**2019 Bar Q: "(c) Insurable interest in property (2%)".**

A: Insurable interest in property is any interest in property, whether real or personal, or any relation thereto, or liability in respect thereof, of such nature that a contemplated peril might directly damnify the insured.
The governing provisions are Section 13 for the definition, Section 18 requiring that the policy be enforceable only for one holding such interest, Section 19 fixing the time at both effect and loss, and Section 17 fixing the measure at damnification.
The question asks for the bare definition, which the Section 13 sentence supplies.
A complete answer therefore recites the Section 13 definition together with the Section 19 timing and the Section 17 measure.

**2017 Bar Q (XI(e)): "(e) The law on life insurance prohibits double insurance. (2%)".**

A: No, the statement is false: the law does not prohibit double insurance. Double insurance is lawful. Recovery is capped at the loss with contribution among insurers, and life policies, being valued, routinely stand in multiples. Applied to the proposition, multiple policies are valid and the only bar is on double recovery. The statement is therefore false.

**2017 Bar Q (I.B): "The newly restored Ford Mustang muscle car was just released from the car restoration shop to its owner, Seth, an avid sportsman. Given his passion for sailing, he needed to go to a round-the-world voyage with his crew on his brand-new 180-meter yacht. Hearing about his coming voyage, Sean, his bosom friend, asked Seth if he could borrow the car for his next roadshow. Sean, who had been in the business of holding motor shows and promotions, proposed to display the restored car of Seth in major cities of the country. Seth agreed and lent the Ford Mustang to Sean. Seth further expressly allowed Sean to use the car even for his own purposes on special occasions during his absence from the country. Seth and Sean then went together to Bayad Agad Insurance Co. (BAIC) to get separate policies for the car in their respective names. SAIC consults you as its lawyer on whether separate policies could be issued to Seth and Sean in respect of the same car. (a) What is insurable interest? (2%) (b) Do Seth and Sean have separate insurable interests? Explain Briefly your answer(3%)"**

A: (a) Insurable interest is the Section 13 interest. (b) Yes, both Seth and Sean hold separate insurable interests. Sections 13 and 14 extend property interest to any damnifiable relation to the thing, Section 19 fixes the timing, and La O recognizes the separate-interest rule. Seth owns (existing interest). Sean holds contractual custody plus use with liability exposure (inchoate/expectancy coupled with existing custody interest). Different interests, so there is no other-insurance breach. Separate policies may therefore issue to each, and each recovers within his own damnification.

**2025 Bar Q (Q5): "X obtained an insurance policy for her Porsche Carrera GT from XYZ Insurance Company. The same car was insured by her creditor-mortgagee, Y, with ABC Insurance Company. When the car was damaged because of an accident, X filed a claim with XYZ Insurance Company. The latter denied the claim since it discovered that the car was also insured with ABC Insurance Company. According to XYZ Insurance Company, double insurance is prohibited by law. Decide with reason(s)."**

A: The denial is wrong, and X recovers on her policy. The owner and the creditor-mortgagee hold distinct insurable interests (Secs. 8 and 13-14 and La O). Double insurance is not prohibited — only double recovery is. X's policy answers her ownership interest, and Y's answers the credit interest. Different interests defeat triple identity and forfeiture. XYZ must therefore pay X within her loss, leaving contribution rules to sort the insurers.

**2023 Bar Q: "Emilio owns a car which he purchased from Gregorio. Emilio agreed to pay Gregorio the balance of the purchase price in the amount of P500,000 within one year. To secure the payment of the balance, Emilio and Gregorio executed a security agreement that complies with the requirements of the Personal Property Security Act (PPSA). The security interest was also perfected in accordance with the PPSA. Who has insurable interest over the car? Explain your answer."**

A: Both Emilio and Gregorio hold insurable interest over the car.
Section 13's property interest covers any damnifiable relation to the thing. Section 14 reaches existing interests and expectancies coupled with an existing interest. A perfected PPSA security interest is such a relation.
Emilio as buyer-owner holds the existing ownership interest. Gregorio as perfected secured creditor holds an interest to the extent of the P500,000 collectible from the car.
Either or both may therefore insure, each within its own measure (Sec. 17), and two policies on different interests are no double-insurance breach (La O and 2025-5).

> Exam trap: Sec. 19 asymmetry — life interest tested once (effect), and property twice (effect and loss). Sec. 20 suspension is not avoidance — reunite thing and policy in one person and cover revives.

## 12. Premium (Accrual, Provision, Forms, Non-Payment, Check, Binding Despite Unpaid, Lapse Prevention, Return, Rebate)

Section 77 provides in substance (standard text) that the insurer earns premium as soon as the thing insured is exposed to the peril. Actual payment follows the policy's time and manner. Secs. 78-81 fix due dates, earned portions, and over-insurance. Secs. 82-83 return premium on voidable annulment, insurer fraud, or no-liability-without-actual-fraud. Sec. 84 gives ratable return on over-insurance (also carried in Part I-B R1).

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Accrual | Earned on exposure, not on paper issuance |
| Policy provision | Time, manner, grace, and default clauses bind as written |
| Forms | Cash, check (conditional until honored), offsets, agent receipt |
| Non-payment | General rule: no premium, no cover — the contract never attaches |
| Check | Conditional payment; dishonor retroactively erases payment unless the insurer waived |
| Binding despite unpaid | Statutory or stipulated binds: agent's binding receipt, credit agreement, estoppel by knowing collection (La O; Qua Chee Gan), TPL immediacy (Shafer) |
| Lapse prevention (life) | Grace period, automatic premium loans, reduced paid-up, reinstatement — keeps Sec. 48 clocks and values alive |
| Return | Secs. 82-84: voidable annulment, insurer fraud, no liability without actual fraud, ratable over-insurance return |
| Rebate | Prohibited discrimination: no premium rebates or special favors as inducement |

**2024 Bar Q: "Flavio obtained from Safe Assurance Corporation (SAC), through its agent Laureen, a fire insurance policy over his residential house for a period of one year from August 25, 2024 to August 24, 2025. Flavio delivered on August 26, 2024 a check representing premium payment to Laureen who then issued an acknowledgment receipt. On August 27, 2024, a fire completely razed Flavio's house. Oblivious of the incident, Laureen deposited the check on August 28, 2024 to SAC's bank account and was credited on the same day. Thereafter, Flavio filed against SAC an insurance claim. However, SAC denied the claim and argued that there was no payment of premium yet to make the insurance policy effective at the time of the loss. SAC explained that it received the premium payment only on August 28, 2024 or after the fire occurred on August 27, 2024. Is there a valid and binding insurance contract between SAC and Flavio? Explain."**

A: Yes, a valid and binding insurance contract exists between SAC and Flavio, and SAC must pay the claim.
A premium delivered to the authorized agent with an acknowledgment receipt is payment to the insurer. Cover attaches upon perfection plus exposure within the policy period. Later bank crediting does not move the payment date back.
Flavio paid by check to Laureen on August 26, inside the August 25, 2024 to August 24, 2025 term, and the receipt placed payment before the August 27 fire, which fell within cover. The August 28 deposit is merely internal timing.
The denial therefore fails: the contract is valid and the loss is covered.

> Exam trap: 2024-14 (agent receipt before loss) vs 2016-II (no perfected contract at loss) — same "unpaid at fire" smell, opposite outcomes. Perfection plus payment-to-agent is the divider. And La O/Qua Chee Gan waiver needs knowledge before collection — post-loss knowledge waives nothing.

## Exam Traps

1. Sec. 2(a) defines the contract. Sec. 3 tells what may be insured — never swap the numbers.
2. Sec. 2(b) has four acts plus the no-profit sentence. Non-profit pooling is still the business.
3. The old Code's Sec. 2 became RA 10607's Sec. 2(a)-(c) with lettered subsections — cite 2(a)/2(b), not bare "Sec. 2" alone.
4. Sec. 6 is who may insure, Sec. 7 who may be insured, Secs. 8-9 the mortgagee relation — the mortgagor cluster moved down from the numbers memory assigns.
5. Sec. 18 (enforceable only for interest-holders) is not the time rule. Sec. 19 is the time rule.
6. Sec. 19 asymmetry: life once (effect), property twice (effect and loss).
7. Sec. 20 suspends, never avoids — reunited ownership revives without a new contract.
8. Loss "payable to the mortgagee" (Sec. 8) directs payment. It does not make the mortgagee the insured.
9. Sec. 9's new-contract assent is the only clean cut of the mortgagor's avoiding acts.
10. Revocable is the default (Sec. 11). "Irrevocable" needs express waiver or lifetime silence.
11. Sec. 12 forfeiture needs willful killing as principal, accomplice, or accessory — negligence is not forfeiture.
12. 2025-8: void as to the bigamous spouse, valid as to the innocent child — never void the whole designation.
13. Agent knowledge is insurer knowledge (2023 Q10). Broker knowledge is not.
14. Agent receipt before loss is payment (2024-14). Crediting date is internal timing.
15. 2016-II vs 2024-14: both smell "unpaid at fire" — perfection plus payment-to-agent divides them.
16. Double insurance is lawful. Double recovery is not — "prohibited by law" misstates the rule (2025-5).
17. Coinsurance (Galian) scales underinsurance. Overinsurance returns ratably (Sec. 84) — different machines.
18. Adhesion construes ambiguity against the insurer, never clear terms against the parties' intent (Gaw counterweight).
19. PPSA perfection does not merge interests: debtor-owner and secured creditor each insure within their own measure, and two policies on different interests are no breach (2023-9 and La O).
20. Accident trio: Biagtan (intentionally inflicted by another) is excluded, while Sun and Finman (unforeseen on the victim's part) are covered — victim-side fortuity plus express exclusion is the divider.
