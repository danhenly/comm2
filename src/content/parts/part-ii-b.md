## 2. Learning Objectives

1. State the duty to accept with its lawful grounds for refusal and apply it to dangerous goods, unfit packing, tenders beyond capacity, and live animals.
2. Run delivery problems on delay, place, and recipient, including bill-of-lading surrender, forged bills, consignee refusal, and damaged goods.
3. Apply the passenger duty on delay and on children, the aged, the ill, and persons with disability.
4. Recite the diligence standard with its rationale, non-delegable character, presumptions of fault, duration for goods, and commencement for passengers.
5. Test seaworthiness, cargoworthiness, manning, stowage, route, and transshipment on sea facts, and decide when the abandonment privilege is lost.
6. Test roadworthiness, crew selection, train operation, and crossing warnings on land facts without imputing driver fault to passengers.
7. Sort checked-in baggage from hand-carried baggage and assign the burden of proof for each kind.
8. Price freight, time its payment, enforce the carrier lien, and charge demurrage while holding shippers and passengers to their own duties.

## 1. Duty to Accept Goods; Grounds for Refusal; Animals

> "Common carriers are persons, corporations, firms or associations engaged in the business of carrying or transporting passengers or goods or both, by land, water, or air, for compensation, offering their services to the public."
> — Art. 1732, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Public duty to serve | Holding out to the public carries a duty to accept all goods duly tendered, since refusal without lawful ground breaches the public-service undertaking |
| Within route and means | The tender must lie on the carrier route and within its capacity, schedule, and equipment, since no carrier must take what it cannot carry |
| Proper condition | Goods must be packed, marked, and fit for the contracted voyage, since unfit tender excuses acceptance until cured |
| Lawful cargo only | Contraband, smuggled, or illegally traded goods need never be accepted, since no contract can compel an unlawful act |
| Dangerous goods | Explosives, acids, and like hazards may be refused unless the carrier knowingly engages for them with full disclosure and safeguards |
| Agreement effect | A charter, booking, or tariff may fix the cargo, volume, and voyage, and tender outside its terms is no tender under the contract |
| Animals | Live animals must be taken where the carrier holds out for them, with diligence measured by their nature and with Art. 1734(4) character of goods answering for inherent vice |

The duty to accept is the other face of the Art. 1732 holding-out. A private carrier under special contract, such as the time-chartered vessel serving select charterers in National Steel Corporation v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 112287, 1997) or the customer-controlled trucks in U.S. v. Tan Piaco (G.R. No. L-15122, 1920), takes only what its contract names. The common carrier takes all comers within route and means, and its refusal is judged against the lawful grounds above rather than against its convenience.

| Refuse Lawfully | Accept Despite Cost |
|---|---|
| Dangerous goods tendered without disclosure | Ordinary goods tendered in fit packing within capacity |
| Contraband or unlawful cargo | Lawful cargo with permits obtainable by the shipper |
| Tender beyond route, schedule, or means | Tender inside the advertised run though space is tight |
| Unfit packing that endangers the voyage | Sound packing that merely needs ordinary care |

**Q: A shipper tenders drums of nitric acid in brittle crates for a Friday sailing, plus fighting cocks in flimsy coops. The carrier refuses both. A second shipper tenders bagged fertilizer in sound sacks for the same sailing and is also refused for lack of space though room can be made. Which refusals stand?**

A: The acid and the cocks refusals stand while the fertilizer refusal fails, since lawful ground excuses while convenience never does.
The acid is dangerous cargo in unfit packing and the coops are unfit for live carriage, so both tenders fall outside what the carrier must take until disclosure, safeguards, and fit containers cure them.
The fertilizer is ordinary cargo in sound packing within the advertised run, so tight space alone is no lawful ground where the carrier holds itself out for such goods.
The carrier therefore answers for the fertilizer refusal and keeps its refusal of the acid and the birds until proper tender is made.

## 2. Timely Delivery; Place of Delivery; To Whom Delivered

> "The extraordinary responsibility of the common carrier lasts from the time the goods are unconditionally placed in the possession of, and received by the carrier for transportation until the same are delivered, actually or constructively, by the carrier to the consignee, or to the person who has a right to receive them, without prejudice to the provisions of article 1738."
> — Art. 1736, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Timely transport | Goods must move and arrive without unreasonable delay, since delay is itself a breach that sounds in damages |
| Negligent delay and calamity | Delay through carrier fault keeps liability alive though a natural disaster later strikes, under Art. 1740 |
| Place of delivery | Delivery belongs at the destination named in the contract or bill, including the carrier warehouse where Art. 1738 keeps liability alive until advice of arrival with reasonable opportunity to remove |
| Recipient | Delivery belongs to the consignee named in the bill or to the person with the right to receive them |
| Bill surrender | The carrier should demand the original bill, yet signed delivery receipts by the entitled recipient produce the same discharging effect, as Republic v. Lorenzo Shipping (G.R. No. 153563, 2005) holds |
| Forged bill | Release to a forger is no delivery at all, since the forger never held the right to receive |
| Refusal by consignee | Refusal leaves custody with the carrier, which must store, notify the shipper, and seek disposition rather than abandon the goods |
| Damaged goods | The consignee should claim promptly with survey and protest, since unexplained acceptance weakens the later claim though it never erases proved carrier fault |

Delivery is where custody ends and where proof matters most. Compañia Maritima v. Insurance Company of North America (G.R. No. L-18965, 1964) shows receipt starting responsibility even through a carrier lighter, while Westwind Shipping v. UCPB (G.R. Nos. 200289 and 200314, 2013) shows good-order receipt with bad-order delivery raising the fault presumption. Unsworth Transport v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 166250, 2010) adds that the forwarder issuing its own bill undertakes this very delivery duty as carrier.

### Case: Republic v. Lorenzo Shipping Corporation, G.R. No. 153563, February 7, 2005

Facts: Donated milk moved under government carriage through the respondent line to Zamboanga. The branch supervisor named as consignee showed only certified copies of the bills, signed or caused the signing of delivery receipts on each delivery, then resigned and vanished.

Issue: The question was whether the carrier was discharged though the original bills were never surrendered.

Held: The Court held that it was. The agents demanded the bills, followed standard procedure, and took signed receipts from the entitled recipient, which proved extraordinary diligence and substantial compliance.

Doctrine: Under Art. 353 of the Code of Commerce, acknowledgment of delivery by signed receipt produces the same discharging effect as return of the bill where the consignee cannot produce the original.

### Case: Lu Do and Lu Ym Corporation v. I.V. Binamira, G.R. No. L-9840, April 22, 1957

Facts: Films shipped from New York arrived in Cebu in good order and passed into the custody of the customs arrastre operator. The consignee took them three days later and found pilferage.

Issue: The question was whether the carrier answered for loss suffered after discharge into customs custody under a bill that ended responsibility at that point.

Held: The Court held that it did not. The goods left carrier control in good order, and the loss fell inside the customs interregimum the parties had validly allocated.

Doctrine: Responsibility under Arts. 1734 to 1736 lasts only while the goods sit in carrier possession, and a bill stipulation ending liability once customs or other authorities take custody is valid where it offends no law, morals, or public policy.

**Q: A consignee lost the original bills and presents certified copies, then signs delivery receipts for each lot through his clerks. Months later the shipper sues the carrier for non-delivery, blaming the missing originals. Is the carrier discharged?**

A: The carrier is discharged, since signed receipts by the entitled recipient stand in for the missing originals.
The agents substantially complied by demanding the bills and documenting each turnover, which proves the extraordinary diligence that rebuts the fault presumption.
The shipper left its own supervisor unpursued until resignation, so it cannot pass to the carrier what may have been its own employee faithlessness.
The non-delivery claim therefore fails while the carrier keeps no liability for the lots duly receipted.
## 3. Duty to Passengers; Delay; Special Classes

> "A common carrier is bound to carry the passengers safely as far as human care and foresight can provide, using the utmost diligence of very cautious persons, with a due regard for all the circumstances."
> — Art. 1755, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Safe carriage | Every passenger must be brought to destination alive and whole through utmost diligence, not merely ordinary care |
| Transport on schedule | Unreasonable delay breaches the contract and supports damages, since time is of the essence in passenger movement |
| Special classes | Children, the aged, the ill, and persons with disability get attention fitted to their condition, since due regard for all the circumstances raises what utmost diligence demands |
| Voyage discipline | The carrier must refuse to sail or fly into known peril, since braving a forecasted storm is fault concurrent with the waves |
| Ancillary and narrow service | A resort boat or package-tour ferry with imputed fare is still common carriage, as Sps. Cruz v. Sun Holidays (G.R. No. 186312, 2010) holds |
| Gratuitous riders | A paying passenger rides inside the contract, a permitted guest without fare still rides under carrier care, and a stowaway rides outside it |

Delay in passenger carriage converts quickly to loss when connections, events, or perils intervene. The carrier that explains delay with weather must show the weather stood alone, since a voluntary voyage into a published storm warning keeps human fault beside the waves and defeats the fortuity claim.

### Case: Lara v. Valencia, G.R. No. L-9907, June 30, 1958

Facts: A forestry inspector caught malaria in the field and begged a ride in a private pick-up going his way. The owner charged no fare and invited him to the front seat. The inspector chose the open back instead, fell asleep on a bag, and fell off on a rough road.

Issue: The question was what care a private owner-driver owes a gratuitous guest and whether the death came from driver fault.

Held: The Court held that only ordinary care was owed and that none was breached. The death came from an unforeseen fall joined with the guest own lack of care.

Doctrine: A gratuitous accommodation guest gets reasonable care rather than the extraordinary diligence of Arts. 1755 and 1756, and the passenger duty of Art. 1761 bars recovery where his own negligence proximately caused the injury.

**2023 Bar Q: Hari and Reyna booked a honeymoon package at Anya Resort (AR) in Palawan which included transportation from Batangas to Palawan, and vice versa, via M/B Prinsesa owned and operated by AR. Despite PAGASA storm warning bulletins, M/B Prinsesa proceeded to ferry Hari and Reyna from the port of Batangas to Palawan. While in open seas, the boat was hit by big waves and eventually capsized, drowning Hari and Reyna. When sued by the heirs of the couple, AR denied liability on the ground that it is not a common carrier, as M/B Prinsesa is not available to the general public or other tourists, and exclusively ferries resort guests and crew members. AR further argued that they did not charge Hari and Reyna separate fees to board M/B Prinsesa. Are the arguments of AR correct? Discuss your answer.**

A: The arguments of AR are incorrect, since the resort boat is a common carrier and sailing into the storm breached its utmost diligence.
The boat carries guests by water for the imputed fare inside the package, and Art. 1732 reaches ancillary and narrow-client carriage so long as the service is offered to whoever books the resort.
The storm defense fails because the bulletins preceded the sailing and the captain chose to brave them, which keeps the human fault concurrent with the waves and defeats the fortuity claim.
Death therefore raises the presumption of fault, and only proof of extraordinary diligence could rebut it, which a voluntary voyage into a forecasted storm cannot supply.

## 4. Extraordinary Diligence: Rationale, Meaning, Presumption, Duration, and Stipulations

> "Common carriers, from the nature of their business and for reasons of public policy, are bound to observe extraordinary diligence in the vigilance over the goods and for the safety of the passengers transported by them, according to all the circumstances of each case."
> — Art. 1733, par. 1, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Rationale | Carriage is impressed with public interest, so the law commands more than ordinary care as the price of the public franchise |
| Meaning | Diligence means the utmost care of very cautious persons for passengers and extreme care for goods, fitted to all the circumstances of each case |
| Non-delegable | Hiring guards, agents, or contractors never passes the duty on, as LRTA v. Navidad (G.R. No. 145804, 2003) holds for station security |
| Presumption for goods | Loss, destruction, or deterioration outside the five Art. 1734 causes presumes fault, and only proof of extraordinary diligence rebuts it under Art. 1735 |
| Presumption for passengers | Death or injury presumes fault, and only proof of the Art. 1733 and 1755 diligence rebuts it under Art. 1756 |
| Acquittal effect | Acquittal of the driver or crew in the criminal case never bars the civil action against the carrier, since contract and quasi-delict liability stand on their own proof |
| Duration for goods | Responsibility runs from unconditional receipt to actual or constructive delivery, through warehouses and unloading, under Arts. 1736 and 1738 |
| Commencement for passengers | Duty opens at fare payment with entry to the station, vehicle, or premises, as British Airways v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 92288, 1993) and LRTA v. Navidad (G.R. No. 145804, 2003) hold |
| Ticket issuance | The ticket evidences the contract and fixes its terms, yet perfection rests on consent and carriage already begun needs no ticket to bind |
| Compliance | Diligence is proved by concrete precautions, inspections, and procedures, since bare denial never rebuts a presumption |
| Third persons | Assaults or acts by strangers and fellow passengers bind the carrier where good-family diligence could have stopped them, under Art. 1759 |
| Stipulation effect | Terms that erase responsibility, cut diligence below extraordinary, or free the carrier from its employees answer are void, while reasonable agreed valuations with consideration may stand |

| Diligence Pair | Carrier Side | Other Side |
|---|---|---|
| Extraordinary vs ordinary | Utmost care of very cautious persons for passengers and extreme vigilance for goods | Good-family care that suffices only between private parties and for gratuitous guests |
| Non-delegable vs delegated | Carrier stays liable though guards or contractors failed | Private owner answers only for chosen-agent fault proved against him |
| Presumed vs proved fault | Contract action starts with fault presumed on loss or injury | Private action starts with fault to be proved by the claimant |
| Actual vs constructive delivery | Hands on the goods to the entitled person | Legal turnover through warehouse advice, deposit, or customs custody under valid terms |

### Case: Ganzon v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. L-48757, May 30, 1988

Facts: Scrap iron was delivered unconditionally to the captain of a carrier lighter for loading, with no qualification on receipt. An acting mayor with armed men then ordered the crew to dump the loaded scrap into the sea as part of a shakedown of the shipper.

Issue: The question was whether receipt started carrier responsibility and whether the mayor order counted as competent public authority.

Held: The Court held that responsibility had started and that the order was no competent authority. The carrier stayed presumed at fault and proved no extraordinary diligence.

Doctrine: Unconditional delivery starts Art. 1736 responsibility though loading is unfinished, and the Art. 1734(5) exemption needs proof that the official had power, that the order was lawful, and that it issued under legal process.

### Case: Regional Container Lines of Singapore v. The Netherlands Insurance Co. (Phils.), Inc., G.R. No. 168151, September 4, 2009

Facts: Refrigerated chemical cargo arrived in Manila and fluctuated to destructive heat after discharge while plugged at the pier. The carrier pointed at the arrastre operator and rested on demurrer after the insurer paid the consignee and sued by subrogation.

Issue: The question was whether custody and diligence survived discharge and what the lost demurrer cost the carrier.

Held: The Court held that both survived and that the presumption stood unrebutted. Unloading cargo generally stays in carrier custody, and reversal of an erroneously granted demurrer bars the demurring party from presenting evidence.

Doctrine: Goods lost or damaged in transit raise the Art. 1735 presumption without need of an express negligence finding, and the carrier must prove extraordinary diligence rather than merely point at another possible culprit.

**2018 Bar Q: Ysidro, a paying passenger, was on board Bus No. 904 owned and operated by Yatco Transportation Company (Yatco). He boarded the bus at Munoz, Nueva Ecija with Manila as his final destination. He was seated on the first row, window seat on the left side of the bus. As the bus was negotiating the national highway in front of the public market of Gerona, Tarlac, the bus came to a full stop because of the traffic. The driver of the bus took this opportunity to check on the tires of the bus and to relieve himself. As he was alighting from the bus to do these, an unidentified man standing along the highway hurled a huge rock at the left side of the bus and hit Ysidro between his eyes. He lost consciousness and immediately the driver, with the conductor, drove the bus to bring him to the nearest hospital. He expired before the bus could reach the hospital. Ysidro's wife and children brought a civil action to collect damages from Yatco, alleging that as a common carrier, it was required to exercise extraordinary diligence in ensuring the safety of its passengers. They contended that, in case of injuries and/or death on the part of any of its passengers, the common carrier is presumed to be at fault. In its defense, Yatco alleged that it is not an absolute insurer of its passengers and that Ysidro's death was not due to any defect in the means of transport or method of transporting passengers, or the negligent acts of its employees. Since the accident was due to the fault of a stranger over whom the common carrier had no control, or of which it did not have any prior knowledge to be able to prevent it, the cause of Ysidro's death should be considered a fortuitous event and not the liability of the common carrier. (a) Is a common carrier presumed to be at fault whenever there is death or injury to its passengers, regardless of the cause of death or injury? (2.5%) (b) What kind of diligence is required of common carriers like Yatco for the protection of its passengers? (2.5%) (c) Will your answer be the same as your answer in (b) above, if the assailant was another paying passenger who boarded the bus and deliberately stabbed Ysidro to death? (2.5%)**

A: A carrier is presumed at fault on a passenger death or injury, yet the presumption yields to proof of utmost diligence, and a stranger attack still tests what diligence could have prevented.
The law presumes fault or negligence on death or injury under Art. 1756, so the carrier opens every case carrying the burden of proving the extraordinary diligence of Arts. 1733 and 1755.
That diligence is the utmost care of very cautious persons with due regard for all the circumstances, and the carrier is no absolute insurer since an unforeseeable stranger act it could not have prevented with good-family vigilance breaks liability under Art. 1759.
A fellow-passenger stabbing is judged the same way, since the carrier answers only where its employees could have prevented or stopped the assault through the diligence the law demands.

## 5. Extraordinary Diligence by Sea

> "Common carriers are responsible for the loss, destruction, or deterioration of the goods, unless the same is due to any of the following causes only: (1) Flood, storm, earthquake, lightning, or other natural disaster or calamity; (2) Act of the public enemy in war, whether international or civil; (3) Act or omission of the shipper or owner of the goods; (4) The character of the goods or defects in the packing or in the containers; (5) Order or act of competent public authority."
> — Art. 1734, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Seaworthiness | Hull, machinery, and complement must be fit to meet the voyage perils at sailing, since an unfit ship starts in breach |
| Cargoworthiness | Holds, hatches, ventilation, and refrigeration must fit the particular cargo, since a sound hull with unfit holds still fails |
| Proper manning | Officers and crew must be licensed, sufficient, and competent for the vessel and voyage |
| Adequate equipment | Safety, navigation, firefighting, and cargo gear must be aboard and working |
| Overloading | Loading beyond marks or stability limits breaches diligence by itself and invites the fortuity defense to fail |
| Proper storage | Stowage must suit the goods, and deck stowage presumes unseaworthiness unless shown harmless to ship management |
| Captain and crew negligence | Fault of master and crew in cargo care binds the ship agent, yet abandonment under Art. 587 of the Code of Commerce saves only the blameless owner whose captain alone was at fault |
| Passenger safety at sea | Routes into known storms, missing life-saving gear, and disorderly muster breach the Art. 1755 duty afloat |
| Proper route | The vessel must keep the usual and agreed route, since unjustified deviation answers for the consequences |
| Transshipment | Transfer to another bottom must rest on agreement or necessity with notice, since secret substitution breaks custody |
| Inspection | Pre-voyage and en-route inspection of hull, gear, and cargo condition proves the diligence the presumption demands |
| Insurance | Marine cover protects the owner and the cargo interests, yet it never dilutes the carrier diligence the Code commands |

A sea carrier that pleads storm must first prove a seaworthy ship. Ordinary swells are voyage vicissitudes that only test fitness, and a top-heavy vessel with improper deck cargo was already unseaworthy before the waves arrived. The limited-liability rule then follows fault: the owner whose captain alone erred may abandon vessel and freight under Art. 587, while the owner whose own want of care concurred answers in full under the Civil Code.

### Case: Philippine American General Insurance Co., Inc. v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 116940, June 11, 1997

Facts: A fishing vessel rebuilt for cargo work loaded thousands of softdrink cases with about two thousand five hundred cases stowed on deck. She sank in ordinary weather on an overnight run, and the insurer paid the bottler and sued the line by subrogation.

Issue: The question was whether the vessel was seaworthy, whether abandonment could cap liability, and whether subrogation was proper.

Held: The Court held that the vessel was unseaworthy from sailing, that abandonment was unavailable to the negligent owner, and that the insurer was properly subrogated. The line paid the full insured value with interest.

Doctrine: Deck cargo raises a presumption of unseaworthiness unless harmlessness is shown, and the Art. 587 abandonment privilege frees only the owner whose captain alone was at fault, never the owner whose own negligence concurred.

**2016 Bar Q: Nautica Shipping Lines (Nautica) bought a second hand passenger ship from Japan. It modified the design of the bulkhead of the deck of the ship to accommodate more passengers. The ship sunk with its passengers in Tablas Strait due to heavy rains brought by the monsoon. The heirs of the passengers sued Nautica for its liability as a common carrier based on the reconfiguration of the bulkhead which may have compromised the stability of the ship. Nautica raised the defense that the monsoon is a fortuitous event and, at most, its liability is prescribed by the Limited Liability Rule. Decide with reasons. (5%)**

A: Nautica answers as a common carrier and keeps neither the fortuity defense nor the limited-liability cap on these facts.
The bulkhead reconfiguration that may have compromised stability puts seaworthiness in issue, and ordinary monsoon rains are voyage vicissitudes rather than an irresistible calamity standing alone.
The storm defense needs an excepted cause with extraordinary diligence, yet sailing a possibly unstable rebuilt ship with passengers aboard proves the opposite of the diligence the law demands.
The abandonment privilege then fails with it, since the owner whose own fault concurred with the casualty cannot limit liability to vessel and freight.
## 6. Extraordinary Diligence by Land and Train; Railroad Crossings

> "In case of death of or injuries to passengers, common carriers are presumed to have been at fault or to have acted negligently, unless they prove that they observed extraordinary diligence as prescribed in articles 1733 and 1755."
> — Art. 1756, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Roadworthiness | Brakes, tires, lights, steering, and body must be fit for the run, since a defective unit starts in breach |
| Traffic rules | Speed, loading, signaling, and right-of-way under R.A. No. 4136 must be obeyed, since violation evidences want of diligence |
| Selection and supervision | Drivers and conductors must be hired for competence and watched in service, since a careless crew choice is carrier fault |
| Inspection | Pre-departure and en-route checks of unit and cargo prove the diligence the presumption demands |
| Insurance | Compulsory motor cover answers third persons within its limits, yet it never dilutes the carrier diligence the Code commands |
| Train operation | Speed, braking, lookout, and station procedure must fit the run, since LRTA v. Navidad (G.R. No. 145804, 2003) measures even platforms by utmost diligence |
| Falls from trains | Open doors, sudden jerks, and overcrowding that spill riders breach the duty to carry safely |
| Run-overs | Track patrol, lookout, and braking decide liability to persons and animals on the line |
| Near-track damage | Vibration, sparks, and debris that harm nearby property and persons test fencing, speed, and maintenance |
| Crossing warnings | Bells, lights, barriers, and watchmen must work at crossings, since a broken barrier invites the tragedy it was built to stop |
| Third-person duties at crossings | Motorists must stop, look, and listen and never stall upon the rails, since the track gives the train the right of way |
| No imputed negligence | The driver fault is never charged to the riding passenger, so the passenger claim survives though the jeepney or car driver shared the blame |

Land carriage multiplies the actors, so the diligence inquiry always asks two things together. It asks what the crew did wrong, and it asks what the operator failed to build, inspect, or warn about before the trip began. A school van for a fee answers as a common carrier, as Sps. Pereña v. Sps. Nicolas (G.R. No. 157917, 2012) holds, and app-based vehicles offered to the riding public answer in concept as carriage, as LTFRB v. Valenzuela (G.R. No. 242860, 2019) treats them.

**2016 Bar Q: A railroad track of the Philippine National Railway (PNR) is located near a busy intersection of Puyat Avenue and Osmeña Highway. One afternoon, the intersection was heavily congested, as usual. Juan, the driver of a public utility jeepney (PUJ), drove onto the railroad tracks but could go no farther because of the heavy traffic at the intersection. After the jeepney stopped right on the railroad track, it was hit and overturned by a PNR train, resulting in the death of Kim, a passenger of the PUJ, and injuries to Juan and his other passengers. Juan, the injured passengers and Kim's family sued the PNR for damages for its negligence. It was established that the steel pole barrier before the track was broken, and that the PNR had the last clear chance of avoiding the accident. On the other hand, the PNR raised the defense that the track is for the exclusive use of the train and that motorists are aware that it is negligence per se to stop their vehicles on the tracks. Decide the case and explain. (5%)**

A: The PNR answers though Juan was also at fault, since the broken barrier and the last clear chance keep the train operator liable while Juan fault never travels to his passengers.
The carrier and operator owe utmost diligence over the line with working warnings, and a barrier left broken at a known congested crossing breaches that diligence before any jeepney stalls.
Juan breached his own motorist duty by entering rails he could not clear, so his personal claim suffers his contributory fault under the avoidable-consequences and last-clear-chance sorting.
Kim and the riding passengers recover in full, since the driver negligence is not imputed to them and the PNR with the final opportunity to stop must answer for the injuries its train inflicted.

**Q: A bus company hires an unlicensed driver without road test, skips brake inspection for a month, and the bus with worn pads rams a truck. The company blames the truck driver. Who answers the injured bus passengers?**

A: The bus company answers the passengers, since its own want of diligence concurs regardless of the truck fault.
Hiring without license or test breaches selection diligence and skipping brake checks breaches inspection diligence, so the presumption of fault stands unrebutted.
The truck fault may divide the ultimate loss between the operators through contribution, yet division never delays or defeats the passenger recovery.
The passengers therefore collect from their carrier upon the presumption, and the company pursues the truck operator separately for its share.

## 7. Passenger Baggage

> "Such extraordinary diligence in the vigilance over the goods is further expressed in articles 1734, 1735, and 1745, Nos. 5, 6, and 7, while the extraordinary diligence for the safety of the passengers is further set forth in articles 1755 and 1756."
> — Art. 1733, par. 2, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Defined | Baggage means the personal effects a traveler carries for the journey, including clothes, necessities, and articles of personal use |
| Checked-in baggage | Pieces handed to the carrier and tagged for the hold ride in carrier custody, so the goods rules on diligence, presumption, and agreed valuation govern |
| Hand-carried baggage | Pieces kept by the passenger in cabin or seat stay in passenger custody, so the passenger proves carrier fault for their loss |
| Declared value | A higher declared value with added freight binds the carrier to that value, while undeclared pieces recover under the proved or agreed measure |
| Custody test | The hand that keeps the piece fixes the rule, since custody and diligence travel together through this whole Part |

| Baggage Kind | Custody | Rule on Loss |
|---|---|---|
| Checked into the hold with a tag | Carrier from check-in to carousel delivery | Presumed carrier fault outside Art. 1734 causes |
| Registered excess with declared value | Carrier with valuation agreement | Agreed value within stipulation limits |
| Hand-carried into cabin or seat | Passenger throughout | Carrier liable only on proved fault of its employees |
| Left in the terminal before check-in | Passenger until carrier receipt | No carrier responsibility yet under Art. 1736 |

**Q: A tagged suitcase checked into the hold vanishes before arrival, while a laptop kept in the overhead bin is snatched when the passenger leaves it to use the lavatory. What must each claimant prove?**

A: The suitcase claimant proves turnover with loss at arrival while the laptop claimant must prove carrier fault, since custody fixes the burden.
The tagged suitcase rode in carrier custody, so vanishing baggage raises the presumption of fault and the carrier must prove extraordinary diligence or an Art. 1734 excuse.
The laptop stayed in passenger custody throughout, so no presumption runs and the passenger must show the snatching came through employee fault or want of due precautions.
The first claim therefore succeeds on custody and loss alone unless rebutted, while the second needs specific fault proved against the carrier.

## 8. Obligations of the Passenger and Shipper

> "The passenger must observe the diligence of a good father of a family to avoid injury to himself."
> — Art. 1761, Civil Code of the Philippines

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Shipper care | The shipper must tender goods honestly packed, marked, and fit, since his own negligence that causes the loss breaks the carrier chain |
| Disclosure | Dangerous character, hidden defects, and special handling needs must be declared, since silence that endangers the voyage is shipper fault |
| Damage by cargo | The shipper answers for injury his goods inflict on fellow cargo, the vessel, or third persons through undisclosed danger or vicious nature |
| Freight payment | The price of carriage must be paid as agreed, since carriage for compensation is of the essence of the Art. 1732 undertaking |
| Who pays | Freight falls on the party the contract or bill names, commonly the shipper prepaid or the consignee collect |
| When paid | Freight falls due as the contract or bill fixes, commonly on shipment or on delivery |
| Carrier lien | Unpaid freight and charges hold a lien on the goods in carrier hands, so delivery may wait until lawful charges are settled |
| Loading and unloading | Handling follows the contract and bill terms, with free-in-and-out stowed terms putting stevedoring on the cargo interests as agreed |
| Demurrage | Delay beyond the lay days allowed for loading or unloading earns the agreed daily charge to the waiting vessel or vehicle |
| Permits | Export, import, quarantine, and customs papers belong to the cargo interests, and want of them excuses the carrier though delay results |
| Shipper load and count | Containers stuffed, sealed, and counted by the shipper alone bind the carrier only to the seal and exterior, never to the unverified count |
| Passenger duties | Passengers must pay fare, obey lawful crew orders, and use good-family care for their own safety under Art. 1761 |
| Travel documents | Passports, visas, and health papers belong to the passenger, and the carrier may refuse carriage without them |
| Air transport | International air duties, tickets, baggage limits, and delay rules follow the Montreal Convention of 1999, treated in full in Part II-E |

The shipper who hides danger pays twice over. He pays for the damage his cargo inflicts, and he loses the claim for his own goods where his concealment caused or aggravated the loss. Planters Products v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 101503, 1993) shows the companion point that agreed unloading terms allocate handling between ship and shore and defeat claims outside the allocation.

**Q: A shipper declares general merchandise but hides drums of calcium carbide that explode on contact with seawater, damaging fellow cargo. He then refuses freight, claiming the carrier failed to deliver. Who pays for the damage and the freight?**

A: The shipper pays for the damage and still owes the freight, since concealment caused the casualty while carriage was duly performed.
The undisclosed dangerous character is shipper fault under Art. 1734(3) that breaks the carrier chain, so the explosion loss falls on the concealing shipper with recourse from him by the fellow cargo owners.
The carrier earned its freight by receiving, stowing, and sailing the goods with the diligence the known facts demanded, so refusal to pay has no basis in the failed-delivery claim.
The carrier therefore keeps its lien on surviving goods for the unpaid charges while the shipper answers for the explosion damage.

**Q: A passenger boards without a visa for the destination state and is refused onward carriage at the transit stop. He demands a full refund and damages for delay. Does he collect?**

A: He collects no damages though fare rules decide the refund, since travel papers are his own duty.
Passports, visas, and health papers belong to the passenger under his Art. 1761 self-care and the contract terms, and want of them lawfully excuses carriage without breach.
Delay from the refusal is therefore his own making rather than carrier fault, so no delay damages run.
The carrier keeps the flown legs earned and refunds only what its tariff promises for the unflown portion.

## Exam Traps

1. A common carrier must accept fit lawful tender within route and means, since inconvenience is no lawful ground for refusal.
2. Dangerous goods without disclosure may be refused, and concealment turns the later explosion into shipper fault.
3. Animals ride under the same diligence with their nature counted, and inherent vice answers under character of goods.
4. Negligent delay keeps liability alive though a later storm strikes, since Art. 1740 punishes the carrier that dawdled.
5. Delivery means the entitled recipient, so release to a forger is no delivery at all.
6. Signed receipts by the entitled recipient discharge like returned bills, yet only where substantial compliance is proved.
7. A valid customs-cutoff bill term ends liability at customs custody, since the Code binds only goods in carrier possession.
8. Unloading cargo stays in carrier custody, so pointing at the arrastre operator never rebuts the presumption by itself.
9. An illegal mayor order is no competent public authority, since power, lawfulness, and legal process must all be proved.
10. Criminal acquittal of the driver never kills the civil carrier suit, since contract liability stands on its own proof.
11. Hiring guards or contractors never delegates diligence, since the duty follows the franchise.
12. Duty to train passengers opens at fare payment with entry, so platform injuries already sound in utmost diligence.
13. A gratuitous guest in a private car gets ordinary care only, since Arts. 1755 and 1756 bind common carriers alone.
14. Deck stowage presumes unseaworthiness, and ordinary swells never excuse a ship that sailed top-heavy.
15. The owner whose own fault concurred cannot abandon under Art. 587, since the privilege saves only the blameless owner.
16. A broken crossing barrier with last clear chance keeps the operator liable though the motorist stalled on the rails.
17. Driver fault is never imputed to the riding passenger, so the passenger collects though the jeepney driver shared blame.
18. Checked baggage runs on presumed fault while hand-carried pieces need proved fault, since custody fixes the burden.
19. Unpaid freight holds a carrier lien, so the goods wait until lawful charges are settled.
20. Shipper-stuffed and sealed containers bind the carrier to seal and exterior only, never to the unverified count.
