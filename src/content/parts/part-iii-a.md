## 1. Public Utility Concept; Public Service under PSA

> "Public Utility. — Public Utility refers to a public service that operates, manages or controls for public use any of the following: (1) Distribution of Electricity; (2) Transmission of Electricity; (3) Petroleum and Petroleum Products Pipeline Transmission Systems; (4) Water Pipeline Distribution Systems and Wastewater Pipeline Systems, including sewerage pipeline systems; (5) Seaports; and (6) Public Utility Vehicles."

> "All concessionaires, joint ventures and other similar entities that wholly operate, manage or control for public use the sectors above are public utilities."

> "Nothing in this Act shall be interpreted as a requirement for legislative franchise where the law does not require any. No other person shall be deemed a public utility unless otherwise subsequently provided by law."

> "(b) The term 'public service' includes every person that now or hereafter may own, operate, manage, or control in the Philippines, for hire or compensation, with general or limited clientele, whether permanent, occasional or accidental, and done for general business purposes, any common carrier, railroad, street railway, traction railway, sub-way motor vehicle, either for freight or passenger, or both with or without fixed route and whether may be its classification, freight or carrier service of any class, express service, steamboat or steamship line, pontines, ferries, and water craft, engaged in the transportation of passengers or freight or both, shipyard, marine railways, marine repair shop, [warehouse] wharf or dock, ice plant, ice-refrigeration plant, canal, irrigation system, gas, electric light, heat and power water supply and power, petroleum, sewerage system, wire or wireless communications system, wire or wireless broadcasting stations and other similar public services."

| Element or Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Public utility closed list | Only the six Sec. 13(d) services operated, managed, or controlled for public use, and the President may only recommend that Congress add a service on NEDA advice under the four network, natural-monopoly, necessity, and demand criteria |
| Public service element one | The person must own, operate, manage, or control a covered service in the Philippines, and a mere owner-lessor that never operates is outside the class |
| Public service element two | The activity must be for hire or compensation, and free or incidental service without charge fails this element |
| Public service element three | The activity must be done for general business purposes, meaning for profit or as a means of livelihood, so a State function funded by appropriations is not business |
| Consequence of the split | A service that leaves the six-item list stays a regulated public service and a business affected with public interest under Secs. 17 and 18, yet no nationality requirement may be imposed on it |

A public utility is always a public service, while a public service is a public utility only when it falls inside the six-item list. Operation for the public marks both classes, while sixty percent Filipino ownership marks only the utility. Formation of a corporation marks nothing, while application for authority marks the reckoning point. Ownership of facilities marks a property relation, while operation of the service marks the enfranchised relation.

### Case: *Bureau of Telecommunications v. PSC*, G.R. No. L-27412, October 28, 1969

Facts: The Bureau operated a telephone system alongside PLDT under Executive Order No. 94, charged rates below PLDT levels, served twenty to thirty percent private subscribers, kept no corporate existence, could not use its income, and depended on annual congressional appropriations.

Issue: The issue was whether a government bureau discharging a State communication function was a public service operated for general business purposes.

Held: The Court held that the Bureau was neither, because business means activity for profit or livelihood and the Bureau served the government and the people as a State responsibility with private service only incidental.

Doctrine: A government entity that operates without gain, keeps no income, and lives on appropriations is not engaged in business under the Public Service Act and lies outside Commission jurisdiction.

### Case: *Cruz de Syquia v. Board of Power and Water Works*, G.R. Nos. L-42783-85, November 29, 1976

Facts: The petitioner administered an apartment building, advanced the compound-wide Meralco bill, collected reimbursement pro rata from tenants, owned no power plant, and faced board orders capping her collections below cost.

Issue: The issue was whether a building owner who passes on utility billings sells electricity as a public service.

Held: The Court held that she did not, because Meralco supplied electricity directly to the tenants as end users and the dispute over common-area sharing was a purely civil lease controversy for the regular courts.

Doctrine: One who owns, operates, manages, or controls no plant and merely advances and recovers billings is not a public service and is beyond regulatory jurisdiction.

### Case: *Albano v. Reyes*, G.R. No. 83551, July 11, 1989

Facts: The Philippine Ports Authority contracted the development, management, and operation of the Manila International Container Terminal to ICTSI, the President approved the award, and a legislator claimed that only a congressional franchise could authorize the operation.

Issue: The issue was whether every public-utility operation needs a franchise specially granted by Congress.

Held: The Court held that none was needed, because Congress may delegate licensing power and the port charter let the Authority operate by itself or by contract with presidential approval.

Doctrine: A statute may vest an executive agency with authority to license a public utility, and the valid exercise of that delegated power needs no separate congressional franchise.

**Q: A company owns no power plant and merely advances the building-wide Meralco bill while collecting each tenant's metered share. The tenants complain to the regulator that the company sells electricity without a franchise. Is the company a public service subject to regulation.**

A: No, because the three-element Sec. 13(b) test fails on ownership or control of a covered service. The law requires ownership, operation, management, or control of an enterprise supplying electric light, heat, and power for hire or compensation as a business, and a mere administrator who advances and recovers billings owns and controls no plant while the utility supplies the tenants directly. The ruling in Cruz de Syquia (G.R. Nos. L-42783-85, 1976) annulled the same kind of board orders and left the pro-rata dispute to the regular courts as a civil lease question.

## 2. Regulatory Agencies; Bases of Regulation

> "The Commission shall have jurisdiction and supervision over all public services, including public utilities, and their franchises, equipment, and other properties, and in the exercise of its authority, it shall have the necessary powers and the aid of public force: Provided, That public services, including public utilities, owned or operated by government entities shall be regulated by the Commission in the same way as privately-owned public services."

> "All references to the Public Service Commission in Commonwealth Act No. 146, as amended, shall pertain to any Administrative Agency to which the powers and duties of the Public Service Commission were transferred by subsequent laws, such as but not limited to: (a) Civil Aeronautics Board (CAB); (b) Civil Aviation Authority of the Philippines (CAAP); (c) Department of Energy (DOE); (d) Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR); (e) DICT; (f) Department of Transportation (DOTr); (g) Energy Regulatory Commission (ERC); (h) Land Transportation Franchising and Regulatory Board (LTFRB); (i) Land Transportation Office (LTO); (j) Local Water Utilities Administration (LWUA); (k) Maritime Industry Authority (MARINA); (l) Metropolitan Waterworks and Sewerage System (MWSS); (m) National Telecommunication Commission (NTC); (n) National Water Resources Board (NWRB); (o) Philippine National Railways (PNR); (p) Philippine Ports Authority (PPA); and (q) Toll Regulatory Board (TRB)."

> "SEC. 6. The use of property bears a social function, and all economic agents shall contribute to the common good. Individuals and private groups, including corporations, cooperatives, and similar collective organizations, shall have the right to own, establish, and operate economic enterprises, subject to the duty of the State to promote distributive justice and to intervene when the common good so demands."

| Element or Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Who regulates | The successor administrative agencies exercise the jurisdiction, supervision, and powers once held by the Public Service Commission, each within its chartered sector |
| Whom they regulate | All public services including public utilities, and government-owned or operated services are regulated the same way as privately owned ones |
| Why regulation is valid | Regulation rests on the police power because private property devoted to a public use and affected with public interest ceases to be juris privati only, and Sec. 6 Art. XII confirms the social function of property |
| Limit of the power | Police power cannot create an exclusive franchise or violate the Constitution, and delegated rate and certificate powers apply to utilities already operating when the statute passes |

Police-power regulation is plenary yet constitutional, while ownership of the utility stays private until a lawful taking. Delegated licensing by an agency is valid, while an exclusive grant by anyone is void. Government operation is regulated, while government ownership alone exempts no one from rate control.

### Case: *Pangasinan Transportation v. PSC*, G.R. No. 47065, June 26, 1940

Facts: The operator held long-standing certificates for Pangasinan, Tarlac, Nueva Ecija, and Zambales routes, applied for ten additional trucks, and received new twenty-five-year limits with a Commonwealth acquisition condition on all its certificates.

Issue: The issue was whether the amendatory law unduly delegated legislative power and whether the new conditions could reach existing certificates without hearing.

Held: The Court held that the delegation was valid because public interest is a sufficient standard capped at fifty years, that a certificate is a mere privilege reachable by police power, and that the twenty-five-year limit still needed prior notice and hearing so the case was remanded.

Doctrine: The Public Service Act is a valid police-power measure, public interest standards sustain subordinate legislation, a certificate confers no property right, and new conditions on existing certificates require notice and hearing.

**Q: A bus operator with existing certificates applies for additional units and the regulator adds a twenty-five-year expiry to all its certificates without any hearing. The operator assails the whole statute as undue delegation. How should the challenge fail and the order fall.**

A: The statute stands while the order falls for want of hearing. The regulation of utilities is a valid exercise of police power, the legislature may delegate certificate periods under the sufficient standard of public interest within the fifty-year constitutional cap, and a certificate is a mere privilege so police-power conditions reach existing holders. The ruling in Pangasinan Transportation (G.R. No. 47065, 1940) still annulled the limit because Sec. 16(m) allows amendment only upon proper notice and hearing and the operator never had the chance to present its case.

## 3. Constitutional Provisions and Limitations

> "No franchise, certificate, or any other form of authorization for the operation of a public utility shall be granted except to citizens of the Philippines or to corporations or associations organized under the laws of the Philippines, at least sixty per centum of whose capital is owned by such citizens; nor shall such franchise, certificate, or authorization be exclusive in character or for a longer period than fifty years. Neither shall any such franchise or right be granted except under the condition that it shall be subject to amendment, alteration, or repeal by the Congress when the common good so requires. The State shall encourage equity participation in public utilities by the general public. The participation of foreign investors in the governing body of any public utility enterprise shall be limited to their proportionate share in its capital, and all the executive and managing officers of such corporation or association must be citizens of the Philippines."

> "In times of national emergency, when the public interest so requires, the State may, during the emergency and under reasonable terms prescribed by it, temporarily take over or direct the operation of any privately-owned public utility or business affected with public interest."

> "The State may, in the interest of national welfare or defense, establish and operate vital industries and, upon payment of just compensation, transfer to public ownership utilities and other private enterprises to be operated by the Government."

> "The State shall regulate or prohibit monopolies when the public interest so requires. No combinations in restraint of trade or unfair competition shall be allowed."

| Element or Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| Ownership filter | Only Filipino citizens or domestic corporations or associations at least sixty per centum of whose capital is Filipino-owned may receive operating authority, foreign board participation is capped at the proportionate capital share, and all executive and managing officers must be Filipino citizens |
| Reckoning point | Compliance is tested when the entity applies for a franchise, certificate, or authorization, so a corporation may be formed with alien capital and cure by transfer before that moment |
| Operation against facilities | The filter attaches to operation of the utility and never to mere ownership of facilities, so a foreign owner-lessor whose domestic partner operates needs no sixty percent Filipino capital |
| Capital measure | Capital means shares entitled to vote in the election of directors with full beneficial ownership, applied to voting shares and to all outstanding shares under SEC-MC No. 8, so control follows votes and not the weight of non-voting preferred stock |
| Term cap | No grant exceeds fifty years at any given time, the period is inseparable from the certificate, and renewal weighs exemplary record with substantial infrastructure investment |
| Exclusivity ban | No franchise, certificate, or authorization is exclusive in character, whether created directly or indirectly by the President, Congress, or the Court |
| Modifiability condition | Every grant carries the implied condition of amendment, alteration, or repeal by Congress when the common good so requires, and a franchise remains a mere privilege burdenable with public-service duties |
| Takeover rule | Temporary takeover or directed operation needs a national emergency, public-interest necessity, reasonable terms, and a congressional delegation to the President, while permanent transfer needs national welfare or defense plus just compensation |
| Monopoly rule | The State regulates or prohibits monopolies as public interest requires, a regulated monopoly in an affected industry is allowed, and private advantage without a public grant is not state action |

A primary franchise gives corporate life, while a secondary franchise gives operating authority and only the latter draws the sixty percent rule. Voting common shares elect directors and count, while non-voting preferred shares fund and do not count. Temporary takeover ends with the emergency and needs delegated power, while permanent transfer endures and needs just compensation. A regulated monopoly serves the public under a grant, while an unregulated combination restrains trade and is banned.

### Case: *People v. Quasha*, G.R. No. L-6055, June 12, 1953

Facts: The accused organized Pacific Airways Corporation, named a Filipino subscriber for 60.005 percent of the subscribed capital while the funds belonged to unlisted Americans, and faced falsification charges for hiding the trust to evade the sixty percent rule.

Issue: The issue was whether the Constitution barred mere formation of a utility corporation with alien capital and punished nondisclosure of the dummy arrangement.

Held: The Court held that no crime was committed, because the ownership clause qualifies the secondary franchise to operate and never the primary franchise of corporate existence, the Corporation Law required no disclosure, and the later Party Amendment in any event lifted the bar as to Americans.

Doctrine: The sixty percent requirement is tested at the application for operating authority and never at incorporation, and a corporation formed with alien capital may comply later by transferring shares to Filipinos.

### Case: *Tatad v. Garcia*, G.R. No. 114222, April 6, 1995

Facts: A Hongkong corporation financed and built EDSA LRT III, leased the completed system to the DOTC for twenty-five years with ownership passing for one dollar, supplied technical maintenance and training, and left operation as a common carrier with fare collection to the Department.

Issue: The issue was whether a foreign corporation that owns the facilities of a rail system must meet the sixty percent Filipino capitalization requirement.

Held: The Court held that it need not, because nationality is determined at the franchise application and the private respondent never applied to operate since the DOTC alone ran the trains and dealt with the riding public.

Doctrine: The Constitution limits foreign equity in operation of a public utility and never in mere ownership of facilities, and a mere owner-lessor that neither operates nor serves the public is not itself a public utility.

### Case: *Gamboa v. Teves*, G.R. No. 176579, June 28, 2011

Facts: The petitioner questioned PLDT ownership after the sale of the government's PTIC block raised foreign holdings, and the 2010 general information sheet showed foreigners holding 64.27 percent of the voting common shares while non-voting preferred shares dominated authorized capital.

Issue: The issue was whether capital in Sec. 11 Art. XII means total outstanding stock including non-voting preferred shares or only shares entitled to vote in the election of directors.

Held: The Court held that capital means voting shares only, because control runs through electing directors, the Corporation Code strips voting rights only from preferred or redeemable shares, the Foreign Investments Act defines a Philippine national by sixty percent voting capital with full beneficial ownership, and the broad reading would let a minuscule foreign vote control the utility.

Doctrine: Capital in Sec. 11 Art. XII refers only to shares entitled to vote in the election of directors, the sixty percent rule demands full beneficial ownership with voting rights, and Sec. 11 is self-executing through the SEC.

### Case: *Heirs of Gamboa v. Teves*, G.R. No. 176579, October 9, 2012

Facts: The movants assailed the voting-share construction as a midstream redefinition, invoked SEC and DOJ opinions equating capital with total outstanding stock, warned of massive divestment, and sought reconsideration of the directive to the SEC.

Issue: The issue was whether the construction displaced a settled definition and whether executive opinions bound the Court.

Held: The Court held that no settled definition existed in seventy-five years, that voting control with beneficial ownership reserves effective control to Filipinos, that individual SEC opinions are never conclusive on constitutional meaning, and that sanctions apply only prospectively after finality.

Doctrine: Both the voting-control test and the beneficial-ownership test determine Philippine nationality, the sixty-forty ratio reaches each class of shares on the resolution reasoning, and only the SEC en banc makes rules while the Court alone construes capital.

### Case: *Roy III v. Herbosa*, G.R. No. 207246, November 22, 2016

Facts: The petitioner assailed SEC Memorandum Circular No. 8 requiring sixty percent Filipino ownership of both voting shares and total outstanding stock, intervenors warned of forced divestment worth billions, and the SEC had issued no definitive ruling on PLDT compliance.

Issue: The issue was whether the circular contradicted the Gamboa fallo and whether the Court should void it before any enforcement ruling.

Held: The Court held that the petition failed on justiciability and on the merits, because the circular faithfully applied voting control plus full beneficial ownership to voting shares and to all outstanding shares while the per-class passage in the resolution body was obiter that could never amend the immutable fallo.

Doctrine: Capital means voting shares with the sixty-forty Filipino requirement also measured across all outstanding stock, the dispositive portion controls over body reasoning, and transcendental importance alone does not relax locus standi or hierarchy.

### Case: *Kilosbayan v. Guingona*, G.R. No. 113375, May 5, 1994

Facts: The PCSO charter barred lotteries in collaboration or joint venture with any person, yet the so-called Contract of Lease left PGMC to build at its own expense, keep and replace facilities for eight years, bear shortage risk, train personnel, share receipts-based rentals, and shape policy.

Issue: The issue was whether a lease label could save an arrangement that assigned the franchise itself to a foreign-linked contractor.

Held: The Court held that the contract was void, because intention is gathered from contemporaneous and subsequent acts, a franchise is strictly construed against the grantee with nothing passing by implication, and the PCSO had leased out its very franchise to an operator.

Doctrine: A contract that leaves technical work, operations, management, expenses, risks, and profit participation with the contractor is a collaboration or joint venture in substance whatever its title, and a franchisee cannot share, assign, or transfer its franchise.

### Case: *RCPI v. NTC*, G.R. No. L-68729, May 29, 1987

Facts: RCPI operated radio stations under a 1957 legislative franchise, a rival received NTC authority for the same areas, and the Commission ordered RCPI to cease operations conducted without a certificate of public convenience and necessity.

Issue: The issue was whether a prior franchisee could block a newcomer and operate on its franchise alone without a certificate.

Held: The Court held that the franchise required prior allotment and licensing, that executive reorganization had removed the old radio exemption and imposed the certificate requirement, and that a franchise can never be exclusive so prior operations yield to a new grant made for the public interest.

Doctrine: A legislative franchise is a mere privilege subject to police-power regulation, it cannot be exclusive in character, and an existing operator has no insuperable right against later authority issued over the same area.

### Case: *ABS-CBN v. PMSI*, G.R. Nos. 175769-70, January 19, 2009

Facts: ABS-CBN held a legislative franchise and broadcast free-to-air signals, PMSI carried those signals unaltered to DTH subscribers, and the NTC must-carry rule required cable and DTH operators within signal contours to carry authorized broadcast stations.

Issue: The issue was whether unaltered simultaneous carriage infringed broadcast and copyright rights and whether the must-carry burden on the franchise was valid.

Held: The Court held that no infringement occurred, because simultaneous unaltered carriage is cable retransmission and not rebroadcasting, the carrier selects nothing and authors nothing, and the government-directed use falls within the statutory limitation for public-interest carriage.

Doctrine: A franchise carries amendment for the common good, simultaneous unaltered carriage of free-to-air signals is retransmission and not rebroadcasting, and the must-carry rule validly burdens the privilege without effecting a taking.

### Case: *TELEBAP v. COMELEC*, G.R. No. 132922, April 21, 1998

Facts: The election law required procurement of COMELEC time and amended all broadcast franchises to carry free equal and impartial air time, the implementing resolution first commanded thirty minutes of daily prime time, and a network claimed tens of millions in lost revenue.

Issue: The issue was whether free air time took private property without due process or just compensation and denied broadcasters equal protection against print media.

Held: The Court held that Sec. 92 was valid, because a franchise is a mere privilege over airwaves the broadcaster never owns, unrealized advertising revenue is no protected property, spectrum scarcity justifies heavier broadcast burdens, and only the implementing resolution failed since an agency cannot amend a statute.

Doctrine: A broadcast franchise is a temporary privilege burdenable with public-service duties such as free election air time, spectrum scarcity sustains classifications that print media escapes, and the amendability condition reaches every franchise when the common good so requires.

### Case: *David v. Arroyo*, G.R. No. 171396, May 3, 2006

Facts: The President declared a state of national emergency, commanded the armed forces to suppress lawless violence, invoked Sec. 17 Art. XII takeover language without any statute delegating emergency powers, and enforcers raided a newspaper office and dispersed rallies.

Issue: The issue was whether the emergency declaration alone authorized the President to take over or direct privately owned utilities and businesses affected with public interest.

Held: The Court held that the declaration called out the armed forces validly yet authorized no takeover, because Sec. 17 Art. XII is an aspect of emergency powers reposed in Congress under Sec. 23(2) Art. VI, delegable only by law for a limited period to carry out declared national policy.

Doctrine: Only Congress holds emergency powers and the President exercises them solely upon legislative delegation, so a takeover without an emergency-powers act is void even during a validly declared emergency.

**2017 Bar Q: TRUE or FALSE. Explain briefly your answer. (b) The term capital in relation to public utilities under Sec. 11, Art. XII of the 1987 Constitution refers to the total outstanding capital stock comprising both common and non-voting preferred shares. (2%)**

A: FALSE, because capital in Sec. 11 Art. XII means only shares entitled to vote in the election of directors and never the total outstanding stock with non-voting preferred shares. The law ties control to electing directors and demands full beneficial ownership with voting rights, so the sixty-forty test applies to voting shares under the 2011 decision in Gamboa (G.R. No. 176579, 2011) as implemented by SEC-MC No. 8 sustained in Roy III (G.R. No. 207246, 2016). A corporation whose foreigners hold the voting common shares fails the test even when Filipinos hold all the non-voting preferred shares.

**Q: A water district charter provides that no other franchise for water service inside the district may issue without board consent. A cooperative seeks a franchise and the board refuses. Is the consent requirement valid.**

A: No, because the Constitution bans every exclusive franchise whether created directly or indirectly. The law states that no franchise, certificate, or authorization for a public utility shall be exclusive in character, and police power includes no power to violate that ban. The book rule voids Sec. 47 of P.D. No. 198 as amended for creating exclusivity through a consent veto, following the RCPI mandate (G.R. No. L-68729, 1987) that one operator never blocks another.

## 4. Foreign-Owned Enterprise; Critical Infrastructure

> "Critical Infrastructure refers to any public service which owns, uses, or operates systems and assets, whether physical or virtual, so vital to the Republic of the Philippines that the incapacity or destruction of such systems or assets would have a detrimental impact on national security, including telecommunications and other such vital services as may be declared by the President of the Philippines."

> "Foreign State-owned Enterprise refers to an entity in which a foreign State: (i) directly or indirectly owns more than fifty-percent (50%) of the capital taking into account both the voting rights and beneficial ownership; (ii) controls, through ownership interests, the exercise of more than fifty percent (50%) of the voting rights; or (iii) holds the power to appoint a majority of members of the board of directors or any other equivalent management body."

> "In the interest of national security, the President, after review, evaluation and recommendation of the relevant government department or Administrative Agency, may, within sixty (60) days from the receipt of such recommendation, suspend or prohibit any proposed merger or acquisition transaction, or any investment in a public service that effectively results in the grant of control, whether direct or indirect, to a foreigner or a foreign corporation."

> "An entity controlled by or acting on behalf of the foreign government or foreign state-owned enterprises shall be prohibited from owning capital in any public service classified as public utility or critical infrastructure."

> "Foreign nationals shall not be allowed to own more than fifty percent (50%) of the capital of entities engaged in the operation and management of critical infrastructure unless the country of such foreign national accords reciprocity to Philippine Nationals as may be provided by foreign law, treaty or international agreement. Reciprocity may be satisfied by according rights of similar value in other economic sectors."

| Element or Requisite | Content |
|---|---|
| What counts as critical | Any public service owning, using, or operating systems or assets so vital that incapacity or destruction would harm national security, including telecommunications and other vital services the President declares |
| State-owned ban | An entity controlled by or acting for a foreign government or a foreign state-owned enterprise may not own capital in a public utility or critical infrastructure for post-effectivity investments, except that sovereign wealth and independent pension funds may collectively own up to thirty percent |
| Reciprocity cap | A foreign national may not own more than fifty percent of a critical-infrastructure operator unless his country accords reciprocity to Philippine nationals by law, treaty, or agreement, satisfiable by rights of similar value in other sectors |
| Presidential suspension | The President may suspend or prohibit a merger, acquisition, or investment granting direct or indirect control to a foreigner within sixty days from receipt of the department or agency recommendation |
| Employment and audit | Foreign hiring needs non-availability of a competent Filipino with permits and understudy programs, and critical-infrastructure operators face annual performance audit with risk, emergency-response, and cybersecurity metrics plus ten-day complaint action and monthly reports |

Full foreign ownership is allowed for a public service outside the utility list, while sixty percent Filipino capital still gates every public utility. Private foreign capital negotiates the reciprocity cap, while foreign state capital faces the ownership ban. Presidential suspension guards control transfers, while NEDA advises on subcontracting without risking operational resiliency.

**Q: A foreign state-owned enterprise proposes to acquire forty percent of a declared critical-infrastructure telecommunications operator after the effectivity of R.A. No. 11659. Its lawyers invoke the thirty percent sovereign wealth exception. May the acquisition proceed.**

A: No, because the ban reaches every entity controlled by or acting for a foreign government with only the narrow fund exception available. The law prohibits a foreign state-owned enterprise from owning capital in a public utility or critical infrastructure for post-effectivity investments, and the thirty percent allowance belongs solely to sovereign wealth funds and independent pension funds collectively. A forty percent acquisition by the state enterprise itself exceeds the only exception and remains prohibited.

## Exam Traps

1. A broad pre-2022 definition of public utility no longer controls since Sec. 13(d) closes the list to six services.
2. A foreigner may own facilities yet never operate, and only operation draws the sixty percent rule.
3. Incorporation with alien capital is lawful, and the test falls only at the franchise application.
4. Non-voting preferred shares never elect directors, so a Filipino-heavy total stock with foreign-held votes still fails.
5. The Gamboa resolution body discusses per-class application, yet the immutable fallo as implemented measures voting shares and all outstanding shares.
6. A lease label never decides, and a contractor that operates, risks, and controls holds a void joint venture over the franchise.
7. A legislative franchise is a privilege and never property, so new burdens for the common good effect no taking.
8. Airwaves belong to the State, so free COMELEC time takes nothing compensable from the broadcaster.
9. An agency may implement a statute yet never amend it, so a paid-time rule that rewrites free time is void.
10. A certificate is a mere privilege, so police-power conditions reach existing holders with notice and hearing.
11. Public interest sustains delegation, yet the fifty-year cap never yields to agency discretion.
12. Exclusivity fails whether created by statute, charter consent veto, or prior-operation claim.
13. A regulated monopoly under a public grant is allowed, while a private combination restraining trade is banned.
14. Emergency declaration is presidential, yet takeover power is congressional and needs a delegating law.
15. Temporary takeover needs reasonable terms during the emergency, while permanent transfer needs just compensation always.
16. Telecommunications sits inside critical infrastructure by statute, with more vital services declarable by the President.
17. A foreign state-owned enterprise faces a capital ban, while sovereign wealth and pension funds share a thirty percent allowance.
18. The fifty percent reciprocity cap measures the foreign national owner, and similar-value rights in other sectors satisfy it.
